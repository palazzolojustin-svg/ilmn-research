## UK precision-medicine lead at Roche, formerly at Thermo Fisher (29 Jul 2026)

### Who the expert is and how to weigh them

The expert is the Precision Medicine and Genomics Lead at Roche Holding in the UK. Their job is to use genomics to match patients to drugs, oversee genomic testing, and make sure tests exist before new medicines arrive. They also advise NHS England (the UK's public health service) on which drugs and technologies are coming. After a 2004 PhD they worked at a forensics company that used next-generation sequencing (NGS, the modern high-speed method of reading DNA). They then spent about 15 years as an application specialist at what is now Thermo Fisher, an Illumina competitor, building cancer, non-cancer and even plant applications, and increasingly clinical solutions covering instruments, software and reagents (the chemical kits used in each run).

The interviewer was a buy-side investor who had trained as an internal-medicine doctor. This was a repeat conversation. It took place on 29 Jul 2026 and was published 31 Jul.

The expert's observations on the NHS are direct and current. Their comments on rivals' accuracy are hearsay ("we have heard"), and their views on future technology are speculation. They work for a competitor and could not name Roche's own sequencer. Their view is UK-centric, and the interviewer noted that the US market is "extremely fragmented" by comparison.

### Illumina's dominance is eroding, one segment at a time

The expert's core thesis is that Illumina "is definitely the gold standard" and still holds dominant share, "but it definitely is being eroded" [Expert: Roche precision medicine lead, Jul 2026]. Illumina began in research and moved into clinical work, which the expert sees as where most of its growth now lies. Its range spans high to low throughput (how much DNA a machine reads per run).

Each rival attacks a different slice of that range:

- **Ultima Genomics** goes after population-scale and high-throughput clinical work and is driving cost down. The expert says this will "really impact Illumina's growth in that space."
- **Element Biosciences** targets mid-throughput labs, competing on precision rather than volume.
- **MGI** sells into discrete parts of Illumina's business.
- **Oxford Nanopore and PacBio** (long-read sequencers, which read much longer continuous stretches of DNA) play in complex disease research, in epigenomics (chemical marks on DNA that change gene activity), and in point-of-care diagnostics. The expert calls point-of-care "quite a big area" in the UK, where there is a push to test at the bedside.

Because each rival "is bringing a unique feature," the expert thinks Illumina will "struggle to retain the dominance" it has had. They expect the trend to accelerate and to be visible "over the next five to 10 years."

### Bedside testing and reagent rental

Oxford Nanopore, which is UK-based, has taken its technology "out into the wild" for pathogen testing. During COVID, for example, it was used at the bedside to identify the strain and move non-COVID patients out of COVID wards. A patient with diffuse cancer could similarly get a fast answer rather than "bouncing around the system." The expert describes a "huge governmental shift" toward faster diagnostics and calls Nanopore devices "like matchbox... super tiny, super easy to use."

The interviewer relayed that Nanopore hands out benchtop sequencers and makes its money on consumables. The expert replied, "To be fair, that's what Illumina used to do." This is **reagent rental**: the instrument is cheap, but the customer commits to buying the vendor's reagents for two to three years. Many UK and global companies use it because it produces stable, repeat business.

### How NHS testing is decentralizing (direct experience)

The expert describes a recurring cycle. A test is centralized while expertise is scarce. It then spreads out to many labs as it commoditizes (becomes standard and widely available), "until the next test comes along." The table below shows where three test types sit in that cycle in NHS labs, per the expert.

| Test | Past | Now | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP, a broad panel reading many genes in a tumor) | Seen as needing central expertise | Seven labs offer it as NHS standard of care | Still fairly centralized, but much less than before |
| Liquid biopsy (cancer testing from a blood sample) | One lab for all NHS patients | Three labs | Expected to expand as it commoditizes |
| MRD (minimal residual disease, detecting traces of cancer left after treatment) | Not widely deployed | NHS choosing which lab(s) can run it | Expected to decentralize |

For CGP, labs initially used Illumina instruments and each designed its own tests to cover the genes in the NHS National Test Directory. Some labs partnered with Thermo Fisher and some with Illumina. Wales, for example, deployed Illumina instruments and reagents.

Liquid biopsy and MRD work differently, through **technology transfer**. Because the reagent design is complex, the NHS and other European groups partner with companies such as Foundation Medicine and Guardant. The company trains a local lab to run its test at the same standard as its own send-out service (where samples are shipped to the company's central lab).

- In Manchester, an NHS lab has an Illumina instrument "purely dedicated" to liquid biopsy using Foundation Medicine reagents. It is validated to match Foundation Medicine's Penzberg send-out lab. "Effectively, it's a Foundation Medicine lab."
- London has the same arrangement with Guardant.
- For MRD, Natera "was one that was being discussed quite closely." Guardant is another candidate because it already has a UK lab inside an NHS lab. A Natera site would run "as a true Natera lab" staffed by NHS employees.
- Such labs typically hold the partner's recommended instruments plus a second Illumina sequencer for CGP and other routine testing.

This matters for Illumina because technology-transfer contracts require the NHS lab to buy the same sequencer, library-preparation kits and ancillary machines the partner uses at headquarters. Natera uses Illumina today. "That's where potentially Illumina could have some erosion if these other companies, like Natera, start to validate their tests on other sequencing instruments." The NHS labs would then follow.

The expert sees "a big shift away across Europe from doing send-out services to bringing the technology in-house," which is driving "a huge uptick of technology transfers." They count seven NHS labs in England, potentially four in Scotland (not confirmed) and one in Wales, about 11 in total, all able to operate independently. If Illumina "can't be competitive in that space, they will lose out," again over five to 10 years.

### Are the big send-out labs leaving Illumina?

The expert says validation is under way, but today everything still runs on Illumina [Expert: Roche precision medicine lead, Jul 2026]. Foundation Medicine, Guardant and Natera are talking to Ultima and to makers of newer sequencers, including "the Roche sequencer," because these offer lower cost. If running on Ultima lowered Guardant's test cost, that would be "a huge competitive advantage." "There is validation ongoing with some of the other platforms. Whether they'll be successful or not is still up in the air."

Labs would not validate anything below Illumina's quality, and they also check that throughput fits, since a mismatch could raise their costs. Once a new platform is validated, the transferred labs would have to swap their Illumina instruments, a risk the expert says is specific to Illumina. Their suggested defense is for Illumina to partner with companies such as Natera so that its platforms stay inside their send-out services.

### Rival accuracy (hearsay)

All of the following was reported as "we have heard":

- MGI is "not quite as accurate as the Illumina system."
- Element gets very positive reports on data quality.
- Ultima has "super low" error rates, but its scale suits mainly a very high-volume operator such as Foundation Medicine.
- A sequencer transcribed as "Xcelris" or "XcelrisXcelus," which from context is Roche's sequencing-by-expansion machine, gets positive reports on accuracy.

### Long-read: real but niche

The NHS is validating Oxford Nanopore for specific uses such as MRD and epigenomics, amid "a lot of positive noise." It benefits partly from being locally developed. The expert sees less of PacBio, citing "question marks on the reliability of PacBio."

The interviewer offered that long-read is perhaps 10% of sequencing demand. The expert places long-read's strengths in four areas:

- pathogen detection;
- epigenomics and RNA modifications;
- large structural variants (big rearrangements of DNA);
- whole-genome problems that are hard for short reads.

The NHS has moved cancer work back onto short reads. Whole-genome sequencing now pays off mainly in rare disease, where Nanopore "would have a real position." Initial NHS validation is for whole genomes, with epigenomics as phase two. In cancer, some MRD-related uses are possible, but not comprehensive profiling.

On whether nanopore could replace sequencing by synthesis (SBS, Illumina's method of building a copy of DNA one base at a time), the expert explained the theoretical advantage. Nanopore pulls native DNA through a pore and reads changes in electrical current, so it avoids amplification and can see bases and epigenetic marks in one run. Still, "the long-read application is quite niche," and they "can't see everything moving to a Nanopore technology." Short reads keep a lasting place in profiling and biopsies.

### Genomic data, NHS policy and ethics

The interviewer noted that Tempus, Caris and others say the money is in data rather than testing. The expert said the NHS treats data as its "unique selling point" but does not sell it, under a mandate that the data belong to patients.

The NHS is building the **Unified Genomics Record (UGR)**. It could hold a full patient pathway: diagnosis, treatments, the genomic profile, therapy changes, outcomes and adverse events. The expert has seen such data collected through NHS Digital. Their proposal is to de-identify the data and sell it to pharma and developers. "That's definitely where the money is, by far and away," and the NHS "doesn't capitalize on" it, while other countries do better. Outcome data would also let the NHS pay more for drugs that work and less for those that underperform their trials.

The ethical worry is "big bad pharma" and insurers learning about patients' predispositions. The expert thinks guardrails could address this but calls public concern understandable.

Private holders of genomic data have their own gaps. Caris, Foundation Medicine and Guardant lack treatment and outcome data, which would make their data "even more valuable." Flatiron priced its data so high that few could buy it. Pooling rare-cancer data across countries (roughly 10 patients in a UK region, 10 in Europe, 5 to 10 in the US) would be "hugely valuable," and many industry consortia have approached the NHS.

The interviewer floated paying patients, for example $0.50 per data access. The expert called it "a great idea," suggested royalties could go to dependents, and said most cancer patients "want to give something back." The NHS would need an opt-in or opt-out scheme and a paid-access repository. The model is **Our Future Health**, a UK government-funded volunteer cohort that drug companies, including Roche, pay to access. It is not genomic, but it includes consent for genomics.

### What Illumina should do, and market growth (opinion)

The expert's advice depended on location:

1. **A point-of-care product.** It would sell in the UK because "people know the name, people trust the name."
2. **Expansion beyond DNA.** This means transcriptomics (reading RNA), proteomics (measuring proteins), and spatial transcriptomics (mapping gene activity within tissue), which Illumina is reportedly exploring. Customers buy the instrument that an application is validated on.
3. **Data, software and AI.** Sequencing "is becoming commoditized." The question now is "We can develop all this cool data. What the heck do we do with it?" In NHS trusts "the biggest challenge now is the data."

The interviewer relayed an ex-Illumina claim that nobody uses Illumina's bioinformatics software. The expert agreed in part: much analysis software is free and open source, and labs build their own pipelines. The real bottleneck is interpretation and staff: "It's quite easy to run the machines... but actually validating and interpreting what comes out... that's where there's a bit of a bottleneck." Multi-omics (combining several kinds of molecular data) has "a huge gap" here.

On single-cell and multi-omics, where 10x Genomics is "king of the hill" and is validating on other sequencers, the expert said Illumina is "late to the game, definitely." Being second can help if Illumina learns from others and goes a step further. But "if they just come out with a me too, I do think that's where they'll struggle." Illumina must innovate as it did with early NGS, and may need to hire innovative people.

On market growth, the expert's views were these:

- Clinical sequencing is "where all the growth is."
- Liquid biopsy still has "a huge number of unmet need."
- CGP is "quite saturated," with only small year-on-year gains outside new markets. Its liquid-biopsy portion is the faster-growing part.
- MRD is a definite growth area.
- Multi-omics should grow "quite rapidly" within five years, but after liquid biopsy and MRD.

Roche's sequencing-by-expansion machine "will change the landscape if it does deliver what it's supposed to deliver." The expert said "I genuinely do not know" whether a four-hour whole genome is achievable. A fast nanopore-style benchtop could enable bedside genomics with remote analysis hubs, which would be "a definite shift."

### Side discussion: UK drug pricing

This part does not concern Illumina directly. NICE (the UK drug cost-effectiveness body) frustrates some trial consultants when strong drugs with no alternatives are blocked. The expert cited publicity through cyclist Sir Chris Hoy over a prostate cancer drug that was not approved.

The US "most favored nation" pricing policy has, over the past six months, led some companies to delay launches in the UK and Europe. The interviewer said it "backfired," and the expert agreed: "It's backfiring on the U.S., but it's backfiring on the other countries as well." The US remains "the biggest growth market." UK law bans drug advertising to the public, and the expert wants a global settlement so that "everybody needs to pay appropriately for innovation."

## Senior scientist at Abbott, a long-time Illumina customer (29 Jul 2026)

### Who the expert is

The expert is a Senior Scientist at Abbott Laboratories, formerly at the startup Zafrens. They report to the Head of R&D, two levels below the C-suite, and work across drug discovery, sequencing and in vitro diagnostics (tests run on samples outside the body). They hold a PhD in chemistry and biophysics. AlphaSense lists about 13 years in industry and 23 years as an Illumina customer, mainly on the NextSeq (mid-throughput) and the MiSeq (small benchtop). The interviewer was a buy-side investor.

The expert has direct experience evaluating instruments from a startup's point of view. They openly said they have not used the NovaSeq X, are not expert on large core labs, MRD, immunohistochemistry, spatial or reimbursement, and had not seen the latest AXELIOS data. Many answers were labeled "hunch" or "guess."

### Roche AXELIOS: an exciting middle ground

The interviewer said Roche launched AXELIOS about a month earlier, using SBX (sequencing by expansion). The expert called it "a very exciting technology" that opens a space for **mid-length reads**: longer than Illumina's, shorter than Nanopore's. Such reads could help link SNPs (single-letter DNA differences) over longer distances and give higher-confidence calls. The expert has followed it for roughly 1.5 to 2 years. Library prep (preparing DNA for the machine) "doesn't seem too hard."

To win volume from Illumina, Roche must prove three things:

- **Data quality.** Quality scores seem "pretty high," "maybe not quite as good as Illumina currently," though longer reads may offset this.
- **Cost.** Both running cost and device cost matter, because a sequencer is among the largest capital expenditures (capex, spending on long-lived equipment) a lab makes. Leasing will probably be offered.
- **Workflow integration.** The expert credits Illumina's software and cloud tools with making adoption easy.

Mid-length reads might justify switching without the sacrifice in data quantity and quality that very long reads impose.

### Competitors ranked

The interviewer said Element claims a **$100 genome** (the cost to sequence one whole human genome) later in 2026. Labs have historically used focused panels (sequencing only selected genes) for cancer and rare-disease screening, so a $100 genome "might open up new avenues of really preventive medicine." Reimbursement effects are unclear to the expert.

The expert's ranking of threats to Illumina, all labeled as guesses:

- **Element** has the best chance: "Element has a good opportunity to really capture a little more share."
- **Ultima** is promising and a "potentially significant" high-end competitor, but it is younger, smaller and focused on very large scale, so it "might take a longer time." Some labs hesitate over its size.
- **PacBio and Oxford Nanopore** are "still up in the air" and niche, so their shares "may not change a lot."
- **Thermo Fisher** (Ion Torrent and Genexus) is "still a force," at "at least 10%-15%" of the sequencing market, an unsourced estimate.
- **Complete Genomics** has "a very limited presence in the U.S."

On whether large genome centers split volume across vendors, the expert's hunch was that the biggest centers will explore new platforms. Smaller centers resist retraining and new data formats. Startups are flexible and may switch when a lease ends without penalty.

### Long reads

Long reads trade quality for length. For Nanopore, "cost per read is quite a bit higher" and quality "goes down a little bit," so long-read is "usually somewhat more niche." The expert would not predict when it might take real share.

On PacBio selling its short-read assets (which they believe came from Omniome) to Illumina, the expert said "maybe it's a good strategy." Long reads may be better for rare disease, where several SNPs act together across long distances, but clinical adoption would need more regulatory approval, which "might just take a long time."

### What it takes to switch away from Illumina

Speaking from startup experience, the expert listed five considerations:

1. **Technical basics.** Read length, quality scores, and paired reads (reading each fragment from both ends, such as the common 2x300 format).
2. **Capital cost.** This is the biggest friction, and lease-to-own options matter.
3. **Bench space** for the instrument.
4. **Data compatibility.** Output should come as FASTQ files (the standard sequencing-data format), which is now standard across main vendors.
5. **Revalidation.** After reading white papers and talking to users, "it could take a month or two just to really do side-by-side comparison studies." "It's not an instantaneous decision... You shouldn't take it lightly."

### NovaSeq X, NextSeq and iSeq

The NovaSeq X's share "seems like... has grown quite a bit" since launch, which the expert vaguely places four or five years ago. Labs scaling up from several NextSeqs or MiSeqs will likely move to it, because "the cost per reads are lower than any of the other platforms." Budget is "a perfectly valid concern," and the decision depends on years of expected use, demand growth, and capital or lease options.

Cannibalization of NextSeq (Illumina's own products taking each other's sales) is "possible," because pooling libraries onto one large machine saves time. The iSeq, the smallest instrument, replaced the MiniSeq and is a good screening tool. Some of its role may now go to the new MiSeq. The expert called the iSeq "its own little niche that might be hard to grow from."

### Clinical demand, MRD, spatial and new uses

Growth is in oncology and prenatal screening. The expert called prenatal "a constant one for Illumina" rather than a fast-growing one. Their unsourced guess at the clinical mix is prenatal and neonatal at 10% to 20% and oncology at "50%-60% at least, maybe higher." Rare disease has a push but is "not a very large growing market." Pharmacogenomics panels (genes affecting drug response, such as cytochrome P450 variants) are small. Immunohistochemistry (IHC, a stain-based tissue test) won't go away, but sequencing will keep gaining as an extra tool.

The interviewer said Illumina had just launched a whole-genome (WGS) MRD research kit and that Natera's Signatera, a targeted-panel test, dominates MRD with a new NCCN guideline endorsement. The expert thinks WGS "makes sense," since fixed panels such as Illumina's TruSight can miss mutations, including ones outside the exome (the protein-coding part of the genome). They do not know what it would take to beat Signatera, beyond evidence that panels miss things.

On spatial products (Illumina StrataMap vs 10x Atera), spatial mapping has value in cancer, but reads are "more expensive" and the field "a little more exploratory."

The one new growth area the expert named is **neoantigen vaccines**. A tumor is sequenced, tumor-specific markers are encoded "like tiles on a mosaic" into an mRNA, and the immune system learns to attack them. Early trials suggest the approach works.

## Academic genomics core director, South Carolina (30 Jul 2026)

### Who the expert is

The expert is an associate professor who directs a university genomics core (a shared lab that runs sequencing for other researchers) in South Carolina and controls its purchasing. This was an AI-run channel check (a structured interview to gauge demand), published 3 Aug 2026.

The core serves several groups:

- the University of South Carolina;
- Clemson, Coastal Carolina, MUSC and small colleges;
- out-of-state users;
- Prisma Health;
- biotechs in South Carolina and Georgia.

It owns an Illumina NextSeq 500 and MiSeq, an Oxford Nanopore PromethION and MinION, library-prep robots and a single-cell pipeline. It has won NIH funding for an Element AVITI24 and is buying it. High-volume Illumina work is outsourced to NovaSeq X providers, MedGenome and "Somagen" (as transcribed). Its work, ranked, is RNA (transcriptome), then low-coverage whole genomes, then epigenomics (methylation, CUT&RUN and ATAC-seq), plus microbiome.

### Volume is up strongly, but Illumina's in-house share will shrink

Federal funding dominates demand amid "great uncertainty and turmoil." In the short run, uncertainty can raise volume: principal investigators (PIs, the lab heads who hold grants) rush to spend before funds lapse. Other projects stall when renewals fail. Clinical research sequencing is emerging but "not a main... contributing factor yet." Demand for epigenomics, single-cell and spatial work is rising, and a new generation of PIs proposes multi-omic projects.

Output on Illumina instruments, measured in gigabases (billions of DNA letters read), is up about **40%** in Q2 2026 versus Q2 2025, mostly through outsourced NovaSeq X runs on the new **25B flow cell** (a higher-capacity consumable). This beats expectations. The expert gave three reasons for the single-cell boom:

1. Single-cell work is now key to winning grants and publishing in top journals.
2. The core invested in cell-preparation services.
3. Competitors to 10x Genomics have cut library-prep costs, which encourages deeper sequencing.

For the rest of 2026 the expert expects to move volume onto the AVITI24. Providers run fixed outputs that many projects don't need. In-house, the core can use less output per sample and fill flow cells more fully, lowering cost per sample. "We will use less Illumina and more AVITI24." The expert hopes "at least 80%" of in-house sequencing will run on the AVITI24, and stressed this is a hope.

The outlook is better than 90 days earlier: "it's going to be a better year than we thought early in the spring," with NIH and NSF functioning through the budget year. The main risk is 2027. A "huge reduction of NSF and NIH" would mean "significantly less demand."

### Reagents and pricing

Direct Illumina kit use is small: Nextera kits for small Arabidopsis plant genomes, and Illumina methylation kits. The workhorse kits come from NEB and Zymo. Spending rose about 40% "last year" and "at least 40%" this year, against about 15% growth from 2024 to 2025. The wording is ambiguous about which year the 40% refers to.

Provider prices are "very competitive" and flat to lower:

### Competitive landscape

The Element system cost less than the core's NIH grant had budgeted a year ago. Illumina reps offered discounts on the NextSeq 2000.

The expert reported several Oxford Nanopore changes: it is discontinuing the PromethION 2, raising PromethION Solo prices, and has dropped Flongle (a small low-cost flow cell), which "significantly affected logistics." On competitors' products:

- Ultima offers volume and low cost, "although the read quality is still questionable."

- Roche AXELIOS will "just simply die out." Its main edge is reads of about 1,000 bases, but its cost and real output are unknown, Illumina and Element now offer 600-base reads, TruPath covers synthetic long reads, and Nanopore is improving. "I don't see a space for the technology."

Illumina support is "okay," and Element's is better. The expert sees in-cell sequencing and more robust nanopore technology as the future, with clinical applications having the biggest impact.

## Former Illumina staff scientist, now at CRISPR QC (30 Jul 2026)

### Who the expert is

The expert is a former Illumina staff scientist who helped start two unnamed platforms there. They are now Senior Scientific Advisor at CRISPR QC, a startup building quality-control tests for gene editing. They hold a Spanish PhD in RNA regulation and spent 14 years at UCSD collaborating with the Salk Institute (including Joe Ecker's lab). They also helped build Cardea Bio, a CRISPR-diagnostics startup.

They still use Illumina for RNA-seq, single-cell and spatial work. Their Illumina tenure is undated, and they were present during a layoff in which their 12-person team was cut to three. They describe themselves as fond of Illumina, "like talking about relatives," and say many views are "personal opinion."

### History: the car and the gas

Early on "there was only one player, and that player was called Illumina." After acquiring Solexa's surface-amplification chemistry it had "the monopoly." Control rested on in-house consumables and library prep, plus **DRAGEN**, its fast data-analysis system, which "put Illumina at the forefront." The model was to launch an instrument and sell the consumables: "I'm selling you a car, but I'm the only one that can provide you with the gas." Combining research and product development under one roof was smart, because research protocols must be rebuilt into products for thousands of customers.

The expert repeated a rumor ("more than rumors") that Roche tried to buy Illumina 8 to 10 years ago but could not afford it.

### Slow and erratic against faster rivals

"Illumina started walking while the other players started running." Many rival leaders are ex-Illumina, and Illumina's decisions are "very slow and erratic."

In spatial, Illumina "didn't know what to do." Spatial is "a completely different beast" from single-cell because it involves tissue biology rather than purified DNA chemistry. Asked which spatial platform to pick: "Not the Illumina one. I would go for 10x Genomics or BGI." Yet the expert also called Illumina "still the pioneers" in single-cell.

In long reads, Element and PacBio are "good enough" to be close. PacBio will lead long reads, and 10x will lead spatial. Roche is "the one that I will be seriously concerned about." Its sequencing quality is approaching the NovaSeq X, which the expert calls "fantastic." Illumina's former VP of consumables is now a Roche VP. Roche has deep pockets and is visibly "getting ready," though "maybe I'm completely wrong."

The expert recalls Illumina launching its long-read offering around the end of 2024 at the J.P. Morgan healthcare conference. It still lags PacBio, whose instruments are cheaper than the NovaSeq. Specialists "have better knowledge" than a company with decades of short-read history.

### GRAIL and the clinical misstep

The expert's personal view is that "the creation of Grail" was a misstep. Illumina knew research-use-only (RUO) markets but not upstream clinical sample handling. When it moved into in vitro diagnostics (IVD, regulated clinical tests), "the problems started." GRAIL, staffed by ex-Illumina people, shared the same weakness. Illumina hired that expertise "already too late" and "lost focus" while rivals "were cooking things behind scenes."

### Proteomics

Clinical diagnosis increasingly needs multi-omics. Illumina's offering is early and "not strong enough," weak on depth ("you start scratching on the surface") and reproducibility. SomaLogic and Illumina must merge their science, not only their companies. Joining nucleic-acid chemistry with protein biochemistry is like "mixing water and oil." Colleagues with good budgets got only "a partial answer," which is fine for research but not for diagnostics, where you must be "98.5% at least sure."

### Long reads in the clinic (the GeneDx question)

Short reads need "100X or 60X minimum" coverage (how many times each DNA position is read) and lots of analysis. Long reads run up to about 2,000 bases, versus 400 to 500 for Illumina "if you are lucky." For SNPs, short reads are fine. For isoforms (alternative versions of a gene's RNA) in rare disease, the expert would "definitely" use long reads. The two are "apples and oranges." Ideally long reads would recheck short-read results, but clinical cost limits this. Long DNA molecules also break under pipetting, which complicates automation.

For a NICU or pediatric case needing results in two to three days, the expert would "go for Illumina." But a clinical genomics business should evaluate alternatives "now": "The future is here," meaning around January 2027 and the next J.P. Morgan meeting. Their confidence that a large lab like GeneDx (the interviewer's example) adopts an alternative platform is "98.5%," a judgment rather than data.

They gave two anecdotes:

- A microbiology professor is moving microbiome work toward long reads. Illumina gives a good picture "but you still see some pixels," others give "HD," and combining them is "golden."
- Joe Ecker, with a large brain-mapping grant, recently said he wants long reads for epigenome work.

PacBio "can get extremely close or surpass" Illumina depending on the question.

### Automation case study (direct experience, PacBio, about three years ago)

Above roughly 100 samples a day, labs should automate. Several hundred samples done manually "is going to burn through your budget." Around 40 a day, a cheap system such as Opentrons may suffice. Other vendors include PerkinElmer, Tecan, QIAGEN and Hamilton.

In the expert's proof of concept, three operators prepared 30 known samples. Manual deviation (false positives and negatives) was 5% to 15%, but the robot's was close to 40%, which the interviewer called "very high." The cause was miscalibration: the Z-axis was off and deck corners were misaligned. A second platform had to be bought, and the work took about nine months. A typical project takes about a year and two systems. After the fix, deviation fell below 10%, and protocol work cut it to 1.77%, "enough to... start moving into CLIA" (US clinical-lab certification).

### Outlook

### Pricing and barriers

Since AGBT in January 2025, Roche has promised only "competitive" pricing. The two boxes together cost less than a NovaSeq X, and reagents are "already at a competitive footprint." "Nobody's trying to chase Ultima to the bottom," and "Ultima's doomed in the long run anyway."

The expert estimates, from public information, that NextSeq-class instruments are about $500 million to $1 billion of revenue, an eighth to a quarter of Illumina's total. The NovaSeq X is the driver at "$1 million or more per instrument at pull-through" (the measure is unclear). Roche needs "quite aggressive" all-in pricing over 10 years. It has done "extraordinarily well" at $1 to $10 diagnostics, but only "did okay" with Foundation Medicine. Single-digit sequencing prices are not expected "in the next 10 years."

New labs take six to 10 years to reach CLIA/CAP accreditation for an LDT. Switching an existing lab is "a four- or five-year project." Adaptive's move from NovaSeq 6000 to X took years and, per its calls, turned clonoSEQ profitable 1.5 to 2 years after the first shipments. If Adaptive sheds its other businesses, "they're printing cash." Reagent rental would burden Roche with upfront capital recouped over three to five years, and fixed reimbursement stops labs passing on costs.

Oxford Nanopore grew "10% or 12%" in its half-year results. China is lost to Western vendors, and Europe is "less enthusiastic" about capex and more public-institutional, so Roche faces "a 10-year program." "None of these systems move quickly."

### Sizing the threat

Roche "could probably pick away 10%-20% relatively easily" over about three years, depending on Illumina's customer concentration, and is "quite threatening" beyond 10 years. Its openings are tech-development labs, cores at renewal, new entities and CROs (contract research organizations). Plasmidsaurus, a fast-scaling Nanopore-based service firm that added RNA-seq last fall on a recently bought Illumina system, is likely evaluating Roche (unconfirmed).

The table below lists the expert's rough industry figures, several of which are guesses.

| Item | Expert's figure |
|---|---|
| Illumina revenue | "$4+ billion" |
| Oxford Nanopore | Under $500 million a year |
| PacBio | About $150 million ("I don't think they're long for this world") |
| Industry in 10 years | About $5 billion |
| Roche sequencing in 10 years | $1–2 billion ("a complete dart at the wall") |

Fixed reimbursement caps growth. The big upside would be population-scale epigenetics worth "several billions of dollars," which would need a 2008-style funding "renaissance" that the expert does not foresee in the US soon.

## Single-cell specialist at a Brigham and Women's Hospital core (call estimated ~10 Oct 2026)

### Who the expert is

The expert has worked for about five years in a Harvard-affiliated hospital profiling core, sequencing single-cell and spatial libraries on a NovaSeq X Plus. They have hands-on MinION and PacBio experience and **no hands-on AXELIOS experience**, but have analyzed AXELIOS single-cell data. The Tegus transcript is undated.

### AXELIOS: as advertised, plus the expert's data check

The expert is "optimistic" because a major new player should cut prices. According to Roche's marketing and the expert's assumptions, AXELIOS offers:

- runs of a few hours, with data "the same day or next day";
- possibly 25 billion+ reads per run;
- four parallel experiments, which Illumina cannot do;
- multiple flow-cell sizes, from one to 300 libraries;
- lower cost;
- color-coded reagents and laptop FASTQ generation, needing one or two people.

The hurdles are newness ("people need to find confidence"), no public same-sample comparisons (the expert especially wants to check Roche's low-noise claim), and the Illumina ecosystem.

The expert's hands-on comparison of AXELIOS and Illumina single-cell data is summarized below. They found no substantial difference.

| Metric | Result |
|---|---|
| Reads per cell (e.g. 30,000 requested, 20,000–25,000 expected back) | Very similar |
| Saturation (how fully the sample's diversity is captured) | Very similar for the same tissue |
| Genes detected | Very similar, "not losing anything" |
| Error rate | About 0.1% at Q30 (a standard accuracy score) for both |

This rests on a few datasets, not a formal study.

### Reproducibility and artifacts

Studies last four to five years in academia and five to 10 in pharma, so data must stay comparable. The expert gave examples of platform artifacts:

- Smart-seq cells forming their own cluster, which authors misread as biology;
- spurious gene overexpression after re-sequencing on Roche;
- a 1,000-sample exome study that switches platforms mid-way and ends up clustering patients by machine rather than biology.

The cause is chemistry. Roche's SBX is "kind of long polymeric," Illumina's SBS involves amplification, and Thermo uses hydrogen-ion detection. "It is not critical," old samples need no re-sequencing, and switching is "kind of switching from Windows to Mac." Ion Torrent is the closest comparator to Roche. Mixing long-read with Roche brings "too many artifacts."

### Ecosystem, switching and adoption

The ecosystem is "the biggest competitive advantage of Illumina." It comprises bundled systems, support, mature library protocols, bioinformatics, compatibility with 10x and Takara Trekker (a spatial method), and a large community. "It is hard to break that ecosystem." "We sequenced it on Illumina, meaning I can trust this data." Roche needs "five years. It could take 10 years."

Adoption would speed up with error-minimizing workflows, publications, promotions (such as 50% off reagents) and entry via hospital pathology labs. It would slow if Roche targeted small labs.

Researchers say: "I will pay more, but I will not switch." Likelihood of using Roche is 4–5/5 for large biotech and pharma and 2–3/5 for small academic labs. The interviewer summarized this as roughly 50/50 for new projects and about 1/5 for existing ones, and the expert agreed. One large company spent about **$2 million** just on comparative quality checks. Big players expect Illumina "will not probably be the major player anymore in the next five, 10 years." Cores can offer Roche as a second platform.

Roche's wins will come in large-scale clinical work, such as sequencing every patient (the expert says this is the UK model and an emerging US trend), and in biopharma. Single-cell demand is "being replaced by spatial transcriptomics" that uses fluorescent probes "and do[es] not use sequencing."

### Turnaround and price

At the core, batching to fill cheaper large flow cells (10 billion reads versus 500 million) takes a couple of weeks, sequencing about two more, and download plus analysis more again. That adds up to **six to eight weeks** from library to data. Roche saves roughly a week per run through faster runs, parallel runs and less batching (5–10 samples rather than 20–30 suffice, per the interviewer, which the expert confirmed). Illumina has small flow cells, but at much higher per-genome cost.

The expert scored how much turnaround matters to each customer type:

| Customer | Turnaround importance | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Clinical/pathology | 5/5 | Treatment decisions, such as a child with cancer |
| Biotech/pharma | 4/5 | Trial and financial decisions |
| Academic | 2–3/5 | "More chill," accepted "because everybody is following Illumina" |

Speed is Roche's top marketing point and cost the second. Publications are key: "Can I trust the data if it's not in a... paper? Probably not." 10x built its lead by funding comparison studies and conference talks. Two to three years could yield critical-mass publications.

If Illumina cut to the interviewer's $100–$200 genome, "it should be enough... They don't have to really go down too low." Roche's timeline would then stretch to **five to seven years**. The expert endorsed the interviewer's summary: prices fall, Illumina is "not out of moves," and Roche can become a real contender over about five years, mainly with large organizations and new projects.

## Cross-call themes

### Illumina's entrenchment is the dominant fact, but every rival chips a different piece

Every expert called Illumina the incumbent: the "gold standard" [Roche UK lead, Jul 2026], "workhorse" [Nanopore director, ~Oct 2026], "IBM" with 80%+ of installations [ex-Illumina associate director, Aug 2026] and "tried and true" [ex-Illumina scientist now at Roche, Aug 2026]. Its stickiness comes from the end-to-end ecosystem, trained staff, validated clinical assays and trust. Even those predicting share loss expect it to be modest near term. The academic cores and the genome-center VP foresee little or no loss over 12 months.

The bearish versions differ in mechanism:

- segment-by-segment attack, with Ultima high, Element mid and Roche clinical [Roche UK lead; ex-Illumina associate director];
- slow, erratic strategy [ex-Illumina staff scientist];
- innovation fatigue [PacBio lead];
- send-out labs revalidating on cheaper platforms [Roche UK lead].

### Clinical has replaced research as the growth engine, and research funding is the main drag

Experts agree academic demand is weakened by NIH cuts. They cite the 2025 indirect-cost cap [PacBio lead], RUO "off a cliff" [Nanopore director], a VP expecting 2026 volume down about 5% [genome center VP] and 2027 funding fears [South Carolina core]. Clinical work is steadier and reimbursement-backed. It was 52% of Illumina revenue by Sep 2024 [ex-Illumina associate director], with oncology at roughly 50%–60% of clinical [Abbott scientist; ex-Illumina scientist now at Roche].

Liquid biopsy and MRD are the consensus growth areas. One exception to the gloom is the South Carolina core, whose output rose about 40% on single-cell demand.

The flip side is concentration. Illumina depends on roughly 10 to 20 large labs such as Natera, Guardant, Tempus, Caris, BillionToOne and Exact [Nanopore director; ex-Illumina associate director], which hold bargaining power and could defect.

### Roche AXELIOS: a contested newcomer

Views on AXELIOS span the full range:

- "will just simply die out" [South Carolina core];
- not significant within a year [Georgia core];
- minimal near term, more threatening than smaller rivals later [genome center VP];
- "hype without a lot of data" [PacBio lead];
- 10%–20% share over three years, with $1–2 billion in revenue in 10 years [Nanopore director];
- five-plus years to a real ecosystem, or five to seven if Illumina cuts price [Brigham specialist].

There is broad agreement on its strengths: reads of about 1,000 bases, speed (a four-hour genome versus Illumina's 24 hours), roughly Illumina-level or better accuracy, and a $150 duplex genome. Its weaknesses are cumbersome chemistry, two boxes and no epigenetics.

The facts differ on details. Capital cost was put at $750,000 [PacBio lead] or "less than a NovaSeq X" [Nanopore director]. One expert attributed AXELIOS to Element, which appears to be an error. Accuracy was described as "maybe not quite as good as Illumina" [Abbott, Jul] versus "higher than Illumina's" [PacBio, Aug].

### Switching is slow, costly and mostly happens with new projects

Switching estimates rise with the stakes:

- a month or two of side-by-side testing for a startup [Abbott];
- about a year for automation [ex-Illumina staff scientist];
- four to five years to convert a clinical lab, and six to 10 for new LDT labs [Nanopore director];
- $2 million just for quality checks at one large company [Brigham specialist].

The consistent pattern is that rivals are added alongside Illumina rather than replacing it. Wins come at lease or service renewal, in new labs and in new projects.

### Long-read stays a niche complement

Nearly every expert sees long reads as valuable in rare disease, structural variants, epigenetics and pathogens, but limited by cost, throughput, missing population databases and clinical software. The client estimate was about 10% of demand [Roche UK lead, Jul].

The outliers are the ex-Illumina staff scientist (98.5% confident large clinical labs adopt long reads) and the South Carolina core (expects Nanopore to gain). Illumina's synthetic long reads, called TruPath or "Constellation" by different experts, split opinion. Adoption estimates were 5%–6% [South Carolina] and 1%–2% [genome center VP], against "not at all likely" [Georgia]. Experts also give different launch timings: end-2024 [staff scientist] versus early 2026 [Nanopore director].

### Pricing is converging, quotes are opaque, and ancillary costs rise

Short-read pricing is "a race to the bottom" [PacBio lead], with "everyone at around the same price point" [ex-Illumina scientist now at Roche], though nobody chases Ultima downward [Nanopore director]. Genome-price claims cluster at $100–$200: $100 (Element), $150 (Illumina, Roche), and Nanopore at least double.

Illumina's list price was unchanged over 90 days at both academic cores and the genome center, but deals are individualized and promised discounts were sometimes broken [Georgia]. Informatics and storage costs are flat or rising, and capex of roughly $1–1.2 million for a NovaSeq X is the biggest barrier to switching.

### Data and interpretation, not sequencing, is the new bottleneck

Experts from the NHS, PacBio, Roche and the ex-Illumina ranks all say machines are easy and interpretation is hard. Bioinformatics openness, integrated workflows and AI-ready data are seen as the next battleground.

### Multi-omics: right direction, unproven execution

Experts largely call SomaLogic clever or sensible, but it has had little effect on customers so far: the genome center uses Olink, small cores find it too costly, and the Georgia director was unaware of it. They describe it as early, shallow and possibly vulnerable to native protein sequencing. Illumina is seen as late in single-cell and spatial against 10x. PIPseq is the one product drawing positive evaluation.
