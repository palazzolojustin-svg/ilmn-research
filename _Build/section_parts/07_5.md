This final part covers 28 expert calls held between September 8 and October 28, 2026. Most are customer "channel checks", which are scripted surveys of a lab about its volumes, vendors, prices and plans. Many were run by an AI interviewer. In those calls, product facts about Illumina's roadmap came from the interviewer, not the expert. These facts were that NovaSeq X flow cell output would rise by up to 40% and whole-genome runs would be about 30% faster, that TruPath Genome launched in February 2026 at about $395 per sample list, and that the SomaLogic acquisition closed in January 2026. They are not repeated below.

The rest of the set is four moderated interviews (Guidepoint), two Tegus interviews, two competitor-employee calls from Oxford Nanopore, and one former Illumina senior director.

Two pairs of calls appear to be the same person interviewed twice, judging by identical details. The first pair is the Sanofi head of genomics (Oct 5) and the "large pharma head of genomics" (Oct 18). ## Louisville sequencing-center leader and long-read startup CEO (Guidepoint), 2026-09-08

**Who.** Dr. Melissa Laird Smith is Associate Professor at the University of Louisville School of Medicine. They stepped down as director of its Sequencing Technology Center (a shared "core" facility) in early 2025 to run the new Kentucky Center of Precision Medicine. They still chair the core's advisory board, and their team generates about 77% of the core's occupancy. They are also CEO of **Clareo Biosciences**, which uses long reads to profile immune repertoires. Immune repertoire profiling means reading the huge variety of B-cell and T-cell receptors. They have used PacBio since 2012 and openly say they are biased toward long reads. They have not yet run Illumina's new single-cell/spatial workflow or 10x's Atera.

**Illumina demand has been rerouted, not lost.** Illumina volume at small and mid-sized academic cores "has dramatically decreased." The cause is low-priced contract research organizations (CROs, outside firms selling sequencing as a service). Examples are Plasmidsaurus, at about $50 per RNA-seq sample (its large Illumina plant is in Louisville), and Zymo, which just launched RNA sequencing. Cores must recover salaries, so there is "absolutely no way that we can compete on price."

The core still writes letters of support for grants, but then "we don't see the sequencing." The core has only a NextSeq 2000, so users prepare 10x single-cell libraries there and take them elsewhere to sequence on NovaSeqs. Subsidized spatial CRO services such as Michigan's are also drawing work. Their verdict: "I don't think that the demand for Illumina has gone down. I just think that it's been rerouted" [Expert: Louisville center director, Sep 2026].

**Leaning back into Illumina.** Illumina is moving single-cell, spatial and proteomics (protein measurement) onto the NovaSeq X Plus. Their precision-medicine center is negotiating to buy one, expected in use by end-2026. They value Illumina's one-platform, multi-omics integration, meaning DNA, RNA and protein measured from the same cells, with Illumina's DRAGEN analysis software on top and Illumina Protein Prep (the former SomaLogic assay). That integration would be the "tipping point" to leave 10x: "an incredible competitor to 10x."

They gave a price comparison, all-in. Atera costs about $650,000 and is a single-purpose 10x box the size of a refrigerator. The NovaSeq X Plus costs about $1.2 million, roughly twice the price, with "about three times the application capability." Illumina's larger spatial slide and its single-cell input range of 1,000 to one million cells are "a theoretical win" for now. By contrast, Illumina's PIPseq single-cell kit, marketed for over a year, "doesn't seem like it's making much of a dent."

**10x Genomics.** 10x remains "a workhorse" with sticky workflows, but usage is rising only "very slowly" because assays are costly, and its future is uncertain. The Xenium their center had budgeted for is "now obsolete" as 10x moves to Atera. Most users still run the original Chromium controller, and whether they follow 10x to Atera is "the big test… I'm not convinced they will." Current 10x workflows also cannot handle very large cells such as cardiomyocytes.

They are watching alternatives:

- **ArgenTag:** a controller-free chip giving full-length transcripts on PacBio or Oxford Nanopore. It costs about $2,500–3,000 per sample and "worked perfectly out of the gate."
- **Parse:** heavy on pipetting.
- **Cellanome:** places no limits on cell size.

**PacBio.** Their PacBio use is steady, on two Revios plus one Vega (a lower-cost benchtop). PacBio released its SPRQ-Nx chemistry for Vega on August 18, 2026. SPRQ-Nx lets a roughly $1,000 SMRT Cell be rerun four times with pooled libraries.

They see the cost of a genome having fallen from about $200,000 in 2012 to as low as $500. Kinnex concatenation strings about 12 B-cell receptor molecules of 1.5 kb into one roughly 20 kb read, giving 8–12 times more depth. That makes long reads "cost benefit" versus short reads, at Q40 accuracy versus Q30, meaning 99.99% versus 99.9%.

Vega's affordability has cost the core some customers, who now sequence in-house. Their criticisms are that Iso-Seq and Kinnex "[haven't] really taken off," and that PacBio takes analysis "80% there and not all the way."

**Other points.**

- Adaptive Biotechnologies exited research profiling to focus on MRD, leaving "a massive gap."
- Their lab has screened over 3,000 people: no two non-twins share the same immune genes, and some healthy people lack about 25% of them.
- They hold a new NSF grant to sequence 60 vertebrates and an $11 million metabolic COBRE grant. No large genomics grants at Louisville were cancelled.
- A computing system bought for $3 million two years ago "now cost[s] $7 million" because of AI demand.

## Oxford Nanopore senior director of strategic product management, ex-Illumina 2008–2014 (Tegus, est. ~2026-10-01)

**Who.** This expert worked at Illumina from 2008 to 2014, then about three years at Adaptive Biotechnologies, about four at 10x Genomics, about one in spatial genomics, and about 2.5 at Oxford Nanopore (ONT). They believe Illumina's growth is driven by about 10–20 large clinical labs, including Natera, Guardant, Tempus and Caris. "Illumina had a great quarter." 10x was the glaring exception, with flat revenue. ONT's half-year growth was about 10–12%.

**Illumina.** "Illumina still is the workhorse of the industry, remains that way probably into the foreseeable future." Ultima and Element have not succeeded at scale.

Illumina's long-read product, which they think is "still called Constellation," is a "trick" that could "threaten Oxford's long-read clinical position a bit" in rare disease. They put Illumina at about $150 per genome and Nanopore at "at least double."

They also estimated Illumina's mix. Mid-throughput NextSeq-class instruments are worth about $500 million to $1 billion, or an eighth to a quarter of revenue. The NovaSeq X is the driver, at "$1 million or more per instrument at pull-through." In their view Illumina is "$4+ billion" in revenue.

**Roche AXELIOS.** AXELIOS uses SBX (sequencing by expansion). It copies DNA into an "Xpandomer," which the expert called "a Frankenstein monster of an oligonucleotide copy," read through a nanopore. Reads are up to about 1,000 bases. The chemistry is the "weakest link… definitely more expensive than you would want." The two-box workflow (prep plus sequencer) raises capital cost, though both boxes together cost less than one NovaSeq X. AXELIOS cannot do long reads or native epigenetics.

The threat is in read-counting applications, such as RNA-seq and liquid biopsy (blood tests for tumor DNA), at Roche's stated $150 per genome. MRD (minimal residual disease, detecting leftover cancer) is "a slam dunk." They pitched exactly this to Roche at the AMP meeting in November 2025, and "Nobody ever called me back."

Several factors slow switching:

- Clinical labs run "a factory floor full of Illumina systems."
- Labor costs may offset Roche's price advantage.
- Reimbursement is fixed.
- A new lab needs 6–10 years to reach CLIA/CAP accreditation (US clinical lab certifications) with LDTs (lab-developed tests).
- Moving an existing lab takes 4–5 years. Adaptive took years just to go from NovaSeq 6000 to NovaSeq X, and that move turned clonoSEQ profitable.

**Forecast.** Roche "could probably pick away 10%–20%" of Illumina's business in three years, and be "quite threatening" over 10. Roche sequencing could reach $1–2 billion in 10 years, "a complete dart at the wall." The industry could be about $5 billion in a decade.

On Roche itself: it has been "talking a lot and not saying much… put up or shut up." "Ultima's doomed." PacBio, at about $150 million in revenue: "who needs them?"

**Other points.** Plasmidsaurus, a large ONT customer, began RNA-seq on newly bought Illumina instruments, and the expert would be surprised if it were not evaluating AXELIOS. A population-scale epigenetics program would be "an insane growth driver," but would need a 2008-style funding renaissance that they do not expect.

## Cross-call themes

**Illumina's installed base is intact in Q3 2026.** Of roughly 25 customer calls, nearly all run 100% of short-read on Illumina, and only three report actual displacement. These are the South Carolina core (Element), the Tennessee center (Ultima, then Element) and the Element-first academic core.

The moat is described consistently as ecosystem, validation and depreciation, not hardware. Clinical labs cite $100,000–200,000 and hundreds of samples per validation. Pharma cites eight-year depreciation. Several labs said the NovaSeq X roadmap "changes nothing." Compared with earlier periods, the discussion has moved from whether Illumina can defend itself to how fast erosion happens.

**Roche AXELIOS sentiment cooled into launch.** Excitement after AGBT 2025 gave way to wait-and-see. The concerns are:

- a narrow assay menu
- tedious library prep
- carry-over on reusable sensors and drift as they age
- a price of $600,000–750,000, above what some labs hoped
- bioinformatics compared with DRAGEN
- research-only status

Estimates of share loss range from "25% threat" and "niche," through 10–20% in three years, to Illumina losing dominance in 5–10 years. Pharma users rate AXELIOS's cost per read the most seriously, but expect the contest around 2030.

**Ultima, not Roche, is increasingly named the real cost threat.** Customers cite reagents at roughly one-third to one-fifth of Illumina's cost and about $80 per genome. Element wins mid-throughput cost-sensitive cores. A clinical lab rejected Ultima over blind spots in about 10% of the genome.

**Demand split by funding source.** Federally funded academic cores are mostly flat to down about 10%. NIH delays, notices of award arriving late, and a shift of work to CROs and hubs drive this. Biopharma-, philanthropy- and clinically driven labs grew 15–50%, led by single-cell, Perturb-seq, MRD and rare-disease WGS.

Imaging-based spatial (Xenium, Atera) is a new, repeatedly flagged risk of cannibalizing sequencing. Long-read share is creeping up through isoforms, Fiber-seq and PacBio's SPRQ-Nx price cuts, but largely as an addition to short-read.

**Pricing is mixed.** Several labs reported increases of 5–10%, including a 6% tariff surcharge and freight. Others saw flat prices and contract locks. There are signs of competitive response: a NovaSeq X trade-in "discount war," 25B flow cell discounts, cheaper 5B/10B kits and free research reagents. The value of TruPath at $395 was widely doubted.
