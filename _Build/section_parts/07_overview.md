# Key takeaways: expert calls on Illumina and the sequencing market

This section collects about two dozen expert interviews held between early 2026 and October 2026. The experts include former Illumina employees, customers running academic sequencing cores (shared university labs that sequence for other researchers), clinical lab directors, and staff at rivals such as Roche, PacBio and Oxford Nanopore. Most of what they say is experienced opinion rather than verified data. Many work for competitors, and several have Illumina knowledge that is years old. Taken together, though, the calls draw a consistent picture. Illumina remains firmly entrenched, especially with clinical customers. Its core sequencing business is commoditizing (turning into a standard product competed on price). Its competitors each attack a different slice of its market.

## Illumina is still the default, but its share is slipping

Every expert described Illumina as the incumbent. It was called "the gold standard" [Expert: Roche UK precision medicine lead, Jul 2026], "the workhorse of the industry" [Expert: Oxford Nanopore senior director, ~Oct 2026], and "almost the IBM" of sequencing, meaning nobody gets fired for choosing it [Expert: former Illumina associate director now at Kailos, Aug 2026]. Its advantages are an end-to-end system of instrument, sample-prep kits and analysis software, a trained workforce that learned on Illumina, and clinical tests already validated on its machines.

Experts agree that share is eroding, but their estimates of Illumina's current share differ widely and none cites a firm source. The table below lists what each expert claimed, so the spread is visible.

| Expert | Share claim |
|---|---|
| Former Illumina associate director, product management [24 Jul 2026] | Fell from ~80% to mid-60s%, losing ~3 points a year, likely to level off near 50% |
| Former Illumina field application scientist [24 Jul 2026] | Fell from 80% to 70%, with "at least 30%" more to lose by about 2029–2030 |
| Former Illumina associate director, now at Kailos [Aug 2026] | 80%+ of installed instruments |
| LifeStrands Australia lab director [25 Jul 2026] | "Something like 95%" (a loose guess) |

The common thread is gradual decline rather than collapse. As a Natera manager put it, "It's not going to be in six months, they will lose 30%" [Expert: Natera associate director, Jul 2026]. The academic cores and a large genome center expected little or no share loss over the next 12 months.

## Clinical customers are both the growth engine and a concentration risk

The experts broadly agree that clinical sequencing (using DNA tests to diagnose and treat patients) is now where Illumina's growth comes from. Academic research is the main drag. One former employee said clinical was 52% of Illumina revenue when they left in September 2024 [Expert: former Illumina associate director, Aug 2026]. Several experts pointed to two growth areas. The first is liquid biopsy, which tests for cancer from a blood sample. The second is MRD (minimal residual disease) testing, which looks for traces of cancer left after treatment.

Research demand is suffering from US funding cuts, including the 2025 NIH cap on university overhead charges [Expert: PacBio segment lead, Aug 2026]. Research-use sales have "fallen off a cliff," in the view of one expert [Nanopore director, ~Oct 2026]. The academic channel checks are mixed:

- **Large genome center:** expects 2026 volume to fall up to about 5%.
- **Arizona core:** expects 2026 to be roughly flat, against an internal growth target of 10% to 20%.
- **South Carolina core:** output up about 40% year over year in Q2 2026, but it plans to move most in-house work to Element's AVITI24.

The concern about clinical growth is concentration. Clinical growth comes from roughly 10 to 20 large labs such as Natera, Guardant, Tempus and Caris [Nanopore director, ~Oct 2026]. These labs have bargaining power. A rival offering to "cut your cost by half" could have "a really big impact," and Illumina risks being "only a supplier" [former Illumina associate director, Aug 2026].

## The core sequencing business is flat at best, so Illumina is building an ecosystem

Former employees explained why Illumina's growth stalled from about 2022. In earlier generations, a price cut of half would lead customers to sequence four times as much. That pattern broke. Since 2022, Illumina's blended price per gigabase (billion DNA letters) has fallen from about $5 toward $2.50, and the volume gains only offset that decline. For core sequencing consumables (the reagent kits customers buy for every run), "flat would be a huge win" [Expert: former Illumina associate director, 23 Jul 2026].

The NovaSeq X, Illumina's flagship sequencer, made this worse. It delivered so much cheaper capacity that customers "sequenced everything in the freezer" and then ran out of samples [Kailos expert, Aug 2026].

Illumina's response, as the experts describe it, has three parts:

- **New consumables on existing machines.** The 35B flow cell cuts price per gigabase by about 20%.
- **Ecosystem products that only run on Illumina sequencers.** These include its five-base methylation assay, StrataMap spatial product, PIPseq single-cell, TruPath synthetic long reads, and the SomaLogic proteomics (protein-measurement) acquisition.
- **Reagent rental.** Illumina now places instruments free in exchange for multi-year consumable commitments. One expert said Illumina refused this approach until at least mid-2025 [23 Jul 2026]. Another said Illumina now offers it [Expert: former Illumina field application scientist, 24 Jul 2026].

Experts called the multi-omics direction sensible but unproven. Customers have barely responded to SomaLogic so far. Illumina is widely seen as late against 10x Genomics in single-cell and spatial biology (measuring gene activity cell by cell, or by location in tissue).

## Roche's AXELIOS is the most debated threat

Views on Roche's new sequencer span the whole range, and they shifted as the product approached launch. AXELIOS uses a chemistry called SBX (sequencing by expansion). In an early-2026 call, a former Illumina manager said its speed matters little in cancer testing [Tegus, est. Jan 2026].

In July 2026, around the research-use launch:

- A Natera manager called Roche "the main disruptor" [Jul 2026].
- A former Illumina product lead called AXELIOS "not ready." They expected it to win perhaps 10 to 15 of every 100 sequencers bought in 2027 [23 Jul 2026].

From August onward, views were more measured:

- The PacBio lead called it "a lot of hype without a lot of data" [Aug 2026].
- A genome center VP expected minimal impact within a year, but greater pressure than smaller rivals later [31 Jul 2026].
- The Nanopore director thought Roche could take 10% to 20% over about three years [~Oct 2026].
- A Brigham single-cell specialist found AXELIOS data "very similar" to Illumina's on a few datasets. They estimated Roche needs five to ten years to build a real ecosystem [~Oct 2026].

Experts broadly agree on AXELIOS's claimed strengths: reads of about 1,000 DNA letters, a roughly four-hour genome versus about 24 hours on Illumina, and a duplex 30X genome (reading each DNA position about 30 times) at about $150. They disagree on accuracy. One expert put it at Q38, a standard accuracy score, as a best case. Another cited about 45 to 52. Others ranged from "maybe not quite as good as Illumina" to higher than Illumina. The underlying chemistry was called "cumbersome."

## Switching is slow and mostly happens around new projects

The main reason the experts expect gradual change is the cost of switching platforms, especially for clinical labs. The estimates they gave scale with the stakes:

- **Clinical revalidation:** $100,000 to $200,000 [LifeStrands, Jul 2026].
- **Converting an established lab:** four to five years [Nanopore director, ~Oct 2026].
- **Moving an FDA-approved test:** up to $200 million in worst-case clinical trials [Expert: former Illumina bioinformatician, Jul 2026].
- **Adaptive's clonoSEQ:** still runs on an older Illumina model after about a decade [24 Jul 2026].

The pattern is that rivals are added beside Illumina rather than replacing it. They win new labs, new projects, contract renewals and price-sensitive academic work. Ultima, for example, is pulling projects away from a Georgia core [31 Jul 2026].

One mechanism worth watching is technology transfer. In this arrangement, a test company trains a hospital lab to run its test locally. A Roche UK lead noted that NHS labs must copy the partner's equipment. If Natera or Guardant revalidated their tests on a cheaper sequencer, those labs would follow [29 Jul 2026].

## Long reads remain a niche

Long-read sequencing reads much longer continuous stretches of DNA. Nearly every expert sees it as valuable but specialized, at roughly 10% of demand. That figure was usually suggested by interviewers, not experts. Its main uses are rare disease, large DNA rearrangements, epigenetics (chemical marks on DNA) and pathogens. It is held back by cost, missing population databases and immature clinical software.

Illumina's TruPath, a synthetic long-read product, drew mixed views. Adoption guesses ranged from 1% to 2% of volume [genome center VP] to 5% to 6% [South Carolina core], while the Georgia core was "not at all likely" to use it.

## Pricing and data are the new battlegrounds

Experts describe short-read pricing as "a race to the bottom" [PacBio lead, Aug 2026]. Headline genome prices cluster around $100 to $200. Their complaints about Illumina's pricing centered on three points:

- **Opaque individualized quotes:** list prices differ from what customers are actually quoted.
- **Steep small-lab premiums:** small users can pay up to "100X" more per DNA letter than large ones [Daisy Lab co-founder, Jul 2026].
- **Broken discount promises:** one core said promised discounts were not honored [Georgia core, Jul 2026].

Several experts noted that once sequencing is cheap, sample prep and data analysis dominate total cost. Interpreting results, not producing them, is now the bottleneck.

## What changed over the year

The calls show four shifts over 2026:

- **Roche:** went from a theoretical rival to a launched research product with early real data, though no clear customer wins yet.
- **Illumina's commercial stance:** moved from premium pricing toward ecosystem lock-in, a cheaper flow cell, and reagent rental.
- **Academic funding:** worsened, with experts by late July less optimistic about NIH money for 2027.
- **Element and Ultima:** after "a lot of hype" a few years ago, they have gained adoption more slowly than expected [PacBio lead, Aug 2026]. Roche's arrival has displaced them as the main topic of concern.
