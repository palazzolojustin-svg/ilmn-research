Between August 2023 and December 2024, Illumina's management made ten investor-facing appearances: nine conference fireside chats (moderated question-and-answer sessions on stage) and one special strategy call. Together they trace a clear arc. In late 2023, investor relations and the CFO defended a sharp guidance cut while the company had no permanent CEO. A new CEO, Jacob Thaysen, arrived in September 2023 and promised focus and discipline. A new CFO, Ankur Dhingra, arrived in April 2024. In August 2024 the company set out a multi-year strategy with financial targets, and through late 2024 it tracked progress against that plan.

Almost everything below is a management claim rather than a verified fact. Statements made by the hosting analysts are labelled as theirs. These transcripts were produced by automated transcription, and some passages are garbled; where a reading is uncertain, that is noted.

## UBS MedTech, Tools and Genomics Summit (17 Aug 2023)

Salli Schwartz, Illumina's VP of Investor Relations, spoke with UBS analyst John Sourbeer about a week after the Q2 2023 earnings call. Some audience questions came in through an iPad and QR code. Schwartz was upbeat about products and demand but candid about the guidance cut, calling it a "meaningful takedown, which is frustrating, but temporary in a lot of the drivers" [UBS, 2023-08-17].

### Why 2023 guidance was cut

Illumina had guided "core Illumina" to grow 6% to 9% in 2023 and cut that to roughly flat. Core Illumina means the business excluding GRAIL, the cancer-screening company Illumina had acquired. Schwartz attributed about 25% of the shortfall to China. The other 75% was split roughly evenly between the NovaSeq X transition and customer caution caused by the macro environment.

- **China.** The expected second-half recovery "just not happened." There was also a company-specific problem: "more steeply discounted pricing coming from our primary competitor," which lowered Illumina's win rate.
- **NovaSeq X transition.** The NovaSeq X is Illumina's newest high-throughput sequencer, launched in late 2022. Schwartz said it "has been going really well, lots of demand, lots of placements" (instruments installed at customers). However, customers were running down ("bleeding down") their stocks of NovaSeq 6000 consumables faster than expected. Consumables are the reagent kits customers must buy for every sequencing run. Early technical issues also took time to fix across all customers, which delayed consumables purchases.
- **Macro caution.** Schwartz noted hopeful signs, including Walmart's consumer trends, retail sales, inflation data and central bank signals. Even so, customers were taking longer to decide, spending cautiously and managing expenses tightly.

### NovaSeq X demand

At the Q2 call Illumina reported about 260 cumulative NovaSeq X orders, about 60 of them in Q2, and Sourbeer noted that placement guidance had been raised.

- About 40% of orders came from clinical customers (labs running patient tests). Schwartz said this was unlike past launches, because these customers were building new tests rather than replacing NovaSeq 6000 work, so the demand was additive.
- On the Q1 call Illumina had said about 15% of orders came from customers new to Illumina or new to high-throughput sequencing. Part of this came from ambient shipping: X consumables ship at room temperature without dry ice. Schwartz called this "a huge deal to our customers," because it opens "more far flung regions" and emerging markets where cold chain (refrigerated logistics) cannot be guaranteed. Illumina had last cited selling into about 35 countries.
- Customer feedback was described as "pretty phenomenal." Illumina had "rolled out more Xs faster and to more places than we have done any launch before." Schwartz said supporting all those installations took time, which was part of the guidance cut.

### Pull-through and the 6000 replacement math

Pull-through is annual consumables revenue per installed instrument. Schwartz said X pull-through would be volatile because the installed base was growing so fast. At launch Illumina had said stability would take six to eight quarters. The 10B flow cell was available; a flow cell is the consumable chip on which sequencing happens, and the number denotes its capacity. The larger 25B flow cell was "very highly anticipated" for Q4 2023 and was expected to start a consumables ramp. Schwartz allowed for "a little bit of budget flush" (customers spending leftover year-end budgets) but did not want to overstate it.

Illumina had about 1,000 NovaSeq 6000 customers. About 750 owned one unit, and Schwartz expected all of them to replace it over time. About 250 owned several, roughly 25% of the base. About 20% of X orders were multi-unit purchases, broadly matching that share, and some early adopters had placed follow-on orders. Average NovaSeq 6000 pull-through had been cut to $800,000-$900,000 on the Q2 call, because research customers were moving to the X faster than expected. Clinical customers would keep their validated 6000 tests longer. Schwartz was also enthusiastic about liquid biopsy (cancer testing from blood) and MRD (minimal residual disease, testing for leftover cancer after treatment). She noted single-cell news that morning and said "there's a ton of opportunity here."

### Mid-throughput (NextSeq)

Schwartz said sales cycles were lengthening because of both macro conditions and competition, and that procurement departments like to evaluate alternatives. Even so, Illumina's win rate in this segment accelerated in Q2 versus Q1 2023. Pull-through ranges moved as follows:

- **NextSeq 550:** cut from $100,000-$150,000 to $80,000-$130,000, reflecting China and the loss of COVID surveillance demand.
- **NextSeq 1000/2000:** held at $120,000-$170,000. Q2 came in at the low end, while the installed base grew 8% in the quarter.

XLEAP, the new chemistry used on the NovaSeq X, would come to the NextSeq 1000/2000 in the first half of 2024 as a software-style upgrade, with no new instrument needed. Schwartz said anticipation was helping current sales, and the onboard DRAGEN analysis system was a draw. DRAGEN is Illumina's built-in data analysis hardware and software. She expected this transition to be "more contained" than the NovaSeq X transition.

### Costs, margins and long-term growth

Illumina was cutting costs by downsizing, delaying or dropping projects. This meant headcount cuts, lower vendor and travel spend, and a pending real-estate review. R&D was cut but remained "a key point of commitment." The 25% operating margin target came from the October 2022 Investor Day and was reiterated on the Q2 call. Operating margin is profit from operations as a share of revenue. The transcript frames the target year inconsistently: it refers to 2025 in one place, while Schwartz spoke of "next year," meaning 2024.

Schwartz said CFO Joydeep Goswami had called the target a stretch. The gap to close had widened from 300 to 500 basis points, where a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point. The levers were:

- further operating-expense trimming;
- gross margin gains from better absorption of fixed factory costs as X production ramps;
- a mix shift back toward higher-margin consumables;
- cuts to cost of goods sold.

Gross margin is revenue minus production costs, as a share of revenue. Schwartz called the historical 25-30% range "a very reasonable place for us to be."

Sourbeer noted China was about 10% of revenue. Schwartz said mid-teens long-term growth "still makes sense," though probably not in a straight line. That growth would come from lightly penetrated markets, reimbursement and awareness, and elasticity. Elasticity is the idea that lower prices drive disproportionately more use: more samples, multiomics (measuring several molecular layers per sample), and moves from exomes to whole genomes or liquid biopsy. Schwartz described clinical markets as "bigger and faster growing than research." The NovaSeq X Dx, the diagnostic-cleared version of the instrument, had "a nice rollout," including in China and in Europe, where some countries' rules require Dx instruments.

### SEC, GRAIL, long reads and population programs

- **SEC.** The Q2 10-Q disclosed an SEC inquiry. Illumina had received a document request letter in July and was cooperating. Schwartz linked the interest to the "very public proxy fight," regulatory proceedings in several countries and the CEO's departure earlier in 2023.
- **GRAIL decision tree.** A ruling from the U.S. Fifth Circuit appeals court (on the FTC's challenge) was expected by end-2023. A ruling from the EU Court of Justice on whether the EU had jurisdiction at all was expected in late 2023 or early 2024. Winning the EU case would void the €432 million EU fine. If Illumina lost either case, it would divest without further appeal. If it won both, the Board would keep GRAIL or divest based on shareholder value. The European Commission's divestment order was hoped for in Q3, likely September.
- **Long-read sequencing.** Long-read sequencing reads much longer DNA stretches; competitors such as PacBio and Oxford Nanopore were not named. Schwartz called it complementary to short reads, which are cheaper "by quite a bit." Illumina's long-read product was announced in March 2023 and rolled out in May 2023. An enrichment assay was coming "very soon," and combined with the 25B flow cell it would give a $600 whole genome with long reads. More than 40 customers were evaluating it.
- **Population programs.** National sequencing programs numbered "30 something" and made up less than 5% of revenue.

## Morgan Stanley Global Healthcare Conference (11 Sep 2023)

CFO Joydeep Goswami (also Chief Strategy and Corporate Development Officer) and Schwartz spoke with Morgan Stanley analyst Tejas Savant days after Jacob Thaysen was named CEO. Goswami was confident and defensive on competition but candid about missed expectations.

**New CEO.** Goswami said Illumina was "thrilled" and that, on the CEO search, "I'm glad that's behind us." He said Thaysen brings diagnostics and tools experience and strength "on the stronger margins front at a scale company like Agilent."

**China.** Goswami gave no numerical split among the causes, which included macro weakness, BGI competition (BGI is the Chinese sequencer maker), local-supplier preference in tenders (competitive bids), discounting, and U.S. limits on which Chinese entities Illumina may sell to. Illumina had expected China to return to normal growth in 2023 without stimulus, but the recovery stalled from late Q2, and peers then reported "a very similar kind of outlook." Second-half guidance already assumed more deals lost to BGI and some price pressure. Goswami said Illumina is "pretty directive" about which customers it will compete for on price. The guidance did not include China's new hospital anti-corruption crackdown. Goswami said Illumina was "not concerned" about its own compliance but could not control effects on end customers.

**Why the pressure hit mid-throughput, not high-throughput.** In high throughput, Goswami said, customers also value workflow, data analysis, data compression, backward compatibility and ambient shipping. He noted that competition is not new, citing Thermo and QIAGEN in the past. Illumina still earns a price premium even in China, although expired original patents let copycats into mid-throughput. Mid-throughput win rates were unchanged everywhere except China; what had slowed was conversion of the sales funnel. Schwartz called the NovaSeq X "unparalleled or unrivaled" and said "no one else can compete with" it. Goswami saw no spillover to Europe yet: Germany was "one of our faster-growing markets in Europe," despite press reports of German economic strain. Q4 guidance assumed only normal seasonality, including the usual year-end lift in sequencing and microarrays (chip-based genotyping, including direct-to-consumer kits). It did not assume a big budget flush, and the expected Q4 bump was tied to the 25B flow cell.

**NovaSeq X.** About 110 instruments had been purchased, about 10% penetration of roughly 1,000 NovaSeq customers. Among the roughly 750 single-unit customers, penetration was about 5%, or about 50 customers, because heavy multi-unit users switch first. Two unusual Q2 technical issues had fixes deployed from late Q2, and the remaining gaps were mostly due to customer vacations and lab readiness. On batching, the X has at least 2.5 times the capacity of its predecessor. Goswami said customers "somewhere in the middle" wait partly to fill a flow cell, run below full capacity and so pay a "slightly higher effective price that we recover," as happened in the earlier HiSeq-to-6000 transition.

**Mid-throughput and Element.** XLEAP was "on track" for early 2024, with no pull-forward commitment. Savant cited an Element Biosciences press release announcing 100 AVITI units. Goswami countered that rivals report placements, not orders, and may be placing boxes at sites that also own Illumina machines. He expected Illumina's premium to persist even as dollar-per-gigabase prices keep falling (a gigabase is a billion DNA letters read), which was already in the model. He expected NextSeq 1000/2000 pull-through to stay within $120,000-$170,000. Savant cited 15-20% of orders coming from new customers, and Schwartz said ambient shipping makes some of that "truly incremental."

**Pricing philosophy.** Goswami said pricing is based on customers' total cost of operation and is "very selective." New products are priced proactively, with some reactive matching. Cheaper sequencing alone has "limited utility" once sequencing is a small part of customers' costs: "We just dropped the price on DNA, it's not going to help." This is why Illumina invested in DRAGEN and data compression, and he expected competition to become more value-based.

**Margins.** Goswami said "R&D has not been a sacred cow," and cuts had been "very surgical." Illumina had invested ahead of the 2021-early 2022 growth and then found its cost structure "clearly inappropriate," although programs for core instruments and end-to-end assays remained protected. He was candid about why 2024 was a stretch. When the targets were set, Illumina expected 7.5% growth in 2023 and at least 22% margins going into 2024; instead "we are at 20% and close to flat for this year." The transcript is ambiguous about exactly which figures these are. He promised "we will get the margin expansion" but deferred to Thaysen to review the target.

**GRAIL.** Savant expected divestiture under new leadership. Goswami said the EC order "gates the options" and that Illumina had everything "locked and loaded." The two court cases were the Fifth Circuit (FTC) and the European Court of Justice (on Article 22 jurisdiction). Winning both would allow divestiture, a cheaper standalone GRAIL, licensing or other options; "if we happen to lose either one of those two cases, then divestiture is a certainty." He did not endorse Savant's suggestion that GRAIL could be off the books by mid-2024.

**M&A and strategy.** Goswami said tuck-in deals (small acquisitions) continue, but large deals are hard "given where the regulators are these days." A business development team screens technology licenses and partnerships in areas such as proteomics (protein measurement) and spatial biology (mapping molecules in tissue). Deals must improve the sequencer's utility, add value and target large, fast-growing markets. He called GRAIL "a very different kind of bet" that could have rivalled Illumina's own size. Otherwise, he said, "It's not an or, that's an and": modular R&D frees capital for end-to-end workflows. Illumina had "pivoted" this way two to three years earlier, and he said results would show in 2024-2025 launches.

## Evercore ISI HealthCONx (28 Nov 2023)

Host Vijay Kumar opened bearishly. The shares were down about 50% for the year and 50-80% off their peak, and the X had cut cost per sample from about $600 to about $200 with no sign of 3x volumes. Goswami admitted "we and others in our industry were wrong about how China would evolve in '23." He framed the problems as timing, saying "a question of when rather than if," with NGS penetration "under 10%." NGS (next-generation sequencing) is the technology that reads DNA at massive scale.

**Elasticity versus transition.** Goswami said X adopters show faster sequencing growth than non-adopters. He said elasticity runs on "a five or six year cycle": the price drop comes up front, while volume builds for years. In clinical labs, tests validated on the 6000 stay there; for example, some still run on the HiSeq six years after the 6000 arrived, which he called "astounding." Meanwhile the X hosts new tests that were not viable before, driven by reimbursement, MRD and MCED (multi-cancer early detection), so the volume is "accretive." Research customers use the X for larger cohorts, multiomics, single-cell and spatial work. Schwartz said 6000 consumables declines would offset X growth less in 2024: "we should see those lines cross."

**Pull-through and Q4.** Kumar cited a cut in 6000 pull-through to $700,000-$900,000. Goswami corrected the range to $800,000-$900,000, tracking toward the low end. Heavy users had waited for the 25B flow cell, "the real workhorse," which launched in late October 2023. He expected an X consumables ramp in the first half of 2024 and expected 25B pull-through to exceed the 6000's.

The typical customer path was a 6000 inventory burn-down, then roughly a quarter of validation runs, then a ramp with initial stocking. After that he expected the volume trend to resume "or maybe slightly accelerated." Disruption was limited to customers who had ordered an X. Q4 guidance implied sequencing consumables down mid-single digits against an easy Q4 2022 comparison. The software problems were resolved, and the 25B update also improved the 10B flow cell.

**Orders.** Q3 orders were 50 systems versus about 60 expected. Schwartz called this "still a pretty decent quarter." Goswami said the funnel "remains incredibly strong," but conversion slowed. Conversion probabilities on advanced pipeline shifted, which is why Illumina "rewound" that disclosure. He said no customers were swapping two 6000s for one X. The transcript's "about 75% have one X" likely means one 6000.

**Margins and 2024.** Gross margin was about 65-66%. Goswami said a return toward 70% "can. And it will," reaching the high 60s to low 70s over time. Schwartz said the 2017-2018 NovaSeq 6000 cycle followed the same dip-and-recover pattern. Goswami expected sequential gains for several quarters, though Q1 is seasonally weakest. On core operating margin he said "nothing structurally" prevents a return to the high 20s to 30%, but gave no date and said R&D cuts would not harm leadership.

Thaysen, about 60 days in, was running portfolio reviews and budgeting. Goswami's 2024 comment was explicitly "less to be a guidance." With consensus then at 10%-plus growth, he said revenue would be roughly in line with 2023 in dollars. He expected operating margin to be roughly flat, because higher gross margin and lower headcount would be offset by restored variable and stock compensation that went unpaid in 2023. Full guidance was due in February 2024.

**GRAIL.** The EU Court of Justice hearing was set for mid-December 2023, and the Fifth Circuit ruling was expected by year-end. A Board special committee, whose members were not named, would speed up GRAIL decisions. Kumar noted Illumina had outlaid $1 billion-plus. Goswami said inbound interest predated the divestiture order and that a Form 10 (the SEC filing needed for a spin-off) would come before year-end, enabling a sponsored spin or sale. He said "our advisers are telling us there is interest."

## Piper Sandler Healthcare Conference (29 Nov 2023)

In one of his first appearances as CEO, Thaysen spoke with David Westenberg. He gave no financial figures. His tone was upbeat and conciliatory, and he declined to criticize predecessors or be a "Monday morning" quarterback.

**Why he joined.** He cited "passion for oncology and genomics," Illumina's role as pioneer, and the energy of San Diego staff. He thought the Board wanted an industry insider with global experience and a record of commercializing innovation at similar scale, and said his profile "pretty much" fits. Responding to investor concern that he is an operator, he cited a physics PhD and founding a MEMS company (tiny semiconductor-style devices) with many patents. He also cited running R&D at Dako and said his strength is nurturing an innovation culture, including business and process innovation. He dismissed talk of a "brain drain."

**Underrated strengths.** He named three:

- the product roadmap, including automation;
- informatics, which he called "highly undervalued," citing data compressed fivefold and built-in pipelines;
- global service that reaches customers within 24 hours, which he said Illumina is "the only company" able to provide.

On roughly $4 billion of revenue, he said: "I don't want to consider us mature. I will do a lot to keep us agile, but at the same time, drive everything else to go back to be a very high profitable company."

**Competition and technology.** Westenberg said Element, Ultima and BGI were matching Illumina on cost per gigabase. Thaysen said Illumina invented that metric as a proxy for workflow cost, and that the goal now is the cost of running a whole lab, including sample prep, informatics and service. He said SBS (sequencing by synthesis, Illumina's core chemistry), "reinvented" as XLEAP, remains "the best chemistry out there." He cited papers, not company data, showing more than 80% of reads at Q40 (about one error per 10,000 bases). He said short reads suit most solid-tumor and liquid-biopsy samples, and that switching ecosystems is "simply too costly" for customers.

**Clinical elasticity.** Westenberg said over 50% of revenue is clinical. Thaysen said fewer than 50% of U.S. cancer patients are sequenced at diagnosis, and only about 1-2% for monitoring. Clinical validation slows X adoption: "even though we're all frustrated it takes time, it will come, and it will come strong back." His five-year hope is that "at least 90%" of cancer patients get full sequencing profiles, with Illumina "the central part of the ecosystem" for multiomics.

The tone was confident: "our margin and EPS story is just getting started." Management declined to give 2025 guidance.

### Vision and market

Thaysen called Illumina "a biology platform company rooted in NGS." He said the industry is moving "from cost per gigabase" to "total cost of workflow." He sized the total market at more than $125 billion, of which NGS serves about $10 billion. He put Illumina's liquid-biopsy market at nearly $800 million, growing at a 34% compound annual rate. He illustrated MRD as a $10 billion opportunity: about 20 million cancer diagnoses a year, 10 million who could benefit, and about $1,000 of consumables per patient per year.

In a clinical whole-genome workflow, sequencing is about 40% of cost and analysis and interpretation about 45%. Progress cited included 469 NovaSeq X Plus units installed since March 2023, nearly half of high-throughput data on the X, X pull-through above $1 million, and a "much more proactive stance on competition." Thaysen named pharma as a third customer group, "historically underserved." He announced that the Broad Institute would adopt the just-closed Fluent single-cell technology for ultra-high-volume Perturb-seq, screens that show how disabling genes changes cells.

### Pipeline (Barnard)

Barnard cited more than 100 invention disclosures so far in 2024 and nearly 9,000 patents. The NovaSeq X delivers more than 320 times the daily output of a decade ago, rising from 25 to 8,000 gigabases per day. It carries up to eight lanes, onboard DRAGEN for four applications per flow cell, and about 90% less packaging. Barnard called it "the fastest-growing high-throughput sequencer in our history." Two products were due within 12-18 months:

- **5-base genome.** It captures genetic variants plus methylation (an epigenetic chemical mark on DNA) from one library prep. Library prep is the step that readies DNA for the sequencer.
- **Comprehensive genome.** It moves library prep onto the flow cell and covers hard genomic regions and large structural variants. It was already in testing, and Barnard said it "could be the de-facto genome of the future."

Illumina Protein Prep was in early access, with full launch in early 2025, working with Standard BioTools. It measures 10,000 proteins, extendable to 20,000. Barnard also highlighted Partek software and AI tools, PrimateAI and SpliceAI, and said "this isn't PowerPoint science."

### Financial targets (Dhingra)

Consumables are more than two-thirds of revenue. Headcount was down about 10% from peak, 2024 savings were on track at about $100 million, and Q2 gross and operating margins each rose more than 200 basis points versus FY2023. The targets were:

- **Revenue.** High-single-digit growth by 2027, accelerating each year. Core sequencing is "by far" the largest driver, followed by multiomics ("almost more an upside") and services, which already generate more than $500 million a year growing mid-single digits.
- **Volume and the X transition.** Gigabase output averaged more than 25% annual growth over five years and recently ran 35-40%. Around mid-2025, about two-thirds of high-throughput volume and half of high-throughput consumables revenue should be on the X; by the second half of 2025, about 75% of volume.
- **Mid-throughput.** Nearly 60% of NextSeq 1000/2000 customers had upgraded for XLEAP, up from about 25% at RBC in May. Dhingra said "we're not making a call when conditions will change in China."
- **Margins.** Operating margin expansion of 500 basis points through 2027 on top of about 21% in 2024, aiming for the top-quartile "high 20s." Up to two-thirds would come from operating leverage, and $200 million of gross run-rate savings would come over 3.5 years.
- **EPS.** "Double-digit to teens" growth for 3.5 years from a base near $4. Dhingra said "the teens go from 13% to 19%."
- **Cash.** Core 2024 free cash flow of about $950 million, "approaching $1 billion" excluding GRAIL investment, plus a new $1.5 billion share buyback and tuck-in M&A only.

### Notable Q&A

- **Pricing.** "I don't think we're going to do global pricing going forward." Prices will vary by application and geography. Barnard said Illumina is "nowhere near the end of our density roadmap."
- **Volume-to-revenue math.** On McNamara's example of 25% volume growth with a 15% price headwind, Dhingra said that implies mid-to-high-single-digit growth in high-throughput consumables.
- **Growth ceiling.** Thaysen called high single digits a "new baseline."
- **R&D and gross margin.** About 75% of roughly $1 billion of R&D goes to innovation. Instruments are under 15% of revenue, and gross margin should approach about 70%. Old products were being pruned "as we speak."
- **Informatics.** Thaysen envisioned an interface "like going into a ChatGPT" for genome interpretation.
- **Population programs.** About 30 programs were running and about 50 were in the pipeline, typically starting at around 100,000 genomes. A new unit, "Saga," would serve them.
- **Gaps.** Spatial biology was not addressed.

## Wolfe Research Healthcare Conference (20 Nov 2024)

Dhingra spoke with Doug Schenkel about two weeks after the U.S. election and gave no formal guidance. He said "we are in transformation" and that "transformations take time." GRAIL was now "completely divested," with a small stake retained. Since August, key customers had received early pipeline access, and many said they had never had such meetings with Illumina. Dhingra was heavily focused on the "constellation map" genome technology, citing data Neil presented at ASHG (the American Society of Human Genetics meeting). A services leader had been hired, though services would be the smallest growth contributor. He said "cash conversion is significantly stronger than anyone else in the tools" industry and described a "very clearly defined M&A strategy," with Fluent as the first example.

**Election topics.**

- **NIH.** About half of revenue is U.S. and half research, so about 25% is "in some ways exposed to government funding," with direct NIH revenue "really small." Schenkel estimated government-funded research at about half of that and direct NIH exposure at low single digits; Dhingra said "You're probably not too far off." Dhingra noted NIH funding grew during the prior Trump term but said Illumina is not assuming growth this time. He conceded that cuts would hurt in ways "hard for us to quantify."
- **China.** Sales had stabilized for two to three quarters at about $75 million per quarter, about $300 million a year. Schenkel framed that as about 7% of sales, down about 25% year to date, and Dhingra confirmed. This is below the roughly 10% cited through January 2024. A new China leader, Jenny, restructured the team. Illumina won access to more hospital tenders and direct hospital sales and began free-trade-zone talks, which Dhingra called "small incremental steps."
- **Tariffs.** Illumina has three U.S. factories, and Singapore is its second hub. Non-U.S. supply exposure is concentrated around Singapore and Taiwan, with minimal China production. A "layer 2, layer 3" supplier review was under way, and Dhingra said "in aggregate, it's not a very large exposure."

**Volume versus revenue.** Schenkel asked how Q3 high- and mid-throughput sequencing activity could grow 40% while Wolfe modelled consumables up only about 5%. Dhingra said "Largely price": the price decline was at the high end of Illumina's range, about 25%. Slightly more than 10% of high-throughput volume moved to the X in the quarter, and 40% of clinical gigabases were already on it. "I was pleased," he said, because revenue still grew. Once more than 75% of volume is on the X in the second half of 2025, the price drop will be annualized (already in the year-ago comparison). Some customers will never move old tests, as with clinical labs still on the MiSeq.

**Mid-throughput.** "We are not assuming any macro to improve." Dhingra said the biggest drag was biotechs sending samples to core labs on cheaper high-throughput machines, which he expected to reverse when funding returns. A single-flow-cell X priced "well below 1 million" dollars and the new MiSeq i100 would squeeze the segment from both sides. Schenkel cited 2025 consensus of about 4% revenue growth and about $4.40 EPS (the EPS figure is uncertain in the transcript). Dhingra called that "good framing" and said of macro, "We'll call it when it actually happens."

## Evercore ISI HealthCONx (3 Dec 2024)

Dhingra and Schwartz spoke with Kumar. Q3 revenue landed "right at the midpoint." Consumables beat on faster X volume, while instruments missed because of macro and deal timing. Dhingra described macro as "stable, and stable at a pretty low level," and said he would call it improving only after one or two quarters of evidence. Q4 guidance "took optimism out" for any budget flush. Dhingra personally engages with the top 20-25 customers. Gigabase growth was about 40% in each of the last two quarters, versus a ten-year average of about 25%; the midpoint implies volume doubling in about three years.

**China** ran at $75-80 million per quarter, after three years of sequential declines. Illumina had won access to some tenders and local support for manufacturing, and five or six local rivals now compete mostly with each other. "Not to say we're out of the woods, anywhere near that." On stimulus, Dhingra said "I'm not banking on that," and Schwartz said stimulus so far had targeted other tools. The 2027 plan assumes no meaningful China contribution.

**Applications and population programs.** The 25% volume assumption is simply the ten-year average. Dhingra expected MRD to become accretive within three years and therapy selection to stay strong. He placed early detection further out and Perturb-seq on a three-to-four-year horizon. Illumina was in talks on about 40-50 population programs. One proposal included proteomics, and programs had scaled from 50,000 patients to 1 million over five years. A new SVP was hired to lead services.

**Transition.** In Q3, about 55% of high-throughput volume was on the X, equal to 35% of revenue, with about 40% of clinical volume on the X. The goal was at least 75% in the second half of 2025, "another three, four quarters or so." Customers are moving about one test per quarter. "It is a curve. So there isn't a magical number." Q4 consumables growth would moderate for three reasons: lapping the 25B launch, holidays, and X reagents' three-to-four-month shelf life versus at least six months for the 6000, which discourages stocking.

**Instruments.** About 60 X units were sold per quarter, about 240 for 2024, down 40% from the launch year. The installed base was 528 X units against about 1,700 active 6000s, roughly doubling high-throughput capacity in three years. If that framework holds, "200, 240" units in 2025 would be a reasonable landing point. In mid-throughput, "The name changes, the offering changes." Dhingra cited core labs, a biotech lab-space glut and good XLEAP adoption. On margins, 2024 revenue was guided down about 3% after three years without growth. He reaffirmed high-single-digit growth by 2027 and 500 basis points of expansion, with "ratable" gains in 2025. To Kumar's prompting he replied: "You're almost writing the guidance there."

## How the tone and message shifted

- **Late 2023.** Messaging was defensive and explanatory. Management attributed weakness to China, the 6000-to-X transition and macro, insisted on "when, not if," and left margin targets in limbo.
- **January 2024.** Thaysen's arrival brought a reset: no long-term numbers, three priorities, no large M&A, and a promise of discipline.
- **Spring 2024.** Dhingra added return-on-investment language and prudence on guidance.
- **August 2024.** The strategy call replaced the abandoned mid-teens growth and 25% margin goals with lower but explicit targets: high-single-digit growth by 2027, roughly 26% operating margin, and double-digit-to-teens EPS growth.
- **Late 2024.** Management sounded confident on execution, cost and transition metrics, but assumed no macro or China recovery.

Recurring disputes to watch:

- the bear case on elasticity versus management's transition story;
- whether clinical customers will move their validated tests at all;
- conflicting China revenue shares (about 10% versus about 7%);
- installed-base counts (more than 25,000 total versus more than 21,600 active).
