This part covers six management appearances between January 2025 and March 2026. They fall into three groups:

- **Two JPMorgan Healthcare Conference presentations**, each used to preannounce fourth-quarter results.
- **The AGBT 2025 investor session**, a Q&A-only event at the main genomics technology meeting.
- **Three fireside chats** (one-on-one interviews on stage) at Bernstein, Wolfe Research and TD Cowen.

The CEO, Jacob Thaysen, spoke at every event. CFO Ankur Dhingra and CTO Steve Barnard joined some of them. Over the period the conversation moved from "can Illumina return to growth?" to "is the guidance too conservative?" The NovaSeq X transition, the "highest-quality insight at the lowest end-to-end cost" strategy and the 2027 targets stayed constant throughout. China, NIH funding, tariffs and Roche's entry into sequencing came and went as headwinds.

## JPMorgan Healthcare Conference (January 14, 2025)

### Format

This was a 40-minute session. Thaysen gave a prepared presentation, then he and Dhingra took questions from Rachel Vatnsdal of JPMorgan's Life Science Tools and Diagnostics team. All 2025 figures were labelled "preliminary." Full guidance was due at the Q4 2024 earnings call on February 6, 2025.

The tone was confident and enthusiastic. Management deliberately held back on two points: 2025 instrument placements, and the continuing first-half price headwind from the NovaSeq X.

### Illumina's position and the size of the opportunity

Thaysen opened by describing Illumina as the pioneer of next-generation sequencing (NGS, technology that reads DNA in massive parallel batches). He said the company had built it over more than 25 years and that it is now integral to research and clinical work worldwide [JPM, 2025-01-14].

- **Data volume.** In 2024, customers generated more than 480 petabases of data on Illumina machines. A petabase is a quadrillion DNA letters, and this volume is roughly equivalent to 5 million whole genomes.
- **Clinical mix.** 56% of sequencing consumables revenue now came from clinical customers. Consumables are the reagent kits customers must buy for every run.
- **Business model (management claim).** Revenue is diversified across products, markets and geographies. This supports healthy free cash flow and a solid balance sheet.

**Ten-year vision.** Thaysen's view of the next decade had four parts:

- NGS becomes embedded in personalized care.
- Countries adopt whole-genome sequencing, moving "from sick care into health care."
- Multiomics (measuring many biological layers beyond DNA, such as RNA and proteins) unlocks deeper insight.
- AI-powered cohorts of genomic and multiomic data transform partners' drug discovery.

**Changing the industry's yardstick.** Illumina wants customers to stop focusing only on cost per gigabase (the cost of each billion DNA letters read). The new goal is "the highest quality insight for the lowest end-to-end cost."

**Market size.** Thaysen cited a total addressable market of more than $125 billion, of which NGS serves only about $10 billion today.

**Growth drivers.**
- *Clinical:* genomics becoming standard of care in therapy selection, early detection and MRD (minimal residual disease, testing for leftover cancer after treatment).
- *Research:* multiomics, which requires intensive sequencing of many more samples.

### Preliminary Q4 2024 results and 2025 outlook

Thaysen said Q4 2024 beat Illumina's own expectations, "primarily driven by greater and as expected NovaSeq X shipments." The NovaSeq X is Illumina's newest high-throughput sequencer.

The table below shows the preliminary Q4 numbers and the first 2025 outlook. The key point is that Illumina promised margin and earnings growth well ahead of revenue growth.

| Item | Figure | Comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Q4 2024 core revenue | About $1.1 billion | Up about 1% year on year |
| Q4 2024 non-GAAP operating margin | About 19.7% | — |
| Q4 2024 non-GAAP EPS | $0.91–$0.93 | — |
| 2025 revenue growth | Low single digits, constant currency (excluding exchange-rate effects) | 2024 was "negative a few percent" |
| 2025 reported revenue | $4.28–$4.40 billion | — |
| 2025 non-GAAP operating margin | About 23% | Up 170 basis points (1.7 percentage points) from 2024 |
| 2025 non-GAAP EPS growth | About 10% | Helped by "below the line" items (those beneath operating income) |

Non-GAAP figures exclude one-off items such as restructuring costs. Full-year 2024 results were shown on a slide but are not in the transcript. The outlook assumed current macroeconomic conditions and no legislative changes.

Thaysen credited innovation, product launches, customer focus and operational excellence for the progress. He said improving margins "even under tough market conditions" proved the new operating model.

### 2027 targets and capital allocation

**2027 targets.** These were set at the August 2024 strategy update and reiterated here:
- High single-digit revenue growth.
- 500 basis points of operating-margin expansion, which Thaysen put at 26% in 2027.
- Double-digit-to-teens EPS growth over 2025–2027.

He called 2024 a "solid initial step," said Illumina was "well on track," and added that 2027 "is not the end of the journey, we're just getting started."

**Capital allocation priorities, in order:**
1. Invest in the business (platforms, multiomics, and services/software/data).
2. Bolt-on acquisitions, plus partnerships.
3. Keep an investment-grade balance sheet.
4. Share buybacks meant to offset dilution, with opportunistic extras. Buybacks resumed after the August 2024 update, and about $116 million was repurchased over the rest of 2024.

### The three growth drivers

**1. NovaSeq X.**
- Illumina placed 91 X systems in Q4 2024.
- Pull-through (consumables revenue per instrument) averaged $1.3 million per X in 2024.
- By the second half of 2025, roughly 75% of high-throughput gigabases shipped and about 50% of high-throughput consumables revenue should be on the X. Management said that once most volume sits on the cheaper X, the price cut stops diluting revenue and extra volume turns into growth.
- Thaysen said customer data showed deeper and broader sequencing on the X, which "underpins my confidence."

**MiSeq i100.** This is a small benchtop sequencer, announced in October 2024, with shipments starting in December. More than 70 units were placed by year-end. Some customers use it to trial experiments before scaling up on the X.

**2. Multiomics.**
- *Constellation Mapped Reads.* This product reveals large structural variants (big DNA rearrangements) that short reads miss, with a much simpler workflow. Thaysen said key opinion leaders (KOLs, influential researchers) were "thrilled." He cited one KOL who said no technology had previously been able to detect the most common causes of hemophilia A.
- *Proteomics (protein measurement).* Built with Standard BioTools on SOMAmer protein-binding reagents and launching in the first half of 2025. It uses a 9,000-protein panel, which Thaysen called the largest orthogonally validated NGS proteomics panel. He cited a median coefficient of variation (a reproducibility measure; lower is better) under 5% across all 9,000 proteins, and said it outperforms rival NGS proteomics products. He predicted it "will become the standard."
- Both run on the DRAGEN-led software stack. DRAGEN is Illumina's genomic analysis software and hardware.

**3. Services, software and data.** This driver draws on population studies and drug discovery. Partnerships with NVIDIA, Truveta and others had been announced the day before.

Thaysen's closing promise was "We will return to growth in 2025." He said the X would drive most 2025 growth, with multiomics and services adding growth layers in 2026–2027.

### Q&A highlights

**NVIDIA.** Thaysen said multiomics produces more data than anyone can analyze "with an Excel sheet," so AI is essential. The partnership has two parts:
- Bring DRAGEN to GPU and CPU platforms, beyond its current FPGA (hardware-accelerated chip) form.
- Offer NVIDIA's foundation-model tools (tools for building large AI models) on Illumina's platform.

He called it "the starting of a new era."

**The 91 placements.** Vatnsdal noted that guidance had implied about 60 placements in Q4 (the second half was meant to exceed the first half's 117).
- Thaysen said customers were planning for 2025, and existing X owners were buying five- and ten-packs.
- Dhingra called the result "well beyond our expectations." Guidance had assumed the slow deal closings of the first three quarters would continue, but clinical customers showed "pretty sizable interest" at year-end and bought multiple units. Dhingra read this as capacity being added ahead of applications like MRD.
- Neither addressed whether reagent rental (instruments supplied in exchange for reagent commitments) played a role.

**Q4 consumables.** Neither executive confirmed or corrected the analyst's estimate of about $720–730 million.
- Thaysen said Q4 is seasonally slower because of fewer working days and holidays.
- Dhingra said underlying sequencing activity grew 30%–40% in the first three quarters and stayed strong. Usage dipped between Thanksgiving and Christmas, landing within expected ranges.

**NIH and the new administration.** Thaysen said the NIH budget is set in the fall, so he expected little change in 2025. He said genomics "likely will see increased funding" even under overall pressure, and that he was "more positive today" than a few months earlier. He noted "a lot of curveballs over the last five years." No change in customer behavior since the election was cited.

**Why only low single-digit growth in 2025.**
- Thaysen said 2025 is still a transition year. Consumables growth would be positive but "still not to the level that corresponds to the actually underlying volume."
- Dhingra flagged geopolitics as a watch item. He said new launches (Constellation, single cell, proteomics) position Illumina well regardless of funding.

**Margin levers.** Thaysen said 2025 growth "is not strong enough to drive the overall margin improvement," so operational excellence must deliver it, which he said is not simply cost cutting. Dhingra said the gains would span gross margin and operating expenses while Illumina keeps investing.

**2025 placements.** Thaysen warned against multiplying 91 by four. Q4 is always the strongest quarter, and Illumina expected "maybe… a lower level into '25."

**Ramp to 2027.** Thaysen described "a continuous movement," not a step change, driven mostly by having volume on the X by 2026. He said multiomics would not be meaningful before 2026–2027. He also mentioned a new unit, SAGA, focused on arrays and services.

**Constellation.** In August 2024 this product was called "comprehensive genome." Thaysen explained how it works:
- Unfragmented DNA, up to millions of bases long, lies on the flow cell.
- The instrument's camera links each read to its physical position.
- This reveals structural variants and phasing (which variants sit on the same chromosome copy).

"I've tried it. It actually works," he said, predicting it would be "the standard of the genome." Demand exceeded Illumina's capacity to work with researchers, and the target was a 2026 launch. No pricing was given.

**X transition status.** Dhingra said over 60% of volume had transitioned. The pricing impact would be heaviest in the first half of 2025 and would ease in the second half.

**Mid-throughput competition.** Thaysen admitted "a little more competition" but said Illumina still wins many deals and named no rivals. Illumina's responses:
- XLEAP chemistry, offering higher performance at a lower price.
- The MiSeq i100, pushing into mid-throughput with fast turnaround and room-temperature reagents. He said room-temperature reagents will come to "all our platforms."
- Unspecified commercial initiatives.

**R&D priorities.** Thaysen said "customers are not looking for a sequencer. They're looking for an application." He named end-to-end proteomics, single-cell and Constellation-based whole-genome solutions. He called informatics "almost still the best kept secret."

## AGBT 2025 Investor Session (February 25, 2025)

### Format

This session took place at AGBT (Advances in Genome Biology and Technology), in Marco Island, Florida. It was Q&A only, hosted by Head of IR Salli Schwartz, with Thaysen, Barnard and Dhingra answering. No guidance was given.

The tone was confident and combative. Thaysen framed competition as sequencer makers against end-to-end workflow providers, and Barnard pointedly challenged rivals' technical claims. All comparisons below are management claims that the analysts did not test. Several names were garbled in the transcript; for example, "DARCO" is likely Dako.

### What each executive chose to highlight

- **Thaysen chose Constellation.** He called it "an unbelievable cool technology that completely sets us apart" and expected it to become the standard for rare disease and germline (inherited) sequencing. His evidence was a session the day before in which Dr. Stephen Kingsmore showed hemophilia A variants. Thaysen said this had not previously been possible, and that "even other long reach techniques have difficulties."
- **Barnard chose spatial** (measuring gene activity while keeping its location in tissue). He said Illumina now has a full portfolio "from proteomics down to genomics" across bulk, single-cell and spatial modes, being completed that year. He called it "a huge inflection point."
- **Dhingra chose Perturb-Seq**, an experiment type that switches off genes in cells and reads out the effects. Illumina had launched a 5-billion-cell atlas with the Broad Institute, to be built over "two or three years at the most." Dhingra said it lays the foundation for a pharma strategy under the services pillar and could materially speed drug discovery.

### Constellation versus long-read competitors

Thaysen said Constellation targets both the long-read market and deeper insight in existing markets such as rare disease, at Illumina-level pricing. Barnard gave the comparison summarized below; he called it "no comparison across the market today."

| Platform | Throughput and turnaround (Barnard's claims) | Library prep |
|---|---|---|
| Illumina Constellation | 16 complete genomes, with phasing and structural variants, in 24 hours on a 10B flow cell (eight lanes) | About 10 minutes |
| PacBio | "Maybe three genomes incomplete," over 24 hours of sequencing | Two days |
| Oxford Nanopore | About three days for one sample, not at diagnostic-grade variant accuracy | — |

Library prep is the lab step that readies DNA for sequencing.

**Price.** Asked whether Constellation meant $1,000 per genome, Thaysen said the price is "in the hundreds of dollars, it's not in the thousands." It was in early access at one genome per lane. Multiplexing (several genomes per lane) would cut cost further. He said a premium price is possible but warned against dividing the cost of a 10B flow cell by eight: "We want to open up markets with it."

**Single cell and spatial pricing.**
- Single cell: Thaysen claimed a cost per cell "at least two times less than anybody else."
- Spatial: Illumina can image whole slides, whereas rivals cover only small areas. Citing his pathology background, Thaysen said tissue needs "centimeters of insights," not millimeters. Pricing would scale from a few experiments to thousands.

### Roche's new sequencing technology

Roche was presenting its technology later that day.

**Thaysen's view.**
- Customers "are buying into a whole ecosystem," and Illumina already offers one, with automated sample prep feeding directly into the sequencer.
- Roche had shown only a "technology overview," not a product, so he would not speculate on details.

**Barnard's view.** He described two lines of competition:
1. *Whole-genome diagnostics.* Roche's technology is "still classically short read," whereas Constellation adds phasing, structural variants and 10-minute prep.
2. *Counting applications* (RNA, single cell, spatial). Here sequencing is only 10%–20% of experiment cost, so cheaper sequencing targets "the wrong Pareto." Spatial prep takes two days with costly kits, which is why there has been no "really big science in spatial." The Fluent acquisition (instrument-free, scalable library prep) and Illumina's flexible spatial product address this.

Barnard questioned whether Roche "really understands" the NGS market.

**Roche as a diagnostics competitor.** Thaysen said he competed successfully against Roche's Ventana unit while at Dako. His arguments:
- Diagnostics success depends on companion diagnostics (tests paired with a specific drug), and pharma prefers independent partners.
- Illumina's TSO 500 tumor test is now an FDA-approved kit with companion-diagnostic claims, with more being added to its labeling.
- Clinical customers "care about insight." A new IVD (in vitro diagnostic) technology "takes a long time" because it must perform repeatedly in decentralized labs, not just in a KOL's hands.
- A brand alone does not establish a new technology: "it remains to be seen."
- Because Roche owns Foundation Medicine, many of its potential customers "might consider twice" before buying from a vertically integrated competitor.

**Accuracy and the quality bar.**
- Barnard said Illumina has "a very nice IP portfolio in nanopores," but will not launch below Q30. Q30 means about one error per 1,000 bases.
- A technology starting at Q20 must compensate with "exotic, time consuming" library prep, which he said Roche's three-instrument workflow would show.
- He said clinical expectations will exceed Q50 within two to five years because of MRD's "needle in a haystack" demands. Getting there requires a native Q30 start plus library prep.
- He called Roche's "clinical company" positioning "a big question mark."
- Thaysen added that duplex sequencing (reading both DNA strands) "comes with a penalty" of extra sequencing.
- Thaysen noted the MiSeq i100's CMOS chip readout already sequences in four hours, and that Illumina could build bigger chips if speed mattered.

### Pricing logic, Ultima, reagent rental and partners

**End-to-end pricing.** Doug Schenkel (Wolfe Research) asked whether adjacencies protect sequencing spend. Thaysen said that in single cell, spatial and proteomics, lowering sequencing price barely moves total cost, while owning sample prep lets Illumina cut the prohibitive part. Cheaper prep means bigger experiments and more sequencing. "Just coming out with a sequencer" will not win.

**Reagent rental.** Thaysen said instruments are a small part of revenue and Illumina already has reagent rental deals. The model suits clinical labs with known volumes, where price can track reimbursement, but not grant-based academia. A NovaSeq buyer generates about $1.3 million of pull-through a year for five to six years, which supports lifetime-value pricing.

**Ultima.** Ultima had won business from Chan Zuckerberg and UK Biobank. Thaysen did not address those wins.

**Partners such as 10x Genomics.** Thaysen said "we will always make sure that they're successful on our platform" and that Illumina does not lock customers in. He said "some companies like to compete in press releases," and that the Broad 5-billion-cell project "is real."

### NIH, academic demand and China

**NIH.** Thaysen said customer conversations were "very positive." Budgets remain and core labs are still running volume, but there is "more concern, more risk," mostly about future grants. He said normal conditions will return: "And they will."

Barnard said nervous customers favor reliable vendors, deepening ties with core labs and KOLs. Thaysen added: "No CEOs ever get fired by hiring McKinsey… Nobody is going to be blamed with going with Illumina."

**New data point.** Dhingra said that of instruments placed in the quarter to mid-quarter, about half went to research customers.

**China.** Illumina had recently been placed on China's unreliable entity list, a government list that can restrict foreign firms. Thaysen said Illumina was "still operating completely in China." Customers want it to stay, and there had been "multiple conversations with the right parties." Illumina had known of the listing for only 48 hours at its previous check-in.

### Roadmap, informatics and the "shifting money" debate

**Accuracy roadmap.** Dan Brennan (TD Cowen) cited a Q55 slide and roughly $8 billion of Illumina R&D over a decade (the analyst's figure). Barnard said Q55 is in the pipeline for MRD. He said physicians "could care less about the Q scores"; what matters is callability (whether a variant can be called in the genes of interest). Thaysen said calling 95% of a genome fails "if you can't measure BRCA." Dhingra said the Constellation map "paves the path for our most medically relevant genome."

**Spatial roadmap.** Barnard said FFPE compatibility (the standard preserved clinical tissue format) is in research, likely releasing in 2026, "probably second half." Adding protein to single cell and spatial is "absolutely" planned, as is a methylation-based "five base" genome.

**Informatics.**
- Barnard said Illumina Connected Multiomics (ICM), built on the Partek acquisition, will cut three weeks of single-cell analysis to hours.
- He named three differentiators: DRAGEN's cost savings, an AI team with "more access to data than anyone," and multiomics layered on top.
- Thaysen said that once customers adopt Illumina pipelines they won't return to "home brew." He said Illumina is one of the few companies able to build foundation models for pharma.

**Is R&D money just shifting between budgets?** An unidentified analyst asked whether entrants like Element and Ultima, plus spatial, were simply moving money around. Thaysen disagreed: academics run five to ten spatial samples because hundreds are unaffordable, and "pharma is going to be all over this." Barnard cited history: Illumina won roughly 70% of the HapMap project with end-to-end arrays, and later the Genomics England 100,000 Genomes project.

## Bernstein Strategic Decisions Conference (May 28, 2025)

### Format and Thaysen's 20 months as CEO

This was a fireside chat with Eve Burstein of Bernstein. Burstein noted Thaysen's background: CEO since 2023, previously president of two Agilent groups, and R&D vice president at Dako, with over 16 years at those two firms.

Thaysen said 20 months "sometimes… feels like only maybe 20 days." He named four things he was proudest of:

- **The team**, "absolutely the most innovative" he has worked with.
- **Operational excellence**, which he defined as running a resilient company and fixing processes rather than adding people to them, not as cost-cutting.
- **The strategy**, built on elasticity (lower prices create more demand). He still believes in elasticity, but sequencing is no longer the dominant workflow cost for many applications, so Illumina must address the whole workflow.
- **Customer intimacy.** He said successful companies become "inside-out," expecting customers to follow. Illumina now previews roadmaps with customers rather than surprising them.

He also mentioned the GRAIL separation (spinning off the cancer-test company).

### A harder 2025 than planned

Thaysen said 2025 was meant to be an execution year but "the deck was stacked a little bit differently." He listed four headwinds: China's unreliable-entity listing, new competition, NIH funding problems and tariffs.

Rather than complain, Illumina chose to "control what we can control." Management cut about $100 million of cost in 2025, continuing beyond, and shifted some priorities, but kept the strategy "completely" unchanged. His view: if a headwind forces a strategy change, "you have the wrong strategy out the gate," but priorities should adapt to new information.

### The long-range plan and the growth math

Asked whether the plan still held (high single-digit growth and 500 bps margin expansion by 2027, now excluding Greater China, as stated on the Q1 2025 call), Thaysen said: "Exactly, yes." He gave three drivers:

1. **The X transition.** The X cut the sequencing price point by two-thirds, which requires 100% more volume just to stand still. About 65% of volume was on the X but under 50% of revenue, with 50% of revenue expected in the second half of 2025. After that, "growth is going to be easy because the denominator is very different."
2. **New products.** Spatial, Constellation and the 5-base genome had been added since August 2024, worth "probably another 1 point" of growth.
3. **Margin.** The cost cuts were accelerated to offset China.

**Volume and price.** Investors asked whether volume growth would fade once the discount ended. Thaysen said fully transitioned customers keep growing volume, and many customers had raised their own guidance. His long-term model is summarized below; together, the two assumptions imply high single-digit revenue growth.

| Item | Past few years | Long-term model |
|---|---|---|
| Sequencing volume growth | 30%–40% (described as "elevated") | Mid-20s percent |
| Annual price decline | — | Mid-teens percent |

### Academic exposure, China and clinical risk

**Academic exposure.** Burstein noted research is about 50% of revenue (including some pharma). The long-range plan had assumed about 6% annual growth there, and Illumina had cut its 2025 US academic and government guidance by 15%. Thaysen sized the exposure:
- Clinical is over 50% of revenue and research about 45%.
- About half of revenue comes from the Americas, putting research exposure under 25%.
- About half of that is academic, so roughly "10%, 12%" of revenue is truly exposed.

He said uncertainty, not the grants themselves, was the overhang, and he hoped it would clear "maybe over summer." Guidance assumed a market "subdued actually all the way through '25." He said priorities such as autism research need heavy sequencing. New tools could attract shifting grant money by 2026 or 2027, and pharma interest in Perturb-seq should hold up.

**China.** Thaysen said China is "less than 5%" of revenue and that, after the cost cuts, it is "an upside from a P&L perspective." The original plan never counted on China growth, "so without China, it's going to be a little bit easier." But while Illumina was unable to place instruments, "we don't have a future in China" unless that changed. A US–China trade deal was "a little bit out of our hands." He did not say the odds had improved.

**Clinical switching risk.** Burstein noted clinical is about 55% of revenue, Q1 2025 was strong (good X placements, mid-single-digit consumables growth), and the August 2024 plan assumed low-double-digit clinical market growth. Thaysen gave no required market-share figure and said "investors worry too much":
- Clinical labs have built assays, infrastructure and informatics around Illumina.
- "There's nobody in the industry that will match us on the innovations" over three years.
- "Nobody that made a decision going with Illumina has ever regretted it."
- Defectors would be welcomed back "when they realize they made a mistake."

### Products, AI and the insider purchase

**Roadmap sharing.** Thaysen said sharing a longer roadmap at AGBT was about being customer-centric: academics need it to write grants and clinicians to plan assays. Customers now attend full-day campus sessions on unreleased plans.

**Constellation.** Thaysen said Constellation can take "a lot" of the long-read market, starting with human resequencing (reading a genome against a reference) and "probably not de novo" assembly at first.
- *Newborn intensive care example:* a sick newborn normally needs trio sequencing (child plus both parents), but with phasing only the patient is needed, so Thaysen expected Constellation's true turnaround to beat rivals claiming the fastest sequencing.
- On-flow-cell prep alone was not differentiating enough, since labs automate batch runs anyway. It was the added proximity reads that made the product.
- Layering the 5-base genome on top excites him most.

**5-base genome.** This adds methylation (epigenetic marks) to standard reads, replacing a costly two-workflow process.

**Spatial.** Commercial release was set for 2026. Thaysen said spatial had been "way too expensive," so papers used one to three samples over 1–2 mm, covering only part of the transcriptome (all expressed RNA). Illumina's product targets whole-transcriptome, single-cell resolution across a whole slide at a price allowing 40–60 or even hundreds of samples. He did not say what feedback was still being sought.

**AI.** Thaysen said more than 40 Illumina staff work on foundation models using NVIDIA tools. "I don't know any other company… that have the capabilities." He did not address threats from AI.

**Insider purchase.** Thaysen and Dhingra bought shares as soon as their trading window opened; Board changes had delayed it. Thaysen called it "probably one of the best investment decision I've ever made," citing "a lot of upside" and a "moat" no rival matches.

## Wolfe Research Healthcare Conference (November 19, 2025)

### Format and the 2027 targets

This was a fireside chat with Doug Schenkel. Ankur and Conor of Illumina were present but did not speak. Schenkel joked that Thaysen, in a first CEO role since September 2023, "picked a doozy" of policy shocks.

Thaysen's narrative of his tenure:
1. GRAIL separated and is "doing a great job."
2. The strategy was clarified.
3. Execution in 2025 was hit by "a few hiccups."
4. "Knock on wood," Illumina was now "fully in execution."

The opportunity remains "still massive," because sequencing is not yet standard of care.

**Targets.** Thaysen confirmed high single-digit growth in 2027, about 500 bps of margin expansion "up to 26% operating margin," and double-digit earnings growth. One change: "the high single-digit growth is ex China at this point." Schenkel noted China was heading to about 2%–3% of sales by end-2025, so growth should accelerate in any scenario. Thaysen agreed.

**Drivers.**
- The X transition is the main driver.
- Elasticity "does" work, with volume growing "very, very rapidly."
- Multiomics should add about 1–2 points to 2027 growth, up from "probably another 1 point" at Bernstein.
- An academic recovery would be upside but was not assumed.

Schenkel offered his own framework: low single-digit academic growth, the transition plus clinical volume, and share-of-wallet products layered on top. Thaysen endorsed it and made three additions:
- Pharma is "starting to become a real contributor" for the first time.
- It buys at very large scale: "hundreds of thousands" of genomes or "billions of cells" for Perturb-Seq.
- A new BioInsight business would serve multiple pharma partners.

He said Illumina would collaborate even with competing sample-prep vendors.

### Clinical transition detail

On the Q3 2025 call Illumina had shown a case study of 40 clinical customers who fully converted from the NovaSeq 6000 to the X. Schenkel estimated from the chart that growth was under 5% in year one, then accelerating. Thaysen explained the shape: in year one, volume gains and price cuts are "almost in an equilibrium." Customers do not pocket the savings; they compete by offering larger assays within reimbursement (what insurers pay per test). Once the price cut is lapped, volume drives growth.

**Conversion status.** About 50% of high-throughput revenue was on the X. Research had "more or less completely moved," leaving clinical at "less than 50%."

**Four clinical cohorts.** Thaysen and Schenkel described these groups of clinical customers:
1. **Low-volume labs** that stay on the 6000 (a smaller cohort).
2. **Labs that keep old assays on the 6000** while putting new, bigger assays on the X. These grow "out the gate."
3. **Labs moving from exome to whole genome.** An exome covers only the protein-coding regions; whole genome is a much bigger assay. Revenue improves from the start, while the remaining exome business declines. Cohorts 2 and 3 are "absolutely the largest."
4. **Price-driven movers** such as NIPT (non-invasive prenatal testing). These are under 20%, mostly already moved, and "not going to be a big cohort."

Cohorts 2 and 3 use the 25B flow cell (the higher-capacity consumable with the biggest price change). The others likely stay on the 10B flow cell, priced more like high-volume 6000 pricing. The case study excludes brand-new sequencing customers.

### Roche, China, costs and innovation

**Roche.** Thaysen said there had been no change in customer behavior: "I absolutely enjoy competition and especially when we're winning." Schenkel cited Roche's further investment in Freenome, which is evaluating Roche's AXELIOS platform, and cautioned that announcements take a long time to become reality.

Thaysen repeated the Dako/Ventana lesson: pharma preferred non-integrated partners and still prefers Agilent. He saw a "huge opportunity" over 3–5 years in decentralized testing. He also stressed the installed-base advantage: pharma puts its assays where the most instruments are.

**China timeline.** Thaysen gave this sequence:
- *February 2025:* listed on the unreliable entity list after US tariffs, initially without sanctions.
- *About a month later:* an import/export ban on instruments. Illumina could serve existing customers but not install new machines.
- *Around Q3:* permission to supply OEM partners (firms selling Illumina-made instruments under their own brand), including China manufacturing. This was under 20% of China operations but "a good step forward."
- *After the Q3 call:* sanctions lifted, though customers need permits. Clarity was expected "over the next quarter or so," and budget cycles of about six months push real placements "into '26."

Thaysen had met MOFCOM (China's Ministry of Commerce) the prior week. He wants Illumina off the list but said "there's more to it than what we can control." Schenkel judged China growth in 2026 mathematically hard but plausible by 2027. Thaysen said that is "at least our hope," with China general manager Jenny doing her utmost.

**Costs.** Thaysen said cost opportunities were not exhausted: "We haven't even started really on AI yet."

**Innovation.** Schenkel visited Cambridge, UK, and saw no slowdown. Thaysen named 5-base and Constellation as his favorites because clinical growth will come from them. He conceded "everybody have been able to do methylation for years" but said Illumina made it simple end to end. He also cited single cell, spatial, Fluent and the SomaLogic proteomics acquisition. ### Volume, the transition and 2026

**Volume.** Thaysen said volume growth stayed above 30%, mostly clinical. He expected about 25% long term, consistent with Bernstein's mid-20s model. He said "the math is working and it's working according to the expectations," with the price impact lower relative to volume than feared.

**The 40-customer cohort.** Thaysen said the Q3 data showed no "negative bump," only a "flattish" first year. Illumina will not keep reporting cohorts.

**Clinical outlook.** Fewer than 20% of patients receive a relevant NGS test. Rare-disease labs moving from exome to whole genome need about 15x more volume, so clinical volume should keep growing above 30%. Dhingra said whole genome is still "much, much smaller" than exome in clinical, with new tests coming over the next 2–4 years. He did not quantify any headwind.

**2026 framework.** Thaysen said 2026 should look like the second half of 2025: clinical leading, research "muted." Dhingra's new KPIs focus on clinical reimbursements and test launches, research trends, and protein and spatial traction. He said the price effect would be smaller in 2026 than in 2025.

### Strategy, BioInsight and capital

**Installed base and positioning.**
- Illumina has more than 20,000 instruments installed.
- Customer capacity has "doubled" since the X launched in 2023.
- Thaysen claimed some customers saved 30% or more on sample-to-answer cost versus alternatives.
- He said customers want "more than just a box" in "one single open ecosystem," not vendors who "sell them a box and argue for a lower price."
- He expected "a lot of new things" at AGBT 2026.

**Product pipeline.**
- 5-base launched in 2025.
- Constellation was getting "rave reviews in early access."
- Illumina Protein Prep (IPP), built with SomaLogic, measures up to 9,500 proteins per sample.
- A single-cell product had launched handling millions of cells, and spatial was upcoming.
- Academics would adopt first, then clinical customers.

**BioInsight.** This newly launched business combines sequencing at scale, genome-wide CRISPR perturbation (gene editing to switch genes off), compute and AI to build predictive biological models. Its first project is the 5-billion-cell atlas, with Merck, AstraZeneca and Eli Lilly named as engaged partners. Illumina is building a consortium for "billions of cells" experiments.

**Cash and buybacks.** Illumina generates about $1 billion of cash a year, with about 140% free-cash-flow conversion. It returned $740 million through buybacks in 2025, versus $116 million in late 2024.

**China.** Sanctions were lifted after talks that Thaysen described as "very open and good." Illumina remains on the list with "still no clear outcome." "We need to be welcomed into that market."

## TD Cowen Health Care Conference (March 3, 2026)

### AGBT 2026 roadmap

This was a fireside chat with Dan Brennan. Thaysen was now also Interim Chief Commercial Officer. He was relaxed and upbeat, and said the company was growing "very strongly."

His AGBT message was that customers who invest in Illumina will keep benefiting from its innovation. The key items:

- **New NovaSeq X flow cells** for higher and lower capacity needs, a faster-turnaround path, and a 35B flow cell, not yet priced. Customers "really love… that we're not launching an instrument," because their installed machines keep getting better. Research labs short of funding valued this most.
- **Q70 accuracy**, aimed at high-sensitivity MRD, which "nobody can do… today." Things will come out over 18 months, but not all by a date like "June '27." Thaysen said it is "not a pop," because clinical validation takes time.
- **TruPath (formerly Constellation).** It has 10-minute prep and combines short- and long-read information. It launched at $395 per genome, delivering on AGBT 2025's "hundreds of dollars" promise. It runs 16 samples per run, rising to "likely around 96" within 18 months, and any X owner can start immediately. Thaysen expects it to become "the new standard of the genome" in germline testing and blood cancers. On pricing only at launch: "We don't see any value in coming out too early with pricing."
- **Spatial.** It remains in early access, with launch "during the next 6 months," and Illumina was "overwhelmed with the interest." Illumina claimed at least 4x cheaper insight, and Thaysen said "we can do even better." Asked to compare with 10x Genomics panels (Brennan's illustration of about $7,000, or an alternative around $4,000), he declined to give a price. He prefers "cost of insight" metrics: per cell, per sample or per square millimeter.

### Roche, Ultima and Element

Thaysen said Roche had shown "all the promises" and Illumina was now seeing "the consequences."

**His critique of AXELIOS 1:**
- A "very different workflow" with library-prep challenges.
- A single-lane flow cell that complicates mixing applications.
- Difficulty with panels (targeted gene tests).
- A pricing model that "requires you to do high throughput."

**Where Roche is strong.** He conceded strength in counting applications, a segment he put at "probably less than USD 100 million" of Illumina's served space. There, halving sequencing price lets researchers do only "20% more," so it does not drive elasticity.

**No commoditization.** He rejected commoditization as "not true at all" and said he sees no "freezing of the market," which is why Illumina won't preview future instruments.

**Whole genome.** He gave no size for whole genome but said "no other technology… can actually provide the insight that TruPath can." He cited DRAGEN, a tertiary analysis platform with Amgen for rare disease, and an oncology interpretation platform.

**Ultima and Element.** Nothing new: "we fight every day on the streets, which we should."

### 2026 guidance

The conversation had shifted from NIH and tariffs to whether Illumina was being conservative, "a much better conversation to have." The table below shows the 2026 framework as Thaysen described it, against the 2027 goals.

| Item | 2026 guidance | Path to 2027 |
|---|---|---|
| Organic growth excluding China | 2%–4% | At least high single digits |
| Organic growth including China | 1%–3% (guided to midpoint) | — |
| Core operating margin (pre-SomaLogic) | About +150 bps | Another 150 bps reaches 26%; SomaLogic leaves it "within 50 basis points" |
| EPS growth | About 10% pre-SomaLogic, "a little bit lower" with it | "Double-digit to teens" |
| Clinical consumables growth | About 11%–15% (TD Cowen estimate) | Q4 2025 exit rate about 20% |

**Upside and caution.** Thaysen named two upside levers: clinical outperforming and research improving. He cited "another big macro event" the prior week as the reason for prudence. The clinical pricing headwind persists in 2026 but should fade and "shouldn't be worse than '25." He said he can forecast clinical growth only within about one point, so a higher figure is "potentially" possible.

**Clinical drivers.** Thaysen said reimbursement is "starting to more solidify" and panels are expanding from 20 genes to 500 genes to whole genomes. Therapy selection remains the main dollar driver for "at least a year or 2," while MRD and early detection grow fastest in percentage terms.

### Protein, BioInsight and closing

**Protein prep.** This is the largest of the new areas, thanks to SomaLogic's existing revenue. It is reported within library prep revenue for now, with better disclosure under consideration.
- Coverage could expand from 9,500 proteins toward the full proteome of about 20,000, which "no other technologies" can do.
- The workflow is "a little too complicated" today. It suits large automated studies first, and Illumina wants a smaller capital outlay over time.
- Early-cancer data suggest combining genome, methylome and proteome.
- Aptamers (SomaLogic's protein binders) suit future spatial proteomics.
- The Boulder team (former SomaLogic) is excited.

**Spatial versus single cell.** Thaysen would not rank them.

**BioInsight.** Thaysen said biology lacks an "Internet of biology" to train AI. Illumina will generate billions-of-cells datasets using its scale, DRAGEN compute, in-house AI and a genome-wide CRISPR library. It will sell these datasets to pharma by subscription and build models for individual pharma customers, toward a future where customers buy "insights."

**Closing.** Thaysen promised "at least high single-digit growth even in a very competitive space," and praised Illumina's R&D and commercial teams.

## How the message evolved

**Growth.** Management moved from promising a return to growth in 2025 to claiming it delivered, with Q4 2025 up 4%, and then guiding 2026 to just 1%–3%.

**Transition milestones.** The X share of volume went from over 60% (Jan 2025) to 65% (May 2025) to 78–80% (late 2025), meeting the 75%/50% milestones set in January 2025. Volume growth stayed above 30% throughout.

**Definition of the target.** The 2027 target was quietly redefined to exclude China as China fell from about 5% of revenue to about 2%–3%. Margin results beat plan: about 200 bps of expansion in 2025 versus 170 bps guided.

**Constellation/TruPath.** It went from an unpriced technology to "hundreds of dollars" to $395 per genome.

**Roche.** The stance moved from dismissing an unlaunched "technology overview" to detailed critiques of AXELIOS 1. Throughout, management claimed no change in customer behavior.

**Pharma.** It moved from a vision to a named business, BioInsight, with three named partners.
