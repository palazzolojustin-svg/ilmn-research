## VCU Genomics Core co-director (academic customer), 6 January 2026

### Who the expert is

Gregory A. Buck is a professor of microbiology and immunology at Virginia Commonwealth University (VCU) School of Medicine. He co-directs both the VCU Genomics Core and VCU's Center for Microbiome Engineering and Data Analysis. A genomics core is a shared university lab that sells sequencing services to campus researchers. The call was a repeat 40-minute Guidepoint session moderated by Andrew Carmen. Buck is a current customer of Illumina and 10x Genomics, not a former employee, so his evidence is first-hand and current.

He has run the core since it began. It started as a nucleic-acid synthesis core and moved to sequencing in the 1990s. It adopted next-generation sequencing (NGS, massively parallel DNA reading) in 2006-07 with the Roche 454 and early Illumina Genome Analyzers. Over the years it has also had Life Technologies and PacBio equipment and a little Oxford Nanopore (ONT). He labels his projections as tentative and flags where he is speculating.

### Fleet and purchases

The core has owned "pretty much all" Illumina generations. The table below shows its fleet as of the call [Expert: VCU core co-director, Jan 2026].

| Instrument | Status |
|---|---|
| NovaSeq X Plus | Delivered just before the 2025 holidays; production expected "within a month or so" |
| iSeq 100 | Delivered at the same time; replaces the MiSeqs |
| NextSeq 2000 | In production about three years; stays |
| MiSeq (two) | Being mothballed "as we speak" |
| HiSeq 4000 | Long mothballed |

Buck expects no more sequencer purchases in 2026. He may buy automation or more spatial equipment late in the year, and a funding window for expensive instruments opens in summer 2026. The core also owns 10x Visium HD (spatial) and Chromium (single-cell) systems and sequences their output.

The NovaSeq X Plus is meant to relieve the NextSeq 2000. Single-cell and spatial RNA work currently runs there on P4 flow cells (a flow cell is the consumable slide on which sequencing happens). Buck values the X's three flow cell sizes, 1B, 10B and 25B, meaning roughly 1, 10 and 25 billion reads.

His first expected effect is that NextSeq P4 work moves to the 1B flow cell, so "people will get more sequence for less money." He says skeptics who doubted the core had enough volume for a NovaSeq missed that the 1B flow cell makes the instrument usable at modest volumes. The 1B costs more per gigabase (a billion DNA letters, the standard unit of sequencing output) than the 10B or 25B. A filled 10B or 25B beats both the 1B and the NextSeq on cost per gigabase and per read, but only if filled. He guesses "maybe we'll have 25% utilization in 2026."

The iSeq 100 will take the microbial genomes that ran on the MiSeq. It will also serve as a cheap quality check on libraries (prepared DNA samples) before they are committed to expensive 10B or 25B runs.

### Demand and funding

Utilization "continues to increase at maybe 10%." Q3 2025 was flat "for reasons that are not quite clear," and Q4 picked up. Averaged over time, he sees 10-20% annual sequencing growth, which he credits to VCU's funding success. For 2026 he hopes for a "pretty steep increase," tentatively 10-20% or more. If volume does not rise, "we haven't really justified the purchase."

He gives three reasons for expected growth:
- **Repatriated work.** Researchers who outsourced high-throughput work may bring it back; Buck himself outsourced to the Broad Institute and New York Genome Center.
- **New external work.** A new collaboration will sequence for other Virginia state institutions.
- **Lower unit cost.** Cheaper NovaSeq sequencing should encourage more high-throughput work.

He cautions that work moving to his core from other institutions "doesn't necessarily bode that well for Illumina," because it shifts sequencing rather than adding to it. He does expect lower cost to induce more sequencing overall.

About 95% of work is in-house. "Anachronistic" VCU rules force a surcharge on external users, but the core found a workaround in 2026; he expects no big influx. Customers are 80-90% grant-funded, mostly by NIH (the National Institutes of Health), with few NSF grants. VCU has climbed into the top 50 NIH-funded institutions (from about 62nd) and roughly doubled outside funding in a few years. Buck calls this "the one thing that's saving us."

In 2025 one of his multi-year grants was terminated outright and another was cut about 70%. He says this was common, with behavioral genetics and DEI-related work hit hard. Some grants were reinstated after challenges. The shock came early in 2025 and eased over the year.

A CDC center grant spanning five states was cut 75%. His year-four share fell from nearly $1 million to $400,000, and year five is uncertain (possibly cut 50% or 70%). He thinks the CDC was targeted more than other agencies. He now fears further clawbacks less, because researchers are adapting how they write proposals.

### Indirect cost rates

Indirect costs are overhead added on top of grants. They fund compliance offices (human-subjects review, animal care, grants management) and subsidize cores. VCU's rate is 54%, about average. An early-2025 push to cap all rates at 15% would be "devastating," though Buck senses it will not be that dire.

Existing awards seem to keep their original rate, which he thinks is because they are contracts. Rates are renegotiated every five years. Schools that negotiated in 2024 are protected, but VCU renegotiates in 2026; he flags this as speculation from outside "the circle." Roughly 20% of the core's operating budget comes through the research vice-president's office, funded partly by indirects, so a cut would likely hit the core.

### Pricing, tariffs and outsourcing

Illumina applied a roughly 5% tariff surcharge on the instruments the core bought, then gave an offsetting discount, so the core saw no tariff impact. Buck understands Illumina generally covered about 5% of its tariff cost this way. He cited no other cost pressures.

Asked about cheap outsourcers such as Plasmidsaurus, Buck argued that in-house cores add design advice. The Broad "thought they knew more than we did" and returned data about 75% duplicative because it misjudged library amounts. Without upfront design help, users can waste $15,000-20,000 on unhelpful data. Most of his users consult the core first, and its quality controls keep failures rare.

### Single cell and spatial

Single cell means sequencing cells one at a time. About 75-80% of the core's single-cell work runs on 10x Chromium X, much of it legacy projects five or more years old, where protocol inertia blocks switching even for savings. 10x usage is up only "a few percent" year on year. Buck rejects blog claims that single cell is declining but says it is "probably not going up as fast as it used to."

Some users have moved to Parse, which uses combinatorial barcoding (tagging cells with combinations of labels instead of droplets) and is labor-intensive. Two or three have adopted PIP-Seq, Fluent's technology now sold by Illumina, and found it cheap and low-labor. He does not see Parse growing. He does expect PIP-Seq, which "does seem to work" and is being pushed by Illumina's sales force, to "eat into the Chromium business." He has "kind of ignored" Scale, which 10x acquired, lumping it with Parse as labor-heavy. Single-cell ATAC work (mapping open chromatin) is small.

Spatial transcriptomics measures gene activity by location in tissue. Buck is "really disappointed" in Visium HD uptake. Interest was high, but only "a couple people" have used it in over a year, and he feels 10x has "pretty much abandoned" it to push Xenium, its imaging-based product. VCU's NanoString GeoMx units (including two the core bought three or four years ago) have produced perhaps five or six readouts ever, and he hears little interest in CosMx.

VCU may buy a Xenium soon via the summer funding window, though possibly for another core. He gives several reasons spatial is slow:
- A single experiment costs several thousand dollars and typically needs about 20 samples.
- Samples are hard to acquire and handle.
- Planning is complex; one colleague has prepared for at least six months.
- Sequencing and analysis add cost and require a bioinformatician.

His own Visium HD project added about 100 custom bacterial probes to the human set. It worked, though 10x only advised on custom probes and did not support them. Budgets are not shifting from single cell to spatial at VCU; the two are complementary.

### New Illumina products and applications

**Proteomics.** Nobody has requested Illumina's protein assay, which is based on SomaLogic aptamers (short molecules that bind proteins). Dentistry colleagues are considering it, and others are looking at Olink. Buck says mass-spectrometry proteomics is slow and expensive. He cites about $400,000-500,000 for 100 samples on SomaLogic.

**5-base methylation.** Illumina's 5-base kit reads DNA methylation (chemical tags that regulate genes) alongside the genome. It is unused at VCU so far, but he expects interest on cost and throughput.

**Other applications.** Epigenetics is at most 5% of volume. ChIP-seq (10-20 runs ever) and ATAC-seq (under 10) are one-offs that may migrate to Illumina protocols. Small RNA work is a growing interest. Microbiome work is 20-25% of volume. The core is not CLIA-certified (the US lab standard required to return results to patients), and VCU's diagnostics lab does cancer panels, not microbiome.

### Competition

Buck finds Roche's Axelios, which uses sequencing by expansion ("Xpandomers"), very interesting. He thinks Roche is "still a bit overstating their accuracy" because it compares a strand with its fold-back copy rather than with a known reference. Only a few early-access units exist, and new sequencers typically take about a year to become turnkey, so it is too early for his core.

A PacBio Sequel "was nothing but problems" and went unused because reference genomes made short reads sufficient: "the users didn't come through for us." He fears obsolescence: the NovaSeq X is two and a half to three years old, and he hopes Roche or Illumina itself does not leapfrog it soon. He owns an ONT MinION in his own lab (used once or twice a year), as do four or five VCU investigators.

He remains "always bullish on sequencing." He expects clinical use, including routine diagnostic genomes, to carry the market as cost per gigabase falls.

## Former Illumina Senior Manager, Global Market Development (Tegus), ~15 January 2026

### Who the expert is

The expert spent about seven years at Illumina as sales specialist, oncology specialist, market developer and global market developer. Earlier they worked in regional marketing at 10x and in technical sales at Thermo Fisher. They hold a PhD from Nanyang Technological University, with postdoctoral training at the Genome Institute of Singapore and Harvard Medical School. They now lead precision medicine internationally for Astellas, a drug company, and twice flagged possible pharma-side bias.

The call is undated. Its references to Axelios marketing and the SomaLogic deal suggest late 2025 or early 2026. Their view of Illumina's internal plans is dated or secondhand. The two Illumina statistics discussed (20% growth, 65% clinical share) came from the interviewer, not the expert.

### Clinical adoption is the bottleneck

The expert called clinical sequencing, meaning testing used to diagnose and treat patients, the "holy grail" where the big revenue lies. They said its adoption sticks in three places:
- **Reimbursement**: whether insurers and health systems pay for the test.
- **Regulation**: how tests get approved.
- **Prescribing behavior**: how oncologists order tests and how readily pathologists adopt them.

The interviewer said Illumina's clinical consumables (the reagent kits bought for every run) grew about 20% excluding China for two quarters. The expert agreed and tied it to big US labs: Natera, Tempus, Caris and Foundation Medicine. They guessed, explicitly as inference, that the driver is MRD testing over the prior 6-12 months. MRD (minimal residual disease) testing uses blood draws to look for traces of cancer left after treatment.

They expect the big accounts to keep growing on MRD. Internally, though, how to "democratize" clinical sequencing to many smaller labs was "always the tough question to answer."

On Illumina's claim that clinical is over 65% of consumables, the expert called it "a very loose statement": "Illumina has been very generous and very broad in terms of classifying clinical use." The only FDA-approved comprehensive genomic profiling assay (CGP, testing hundreds of cancer genes at once) they cite is TruSight Oncology (TSO) Comprehensive, the approved version of TSO 500, plus legacy assets they doubt anyone runs. Most clinical volume is lab-developed tests (LDTs, tests labs design and validate themselves) at the big labs.

### Reimbursement, community oncology and turnaround

TSO Comprehensive is now reimbursed. The expert sees that as access expansion rather than a share grab from Foundation's send-out model, in which hospitals ship samples to a central lab. They expect "market expansion as opposed to a cannibalization kind of scenario" for now, because CGP coverage of patients is still low.

Whether community oncology practices can adopt NGS at scale is "the billion-dollar question," and the expert expects it to be difficult. Community doctors are only now getting used to CGP reports, as academic centers did 5-10 years ago. Running a test is getting easier, but the expertise to use the data is missing. A hub-and-spoke regional model, where local sites send samples to a regional center, fits best today. Turnaround is driven mostly by batching: a small lab waits until it has enough samples to fill a run.

### Roche

**Speed.** On Axelios's same-day genome, library prep (converting DNA into sequencer-ready form) plus sequencing takes about 24-36 hours on Illumina. Halving that saves only about 12 hours across a workflow that includes biopsy, processing, informatics and reporting, so it matters little in oncology. Speed is more relevant for pediatric rare disease in neonatal intensive care, and the expert has seen little Axelios oncology data.

**Sales force.** Roche's existing diagnostics sales force is "a big advantage." The expert remains skeptical of the product, perhaps from Illumina bias. Converting existing NGS labs is one prize. The bigger one, labs that have never done NGS, requires a "push-button system" pathologists trust.

**Companion diagnostics.** A companion diagnostic (CDx) is a test that determines whether a patient gets a specific drug. The expert sees compliance risk in tying tests to drugs. After Roche bought Foundation, some markets saw patients who bought FoundationOne end up on Roche drugs. Other drugmakers then grew wary of using FoundationOne for their CDx and LDT programs, and the expert expects the same reaction if Roche links its drugs to its sequencer.

**Applications on existing machines.** Roche's single-cell partnership with 10x follows Illumina's own playbook of adding applications to existing instruments rather than launching new boxes every few years. That old cycle "leaves a bad taste in people's mouths." Illumina is adding single cell, spatial (which it is doing "very well," per the expert), 5-base and methylation.

### Sub-$100 genomes, Thermo and 10x

Element's and Ultima's sub-$100 genomes are "a very nice, sexy number" with limited oncology relevance. Germline genomes (inherited DNA) are read at about 30X, meaning each position is read about 30 times. Tumors need "easily more than 120" and sometimes 200X, a point marketing rarely mentions. Prep, biopsy, FFPE blocks (formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue, the standard preserved form) and informatics add further cost. "Even if sequencing is free," regulation and reimbursement take time, so the impact on somatic oncology (tumor-acquired mutations) is "very limited."

The expert has worked at both Thermo and Illumina. Thermo's Ion Torrent wins on a simple DNA-to-report workflow, small panels and slightly faster turnaround, especially in start-up labs. Illumina wins on technology strength and capacity, from small panels up to whole genomes. Labs that scale tend to move to Illumina and then to CGP.

10x leads single cell and spatial as incumbent. The expert recalled bake-offs where Illumina led on spatial and 10x on single cell. Illumina led on transcripts per cell (depth) and 10x on cells per sample (breadth). Because Illumina's installed base can try its products cheaply, the expert thinks Illumina "can close it really, really quickly."

TSO versus FoundationOne CDx depends on the audience. Foundation's test is centralized, so the in-house comparator is Roche's AVENIO kit. Savvy pathologists value TSO's 500 genes over FoundationOne's "300 over" (hedged recollection). Oncologists, however, trust FoundationOne's brand, CDx claims and published evidence.

### MRD skepticism

The expert has "always been a bit skeptical" of global MRD adoption and flagged this as personal bias. Their reasons:
- MRD works today on US interest and US reimbursement; ex-US reimbursement will be very hard.
- Even Foundation has struggled to gain a foothold abroad, and outside the US only Japan has shown traction.
- Building MRD as an in-house LDT is "quite difficult" compared with CGP.
- Clinical demand needs drugs with MRD on the label. De-escalation trials, which cut treatment when MRD is undetectable, "did not pan out," showing little survival difference.

Illumina's tumor-agnostic MRD kit, which does not require prior tumor sequencing, uses a lot of sequencing. That would help revenue if adopted, but the expert struggles to see adoption given cost and unclear benefit.

### Instruments, DRAGEN, SomaLogic and identity

**NovaSeq X.** The expert, hedging, said Illumina puts new applications on the X first, with benefits trickling to NextSeq and older boxes. Heavy users get the lowest per-base cost. Customers see the X as "the pinnacle" with no "next new iPhone" looming. They gave no growth forecast.

**Smaller boxes.** The high-throughput segment is "sexy" and is where Ultima (and Element, less so) attack. Small boxes such as the fast new MiSeq and the NextSeq serve microbiology and quick-turnaround work against Thermo. The expert thinks Illumina has never "really worked that hard" to grow this segment.

**DRAGEN.** Sequencing software has three layers. Primary analysis turns signals into base calls, a solved problem for 10+ years. Secondary analysis aligns reads and calls variants, which is what DRAGEN does. Tertiary analysis turns data into a clinical report (SOPHiA is one player). Because Illumina data dominates, DRAGEN has "taken out the guesswork" and become the standard. Illumina under-markets it because it is "not a very sexy thing to talk about."

**SomaLogic.** Adding SomaLogic is "absolutely the right strategy." Thermo's purchase of Olink "sets up a new battleground." A lab with a NovaSeq X can add SomaLogic more easily than Olink. Clinical protein-by-NGS is distant, however: it is costly and needs a robot for prep.

**Identity.** The expert's biggest point is that Illumina "has not decided whether it wants to be a technology company or it wants to be a clinical company." A clinical push would need many more IVD (regulator-approved diagnostic kit) and CDx assays, plus R&D, commercial and business-development teams, pharma engagement, and a record of taking assays from LDT through approval.

## VP International Business Development, Veritas Intercontinental (European NIPT lab), 28 January 2026

### Who the expert is

The expert is VP of International Business Development and Sales Director for Europe, the Middle East and Africa (EMEA) at Veritas Intercontinental, reporting to the CEO. Earlier they were Product Manager for Genetic Services at BioRep (left March 2021) and worked at an NIPT-only lab. NIPT (non-invasive prenatal testing) is a maternal blood test that screens the fetus for chromosomal conditions.

The interview took place on 28 January 2026 and was published 28 April 2026, with a buy-side interviewer. The expert is a current Illumina customer: Veritas sequences on Illumina instruments and buys Illumina reagents. Some country details are recalled with a stated "if I remember correctly."

Their bio says they know Synlab, Cerba, Geneplanet, Sonic, Health in Code and Eurofins' Italian operations, holding a moderate view of Eurofins' quality. Cerba uses Illumina, and Synlab has advanced technology. Geneplanet uses mixed methods across Sweden, Serbia and Croatia, which makes standardization hard, though it relies on Illumina.

Veritas runs about 55,000 NIPT tests a year from over 65 countries through a central Barcelona lab, with "nine-figure" revenue. NIPT is about 35% of revenue (the interviewer's figure, confirmed by the expert).

### Growth: a capped market

NIPT "exploded officially in the beginning of 2018 before COVID" but has since slowed. Recent growth comes mainly from public systems offering it under defined criteria. The American College of Medical Genetics says it should be offered to all pregnant women aged 20-45, and scientifically "NIPT is for everyone," but national finances prevent universal coverage. Test quality is uniformly high, so labs compete on:
- price;
- certification (the European equivalent of US CLIA/CAP standards);
- "clean and lean" workflow from enrollment to results; and
- personalization.

Veritas does not ask sales for 20% NIPT growth because "the market is really capped." The cap reflects too many labs and players, not internalization. Even so, Veritas grew NIPT organically at a double-digit rate in 2025, which the expert called "incredible." Acquisitions added only 6,000-7,000 samples. The Middle East grew well, while Turkey is crowded. European penetration (share of pregnancies tested) will not "grow like crazy" in the next three to four years because doctors are the gatekeepers and about 90% of gynecologists already offer NIPT.

Uptake follows maternal age:
- **Nordics.** Uptake is low because first-time mothers are well under 30. Norway's penetration is about 10%, Veritas holds about 70% of the market, and the expert does not expect a price cut alone to lift penetration to 25%. Some gynecologists do not suggest NIPT to women aged 20 or 25.
- **Italy.** Uptake exceeds 50% because first-time mothers average 33-35. Volume is about 120,000-130,000 tests a year, only about 10,000 public and over 90% out of pocket. The expert does not expect 200,000.

Out-of-pocket growth looks more plausible in the Nordics than in southern Europe, where out-of-pocket share is already high.

### Illumina and competing platforms

Illumina launched VeriSeq NIPT v1 in 2017 and the genome-wide v2 in 2019. Since then, the expert says, "the main company" has made no big update while rivals build new systems. Illumina's 2021 24-sample batch suits small labs, but per-sample cost "almost double[s]" versus 48 samples.

The competing options:
- **Element with Medicover** runs about 284 samples per run (the expert was unsure of the number); few European labs have that volume, though large batches cut cost sharply.
- **Thermo** is "really flexible," running eight to 16 samples.
- **BGI** is slightly cheaper than Illumina at below €100, which "doesn't change so much."

Big labs want throughput while small labs want flexibility.

The expert says large vendors, Illumina included, "don't care about the partners." They will sell the same CE-IVD (European-approved diagnostic) workflow to labs 100 meters apart because "their main activity is to sell machine and to sell reagent." Fragmentation suits Illumina because small labs pay high reagent prices while big ones negotiate. The expert called this "a little bit strong to say."

Veritas runs NIPT on seven NextSeq 550s (first stated as eight) with five Hamilton liquid-handling robots, in 96-sample preps split into two 48-sample runs. It runs genomes and exomes on five NovaSeq X Plus systems, with one or two Hamiltons shared. One X Plus equals about 30 NextSeq 550s and runs about 140 genomes (70+70), versus two or three on a NextSeq at "crazy" cost. Big machines can cut production cost up to 70% if volume allows.

A genome produces about 90-100 Gb of data versus about 1 Gb for 48 NIPTs. Cloud (Amazon) and IT costs can exceed sequencing itself and are "the main challenge," one that public hospitals underestimate. NIPT interpretation is "super easy," and AI already helps with genomes and exomes. Targeted-panel alternatives named were SOPHiA, Arrogenetic and Illumina TruSeq. SeqCode is a competitor, and the interviewer named BillionToOne as a kit provider.

### Economics and pricing

The expert estimates break-even at runs twice weekly: about 96 samples a week, 400-500 a month, and at least 5,000 a year. A 2,000-test lab could use 24-sample plates but would pay more than outsourcing. Labs internalizing below that scale make "a huge mistake," given high fixed IT, infrastructure and maintenance costs.

Cost per test is mainly reagents. It is "in the hundreds" of euros as a lab, though around €100 at scale (the interviewer's summary, which the expert accepted). Collection tubes cost under €10, and logistics (DHL medical express) is one of Veritas's largest costs. A 55,000-test lab and a 5,000-test lab use the same sequencers; only scale differs. The expert expects no further 30-40% sequencing cost drop soon, and no CE-IVD instrument could cut cost 50%. Genomes and exomes have more room to fall. Invitae's bet on sub-$100 NIPT, which assumed costs would fall fast enough to become profitable, "failed completely."

Patient prices for a basic test fell only modestly, from about €600 in 2016 to about €500 now. Lab-to-lab (B2B) prices for basic tests fell 40-50%, from €350-400 to €220-250, over five to six years: "NIPT became really a commodity." Premium tests hold at €700-900, up to €1,000, with higher B2B prices too, and the expert expects them to stay flat.

Premium tests cost only a couple of euros more to produce on Illumina than basic tests, so everyone in the chain wants premium. At Veritas, premium means copy-number variants above 7 Mb plus microdeletions under 3-4 Mb. Others add monogenic carrier tests such as spinal muscular atrophy and cystic fibrosis. The expert's regional mix estimates follow; public systems lean basic, while out-of-pocket buyers lean premium.

| Region | Standard | Premium | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nordics | 80% | 20% | Full premium panels not allowed |
| Spain, Italy | 20% | 80% | Out-of-pocket patients want full panels |
| Latin America | 30% | 70% | |
| Middle East | 70% | 30% | |
| Romania, Poland | 50% | 50% | Want large panels; price too high |

Italy's public test (€200 to the patient) covers only the three trisomies, without even fetal sex. The Netherlands and Belgium offered genome-wide panels from day one. The expert agreed with the interviewer's summary that growth comes from premium mix, with flat volume and flat premium prices.

### Public sector, internalization and M&A

Vendors push labs to internalize, which makes sense once reimbursement starts. NIPT-only labs are no longer competitive.

**Belgium** pays three accredited labs (one-third of samples each) about €260 per test, plus €80 paid by the patient to the government. The expert calls this "bad," though €250-300 against €100 cost is "a good business." Entering Belgium is "impossible."

**Italy.** A draft plan promised an eight-figure budget for over 75,000 patients at €280 per test to hospitals but has been unapproved for about three years for lack of money. The expert called Italian public health "dramatic." Their own example: €10 million at €280 buys about 35,000 tests.

**Spain** is "probably now… the new Germany" industrially but unlikely to add public NIPT while private works. In the **Netherlands**, two universities run testing (the TRIDENT study, Rotterdam and another), with high penetration.

**Lombardy.** A free-NIPT trial in three Milan hospitals (5,000-10,000 births each) cost Veritas "at least 30%" of its regional market that year. Revenue recovered when funding ended and the offer narrowed to at-risk patients.

The white-label resellers common in 2016-17 have largely vanished from rich countries. Copying a lab is technically easy, because "they pay millions to Illumina and Illumina is building the lab." The barrier is funding.

Big labs buy subscale ones because it is cheaper than winning 5,000 extra tests. Deals run about 10x EBITDA (a profit measure) in theory, but often targets have no EBITDA, so buyers assume debt plus a little extra. Integration means redirecting tests to Barcelona and cutting duplicate cost and management, taking six months to a year. Preferred targets are where Veritas is strong, such as Italy. Greece and cross-border contracts are harder, and the interviewer noted odd practices in Serbia.

### Preventive genomics and conclusion

Veritas has three businesses. Perinatal is slightly under half of revenue (NIPT about 35%, other prenatal about 10%). Diagnostics serves symptomatic patients via hospital contracts and is mostly reimbursed. Preventive testing of healthy people, sold through insurers and checkup clinics with no direct-to-consumer channel, is the biggest growth trend and is 100% privately paid. Clínica Universidad de Navarra has been a customer for nearly 10 years.

Veritas runs exomes and genomes on Illumina end to end, with in-house medical sign-off, and also sells raw sequencing to hospitals. NIPT and genomics share only extraction machines and some cross-trained senior technicians. Cross-selling is "not so strong" because the physicians differ. Gynecologist-facing products include exome carrier screening and myNewborn, a newborn panel of over 450 conditions booked under prenatal.

The expert's bottom line: "If I have €10 million in my pocket, I will not invest in a NIPT lab at the moment." NIPT "is not a moving business."

## UIUC DNA Services director (academic customer), 2 February 2026

### Who the expert is

Alvaro Hernandez has directed DNA Services at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign since 2001, about 25 years. This was a repeat Guidepoint call moderated by Andrew Carmen. His core serves UIUC, other universities, companies, federal agencies such as the USDA, and foreign clients. The base is "very agriculturally oriented," though human projects are rising sharply.

He runs an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (most of his volume), a MiSeq i100, a PacBio Revio and an ONT GridION. He sequences output from a neighboring core's 10x Xenium. He alpha- and beta-tests products for Illumina, so his evidence is direct and current.

### Demand and economics

Billing in the last four months of 2025 was about 15% below other years, "mostly in Illumina." He attributes this to grant cancellations and researchers hoarding money. PacBio was spared by one very large project. The lab has been "in the red in the past three months," a first in 25 years. He expects no layoffs because the university usually covers gaps, easing a fear he raised on the prior call.

His 2026 outlook is "not very positive." Many grants remain cancelled, though some large ones were funded. Funding is shifting toward human rather than animal research, and price competition is intense. He could not quantify the change. Before the current administration, capacity lagged demand; now supply and demand are "very close," with Ultima adding competition.

He had been "ready to pull the trigger" on a second NovaSeq. He self-funds instruments and depreciates them over two years, and his is two years old. He has now held off because demand cannot fill a second unit and profits are "much lower."

New entrants charge "unrealistic prices," and researchers who see campus mass mailings demand them. His core used to sell only full lanes (a lane is one channel of a flow cell). It now offers half-lanes to users with 15-20 samples, "reducing our margins by sharing lanes."

### Funding

Hernandez says NIH's roughly $40 billion budget now pays five-year grants fully up front, which meant "like 3,000 less grants" were funded. The interviewer called it "an accounting trick," and he agreed. He expects researchers to adapt by switching mouse models to human cells and dropping DEI and gain-of-function language.

UIUC lost $67 million in grants campus-wide. A $23 million ARPA grant mostly benefits PacBio and partly Illumina, and one or two more such grants would put his lab "in excellent shape." His main single-cell customer, an influenza researcher working on a universal vaccine, lost a grant "for no reason," which Hernandez calls "100% political."

New PhD admissions fell to about 80% of normal. He fears for postdocs needing H-1B visas, since universities would not pay $100,000 per visa; he says an exemption is being negotiated. He expects "very serious" damage over two to three years, and says losing postdocs would be "irreparable."

### Illumina

Whole-genome methylation, where a few samples fill a flow cell, is the X Plus's main revenue driver. The 5-base kit reads methylation and variants from one library and run, which he calls "something amazing" that "never existed." He has run a first project at about 60x and will reanalyze at 60x, 40x and 30x; "the jury is still out." Methylation alone needs 30x, and Illumina recommends 40-50x for variants too, so the kit implies more sequencing per sample, not less. He is running seminars with Illumina to sell methylation to variant-focused users.

New kits can distinguish 5mC from 5hmC (two forms of methylation), but that takes two libraries and doubles cost, so uptake is slow. P&L :: Profit and loss, the income and costs of a business unit.

Depreciation :: Spreading an instrument's cost over its useful life as an operating expense.

OMB :: The White House Office of Management and Budget, which releases appropriated funds to agencies.

ARPA grant :: A large federal grant from an advanced-research agency.
H-1B visa :: US work visa used by many foreign postdoctoral researchers.

Gain-of-function research :: Research that enhances pathogen traits, discouraged in new proposals.
