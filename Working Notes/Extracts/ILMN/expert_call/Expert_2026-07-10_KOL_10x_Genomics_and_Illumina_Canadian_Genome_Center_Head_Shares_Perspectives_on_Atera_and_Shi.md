# Expert call with McGill Genome Centre head on Illumina, Ultima, Roche AXELIOS, and 10x Genomics Xenium and Atera

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-10_KOL_10x_Genomics_and_Illumina_Canadian_Genome_Center_Head_Shares_Perspectives_on_Atera_and_Shi.md` · Date: 2026-07-10 · Guidepoint expert network; moderator Andrew Carmen; expert Ioannis Ragoussis, Head of Genome Sciences at the McGill Genome Centre and Professor of Human Genetics, McGill University · expert call*

**Summary.** A Canadian genome centre head, who is a customer and evaluator of every major platform, says Illumina sequencers are fully booked and demand is rising because of national Canadian projects, so he is applying for another NovaSeq X Plus. He reports that Ultima sequencing costs about half of Illumina's but is held back by bespoke software and funding inertia, that Roche's AXELIOS is the one instrument he sees as able to replace Illumina, and that 10x Genomics' new Atera will coexist with Xenium rather than kill it. He does not expect spatial or single-cell shifts to reduce overall sequencing demand.

# Expert call: McGill Genome Centre head on sequencing, single-cell and spatial platforms [Expert: Head of Genome Sciences, McGill Genome Centre, Jul 10, 2026]

## Background and relationship to the companies

- The expert is Ioannis Ragoussis, Head of Genome Sciences at the McGill Genome Centre and Professor of Human Genetics (also listed in the department of human health and the department of biology) at McGill University. The call was arranged by Guidepoint and moderated by Andrew Carmen. It is labelled "Academic KOL - Physician Scientist" (KOL means key opinion leader). He had spoken with the moderator before ("once again").
- He has worked in genomics for over 35 years. He set up facilities and workflows for genomic analysis by arrays, sequencing and other high-throughput methods at King's College London, then at Oxford University and the Wellcome Trust Centre for Human Genetics, where he was head of genomics and genomic research.
- He has been at McGill for nearly 14 years and oversees all of the centre's genomics platforms, from arrays to sequencing, single-cell analysis and spatial biology (measuring gene activity while keeping track of where in a tissue it occurs).
- His relationship to the companies is that of a current customer and evaluator. His views on the centre's own usage are direct experience. His views on future products (Roche AXELIOS, Atera's eventual performance, Ultima's trajectory) are expectations or speculation, and I flag them as such below.
- The call was a Guidepoint network call. The primary companies listed are ILMN and TXG (10x Genomics). Associated companies listed are PACB, Element Biosciences, Ultima Genomics, BRKR (Bruker), ROG:SW (Roche) and TMO (Thermo Fisher).
- The advisor was asked to avoid commentary on stock prices, valuation, investment recommendations, confidential (NDA) material and material nonpublic information.

## The centre's instrument fleet

- **Illumina:** instruments "of all types." The workhorses are two HiSeq X instruments running full time. He later says the centre runs X Pluses (NovaSeq X Plus) and will add another.
- **Ultima:** short-read sequencing platform, implemented more recently. The centre has run it for about a year, and early in 2026 became one of the official international service providers for the technology. He says he is "very excited about this platform and its future."
- **Discontinued short-read platforms:** he evaluated PacBio's short-read sequencing-by-binding, which is now discontinued. He also said the centre previously ran MGI instruments, which he described as "a political issue."
- **Long-read:** both PacBio Revio and Oxford Nanopore. Nanopore ranges from small MinION devices to mid- and high-throughput PromethION 24/48 with upgrades.
- **Single-cell and spatial:** Visium, single-cell on many technologies including 10x Genomics, Xenium (10x's higher-sensitivity in situ spatial platform), and NanoString/Bruker GeoMx and CosMx. Olink proteomics (protein measurement read out by sequencing) is also run.
- **On order:** the centre has just ordered 10x's new Atera platform, based on preliminary experience, and expects delivery once 10x can deliver an instrument.

## Illumina: utilization and demand

- Direct experience: the Illumina platform is "in full use." He said this was also true six months earlier. It "is the go-to platform for all types of sequencing applications," and the centre's Illumina capacity is "constantly overbooked."
- He said, "we are doing more sequencing than ever, let's put it that way with Illumina."
- Demand is expected to rise further because of two national programmes now starting to ramp:
  - The Canadian Personal Health Initiative, which aims to sequence about 100,000 genomes with short reads. Samples are only now becoming available and sequencing is starting. All of it is short-read on Illumina.
  - The next phase of the Marathon of Hope cancer genomics project, covering about 20,000 tumours (the number is "not set in stone yet"). It includes whole genome sequencing of tumour and normal material plus RNA sequencing, largely on Illumina.
- Capacity plan: he is applying for one more NovaSeq X Plus, hoping to receive it in about a year, to be added to the fleet.
- Funding lock-in: in the Canadian Personal Health Initiative, the workflow was negotiated with sequencing providers who co-fund by contributing to reagent costs. "Illumina is the main partner in this," so the centre cannot change platforms until the programme ends. He said "there is a lot of inertia basically in the way things are funded in Canada."
- Outlook (his expectation): for at least the next two to three years, "Illumina will remain the predominant force." He added that the market is growing overall, that some of his earlier predictions have materialized, and that more players need not come at the detriment of others.
- Illumina's Constellation (a newer Illumina sequencing-related offering mentioned by the moderator): they tested it, but "it doesn't look as if it's going to be a game changer."
- Illumina's new spatial transcriptomics: he called it "not suitable yet" for the clinical (formalin-fixed) samples he cares about.

## Ultima Genomics

### Cost and applications

- Ultima is "much more cost effective than Illumina." He puts cost at "about half," depending on how it is calculated. Cost is the main differentiator and the reason the centre bought it.
- It suits applications needing very large numbers of short reads at low cost. Examples: Olink proteomics (combined "very successfully" with Ultima, giving low cost and very good readouts), and single-cell sequencing, where Ultima is "making inroads."
- The centre has started its first big whole genome sequencing projects, encouraged by the new Solaris 2.0 chemistry.
- Solaris 2.0 improved sequencing performance, data quality and genome coverage. It also reduced the sequencing cost and raised instrument throughput.
- Two run options now exist: 10 billion or 20 billion reads per wafer. He compares this to Illumina's 10 billion and 20/25 billion configurations, and says Ultima's 10 billion is "much cheaper than Illumina's 10 billion."
- He says Ultima is in a second phase of development with further cost reductions: "Ultima is going in the right direction."

### Barriers to adoption

- Bespoke pipelines: the data output is quite different, so centres must build, validate and automate custom bioinformatics pipelines for each application (RNA-seq, ChIP-seq, methylation and so on). The centre is going step by step and is currently at whole genome sequencing. There is only one other Ultima centre in Canada.
- Immature ecosystem: the Ultima environment "is not as well developed yet as the Illumina," so it cannot simply replace an Illumina instrument. The centre runs it in parallel with Illumina.
- Scientific conservatism: researchers are reluctant to switch platforms mid-project, and many remain sceptical until they see quality and cost effectiveness.
- Funding inertia, as above.
- Timeline (his estimate): it will take "at least another year" for Ultima to come close to Illumina in popularity at his centre. He expects it to sit alongside Illumina for projects that need cost-effective sequencing.

### Price competition view

- If Illumina can keep pace with Ultima on price, "then it can beat it because it's the established technology versus the newcomer." If Ultima's cost advantage does not materialize, "there is no reason for having this platform."
- He said at the moment "Illumina is struggling to keep pace, but you never know. They may decide to dampen the prices dramatically." Of Illumina he said: "we'll keep them under pressure."
- He argued that competition is useful. Without it, "Illumina will not be motivated... to bring the prices down as we have seen." The moderator suggested the second platform is "a hammer for negotiations," and he agreed it works.

### UG100 to UG200 upgrade

- The centre upgraded its UG100 to a UG200 without swapping the instrument. It needed a new liquid handling system (the centre adapted its own, which Ultima is "very interested in" because it could reduce instrument cost) and a high-performance computing (HPC) upgrade. The compute server is separate from the instrument, unlike Illumina where the computer is integrated, and his is housed in another building.
- Upgrade necessity: he thinks many have upgraded but it is "not strictly necessary." The expensive upgrade matters only if a lab needs to double its already high throughput. Without it, the system can decode one 20-billion wafer instead of two 10-billion wafers. The moderator likened this to "single versus dual," and he agreed.
- He said many users were worried when version 2.0 was introduced, but "it's not a disaster": ways exist to adapt first-generation instruments to the improved chemistry without very expensive upgrades.

## Roche AXELIOS

- Status: not yet commercially available. He has met Roche "several times" and plans to get an instrument once available, possibly later in the year; they are in discussions.
- His view (opinion): it is "the instrument that I can see as having the potential to replace the Illumina technology completely." It "is designed to compete head on with the Illumina platform." He added "we have to see how Roche is going to develop this platform further."
- Reads: somewhat longer than Illumina's, but he does not think this is decisive, since reads must be of similar length for similar quality. There is flexibility to produce longer, lower-quality reads that are useful for some experiments. He said he visited Genia (Roche's nanopore group) in Santa Clara a few months earlier.
- Main advantage: speed. It could deliver data in a few hours, and he compared this with Nanopore results in an hour or even 20 minutes. He described it as combining some features of nanopore technology with Illumina-style sequencing by synthesis. He said "that's where it can offer something that Illumina is not able to offer yet."
- The moderator will follow up once he has tested it. This is expectation, not hands-on experience.

## 10x Genomics: single-cell

- Utilization is "very high." There are increasing numbers of industrial partners beyond academia, as early single-cell approaches have been translated into industry workflows. It "has become a standard technology."
- Growth: year-over-year increase of "10%-20%," described as "not dramatic."
- Outlook: for at least another year he sees increasing, not decreasing, demand, though "these things may change." (A transcript line mentions "diminished utilization... so far" but the context shows he means no decline so far.)

## 10x Genomics: spatial (Visium, Xenium, CosMx)

- Visium: the first 10x spatial systems were "very complex to use" and "very often... would not work"; not robust across tissue types. The centre has largely moved off Visium: "very, very little need."
- Xenium: available at the centre for over two years, robust and reliable, "probably the most robust platform for spatial transcriptomic type experiments" when compared with others. Usage has increased "dramatically" and the platform is at capacity. A collaborator has set up a tissue microarray factory that will feed Xenium. He personally uses Xenium for his own projects because of its success rate.
- Bruker/NanoString CosMx: also run at the centre (GeoMx too). Because of Xenium's success, CosMx is now focused heavily on proteomic-type assays. There is internal competition and the choice is left to investigators.
- No additional Xenium planned: he does not plan to buy another Xenium or replace it with Atera immediately.

## 10x Genomics Atera

### Reaction and positioning

- He was "very upset with 10x when we saw the Atera instrument," because he hoped Atera's capabilities would be built on Xenium rather than a completely different instrument. His strategy is to use Xenium as much as possible until Atera arrives, then gradually move many projects to Atera.
- Pricing: Atera is "much more expensive than Xenium... about double as much." Cost per sample will largely determine utilization.
- 10x pricing signal: originally 10x did not differentiate Xenium and Atera costs much, but "very recently" it has been reconsidering costs. If Xenium becomes more cost-effective for some experiments, Xenium may continue. He thinks 10x is trying not to "kill immediately" Xenium because customers "revolted"; no one wants an instrument obsolete after two years.
- Advantages of Atera: much higher throughput and more markers (whole transcriptome, about 25,000 transcripts, versus about 5,000 genes on Xenium currently, which cover more or less all known markers). The extra markers may not always be needed.
- Where Xenium still wins: projects needing speed (one or two tissue sections quickly), and applications needing only cell-type identification and neighbourhood or cell-interaction analysis. Xenium still has very high data quality and resolution, and "it's not clear whether it will be immediately surpassed by the Atera."
- Labs that benefit: Atera is "the instrument of the future for labs that have actually high levels of utilization." His group formed a collective to use it, comparing it to buying an Illumina X instrument: "if you don't have the usage, it's a complete waste of money."
- Timeline: he expects to know "in a year's time" whether Atera replaces Xenium. The moderator will follow up later in the year after delivery.

### Effect on other platforms

- NanoString and others: he thinks it "will shift some business," because 10x's methodology, optimized over years of single-cell work, is well optimized for clinical formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded (FFPE) samples. He expects usage to grow in translational and clinical-research niches.
- Single-cell substitution: for projects where only formalin-fixed material exists, one Atera experiment could "completely eliminate" the need for extra single-cell analysis. 10x technology used for FFPE single-cell is very similar to what was transferred to Atera.
- Limits: Atera is probe-based, so it interrogates only a small fraction of expressed transcripts. It will not work well for detecting isoforms or novel fusion transcripts (for example in tumours) from fresh cells.
- Estimate (speculative): about 20% of experiments that use single-cell technologies could be satisfied with a single Atera experiment, "at this point."
- He expects Atera and Xenium to coexist for a time, with specific application needs deciding. He also mentioned that 10x's analysis software or branding is now called "StrataMap" (as "they call it now," in his words).

## Does spatial or single-cell shift hurt Illumina sequencing?

- His answer: it does not look that way. The clinical FFPE tissues involved are not suitable for standard sequencing and "wouldn't have been done very much anyway," so Atera and Xenium are more complementary and run in parallel.
- Even if some RNA sequencing is displaced by Xenium and Atera, he sees "a huge expansion in sequencing needs" from:
  - Population-level whole genome sequencing, being adopted by governments in many countries including developing ones.
  - Liquid biopsy (testing blood for cancer-related DNA), where cell-free DNA applications such as minimal residual disease (MRD, tiny amounts of cancer left after treatment) are "basically exponentially increasing."
  - Sequencing-based proteomics such as Olink, which is finding increased usage.
- Conclusion: "I don't see that it's going to be influencing the usage of sequencing instruments in general."

## Long-read platforms (PacBio and Nanopore)

- PacBio Revio is "one of our most desired instruments," used at full capacity and with steady year-over-year growth. It is used for the Canadian BioGenome Project (entering its second phase, part of the Earth BioGenome Project), where PacBio is "the best instrument" for high-quality genomes across species, and for clinical and translational work. It is the centre's best long-read technology.
- Another Revio: undecided. The centre also runs Nanopore, and the two are about on par on cost. He expects Nanopore to deliver higher throughput and lower cost "very soon" (expectation). Nanopore remains unique for very long reads.
- Nanopore RNA: growth in direct RNA sequencing, because of a new RNA therapeutics production facility at McGill. Nanopore is becoming the main quality-control analytical tool there, and there is a Gates Foundation grant to translate this to production facilities in Africa and other countries.
- He said usage is up on all of the centre's platforms, even those that compete: "none of those does exactly the same thing as the other."

## Illumina Protein Prep versus Olink

- The centre uses both. Illumina's is "comparable, but not directly comparable," because the detection technology differs. Some projects prefer one, some the other. Running both gives the centre flexibility, and "the one is not there at the detriment of the other."

## Macro, pricing and funding

- Inflation "is real," but so far companies have tried to contain price increases for reagents and instruments. The Canadian dollar lost ground against the US dollar, which hurts purchasing power.
- Grant funding mechanisms have not changed yet, and research funding amounts have not been affected by wider economic problems. Next year's government budget is the key uncertainty. He noted rising use of these technologies in the private sector, which could offset possible government funding losses; "it's not clear." Effects take longer in science than in markets.

## Concluding view

- He said there is "more choice than ever" in technology. Illumina will remain "one of the workhorses," but other specialized workhorses will run alongside: Ultima, long-read platforms, Atera and other spatial platforms. He argued that more competitors drive faster technology development, and that technologies will be distributed to what they do best.
- The centre's overall strategy: keep multiple platforms to drive competition and improvement, introduce Atera while continuing to use existing technologies for specific applications, and expect some applications to move from single-cell to spatial as Atera matures.
