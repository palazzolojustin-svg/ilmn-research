# Canadian genome center assistant director (customer) on sequencing output, Illumina dominance, and the Roche AXELIOS threat, Q3 2026 channel check

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-02_Assistant_Director_at_Genomics_Research_Center_Optimistic_About_Sequencing_Growth_and_Emer.md` · Date: 2026-10-02 · Channel check expert call (AI interviewer) with an Assistant Director at a Genomics Research Center in Canada; interviewed 2026-10-02, published 2026-10-06 · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer at a large, research-use-only Canadian academic genome center says its sequencing output grew more than 10% (under 20%) in Q3 2026 versus Q3 2025, with about 15% growth expected for 2026 and at least 10% for 2027, driven almost entirely by large federally funded projects. All of its short-read work runs on Illumina (NovaSeq X Plus), and it sees Roche's AXELIOS (a new Roche short-read sequencer) as a significant potential threat if its claims hold, while remaining skeptical of its reusable-reagent claim. Illumina's NovaSeq X roadmap and TruPath Genome are described as welcome but not revolutionary for this lab, and pricing, discounts, lead times and support were stable in Q3 2026.

## Expert background and how to weigh this call

- The expert is an Assistant Director at a large academic genomics research center in Canada. Their perspective is that of a **customer** (not a former Illumina employee or competitor). They have visibility into high-throughput sequencing and into writing infrastructure grants (the grant applications that pay for instruments). [Expert: Canadian genome center assistant director, Oct 2026]
- The interview was conducted by an AI interviewer on 2 Oct 2026 and published 6 Oct 2026. It is part of a "Channel Check" series on advanced sequencing that tracks lab-level insight on sequencing output, adoption, consumables, competition, new products and support.
- Most answers are direct experience of the expert's own lab in Q3 2026. Statements about other labs, vendors' internals or the future (for example, why Oxford Nanopore demand is low, long-read cost declines, Roche's performance) are speculation or hearsay, and the expert flags this in places. On consumable price trends the expert said, "I may not be the best person in our organization to ask about that."

## The lab: size, customers and funding

- **Output:** roughly 10,000 human genomes per year at 30X coverage (30X means each DNA base is read about 30 times on average). That is 30,000 x 3 Gb, or about **90,000 gigabases (Gb) per year**. A gigabase is one billion DNA bases, the standard unit of sequencing output.
- **Setting:** a large academic genome center acting as a core facility (a shared service lab) for its own institution and for external clients. It serves **over 800 different labs per year**, each belonging to an individual scientist or researcher.
- **Client mix:** about 85% academic (universities and teaching hospitals); the remaining 15% is split evenly between government/NGO labs and the private sector.
- **Regulatory status:** research use only (RUO). It is not a CLIA-certified clinical diagnostic lab and does no clinical diagnostics work.
- **Funding of sequencing work:** about **90% from federal funding agencies**, about 5% from the private sector (including biopharma), and the remaining ~5% from other sources such as philanthropy.
- **Q3 2026 funding trends:** grant funding available to the researchers it serves "remained the same" versus Q2 2026. Pharma/biotech-funded work was "as expected."

## Drivers, headwinds and tailwinds for output

- The main driver is incoming large-scale projects. Federal or other funders may fund very large genome sequencing projects, and "those are the things that really drive our utilization."
- The lab is "fairly immune to volume changes due to pricing fluctuations" from vendors. Its capacity is such that it generally does not have to turn away projects.
- Main downside risks named by the expert: (1) a primary instrument vendor suddenly upgrading or changing its instruments, or (2) a disruptive technology appearing that would force the lab to consider replacing all its instruments.

## Fleet and short-read versus long-read mix

- **Short-read instruments (read short DNA fragments, cheaper per genome):** two Illumina NovaSeq X Plus, one Illumina NovaSeq 6000, and one Illumina MiSeq (a small, low-throughput instrument used for very small targeted experiments, "does not get used very much").
- **Long-read instruments (read much longer DNA stretches):** one Oxford Nanopore PromethION 24 (described as "quite old," still working, not the vendor's highest throughput) and two PacBio Revio systems.
- **Mix:** about **90% of output is short-read**, driven by cost per experiment; whole-genome short-read sequencing is much cheaper than long-read.
- **Expected shift:** over the next 12 months the expert expects Oxford Nanopore and PacBio to take steps to cut cost per sample, shifting roughly **10%** of work from short-read to long-read ("not enormous"). A larger shift would follow if long-read vendors sustain cost reductions longer term.
- **Science versus cost:** "I think it is purely cost." As a scientist the expert believes long-read "is a better experiment to do and gives you better data and more useful data," but "most things are going to be driven by cost."
- **Throughput tiers:** the lab is a production-scale, high-throughput genome center, with NovaSeq X Plus as its primary instrument and nothing in the middle. Illumina NextSeq or Element AVITI instruments "really do not fulfill a middle throughput role for us."

## Vendor share and competition

### Short-read share

- **100% of the lab's short-read work is on Illumina.** It owns no Element, Roche or Ultima instruments.
- No vendor share changes occurred in Q3 2026 in either short-read or long-read.
- Over the next 12 months the expert does not foresee great shifts in short-read, "although, of course, we are looking at the Roche system as a potential alternate or option as compared with our Illumina systems."

### Long-read share

- Long-read output is roughly **80% PacBio and 20% Oxford Nanopore**, which partly reflects owning two Revios versus one PromethION 24. The expert also cites "major uptake on the PacBio instruments by our power users" and says there has not been as much client demand for Oxford Nanopore: "I do not know why" (speculation).
- Expected change: the expert anticipates **more uptake of Oxford Nanopore** over the next 12 months, mainly for whole-genome sequencing. Nanopore produces extremely long reads useful for de novo assembly (building genomes that have no prior reference sequence), which the expert calls "an advantage over the PacBio system."

### Roche AXELIOS (new Roche sequencer)

- **Status:** the lab has not adopted it. It is a possible purchase within 12 months but the expert "cannot commit."
- **Instrument price:** not a concern whether it is $800,000, $1 million, $1.2 million or $1.5 million, because the lab buys instruments from federal infrastructure grants, so "those are sunk costs for us."
- **Reusable reagents:** the expert is "very unconvinced" the model will work. They understand Roche is saying reagents/cartridges can be reused 20 times and say they cannot think of any current or previous platform with that reusability.
- **Proof required:** the expert would want Roche to place an instrument in their lab to run it themselves. "I am deeply mistrustful of data provided by vendors because you never know how many runs they have done or how they have manipulated the data." They would also not necessarily be convinced by other genome centers' data because of unknown vendor relationships.
- **Threat to Illumina:** "If it works as well as Roche has been telling people that it works, then I consider it to be a significant threat to Illumina." Illumina has a very large installed base and well-established chemistry that is "easy to understand and generates good data," but equivalent data quality at a much lower price point would be "a major disruptive threat to Illumina."

### Other vendors

- **Element Biosciences:** "not really a player" for this lab because its instruments sit in the middle throughput area, roughly like an Illumina NextSeq; they may be very good but "just not really correct for us."
- **Ultima:** "a very interesting company," claiming very high-throughput instruments with very low cost per sample. Colleagues in a different city have one and are trialing it; the lab has none yet, is watching results and is looking at the technology.
- **Events:** the expert plans to attend events at the American Society of Human Genetics (ASHG) meeting in Montreal in a couple of weeks (about mid-Oct 2026), expecting to hear more about the Roche platform.

## Illumina products and roadmap

- **NovaSeq X roadmap** (Illumina has committed to raising NovaSeq X flow cell output by up to 40% and speeding whole-genome sequencing by about 30%, as put by the interviewer): this does not change willingness to use NovaSeq X because the lab is already heavily invested. The expert says a 40% cost reduction is "welcome, but not exactly revolutionary" and a 30% cut in time to results is likewise "welcome but is not exactly revolutionary."
- **TruPath Genome** (launched Feb 2026 per the interviewer; removes library preparation, the step of preparing DNA for the sequencer, from the whole-genome workflow; list price about $395 per sample): the lab is "basically not using TruPath at all." For long-read-like applications it already has PacBio and Nanopore, and for routine whole-genome sequencing it sees nothing worth "the decreases in throughput and increases in cost that it incurs."
- **TruPath for other labs:** the expert thinks it is compelling for facilities that run Illumina and no long-read sequencers. It adds "quasi-long-read de novo assembly" capability that is "incrementally better" than standard Illumina short-read sequencing, a "somewhat compelling use case" for existing Illumina users. For a lab with PacBio and Nanopore already, the value is much weaker (interviewer's summary, expert did not contradict).
- **SomaLogic** (Illumina closed the acquisition in January 2026 per the interviewer): the expert is not familiar with SomaLogic or its products, so it has had no impact on the lab.

## Instrument purchases and capital spending

- **Likely within 12 months:**
  1. A third Illumina NovaSeq X Plus ("very likely"), for functional redundancy when instruments are down for maintenance and for extra throughput.
  2. Probably another Oxford Nanopore instrument, since the single PromethION 24 is old.
  3. A Roche AXELIOS as a possibility "always lurking in the background," contingent on being convinced it works as advertised.
- No instrument placement plans were accelerated, delayed or cancelled in Q3 2026.
- 2026 capital spending on sequencers is expected to be **flat versus 2025**, supported by several overlapping federally funded infrastructure grants; the lab is "relatively stable at the moment."
- Vendor instrument discounts, incentives or placement terms in Q3 2026: the expert is not aware of changes and suspects they were the same as Q2 2026.

## Consumables, pricing and supply

- **Spending:** consumables and reagents spending in Q3 2026 rose about **15%** versus Q3 2025, roughly in line with the growth of the large projects. Reagents and consumables from vendors are the biggest cost in a sequencing experiment, so a 15% rise in throughput means about a 15% rise in reagent spend.
- **Volume versus price:** the expert agreed the increase was almost entirely volume-driven, with pricing relatively stable.
- **Pricing power on the buyer side:** the lab is part of a national network of genome centers with considerable buying power and thus heavy discounting. Swings of plus or minus 10% or 20% in volume year-over-year or quarter-over-quarter "really have no impact on the per experiment reagent cost from the vendors. It is already heavily discounted."
- **Price trend 2026 vs 2025:** as far as the expert knows, reagent and consumable prices have not changed much (flagged as outside their core knowledge).
- **Discounts and reagent commitment terms, Q3 2026 vs Q2 2026:** no change.
- **Inventory:** "sufficient." The lab manages inventory well, not purchasing only on demand but not warehousing excess.
- **Lead times:** unchanged in Q3 2026; no concerning fluctuations in reagent acquisition or delivery.

## Vendor support and service

- **Illumina:** "very satisfied." On-site technical support is "excellent," on-site application support is "excellent," and informatics support (software and data-analysis help) has been "very good."
- **PacBio:** no concerns known to the expert.
- **Oxford Nanopore:** the one outlier. "The one complaint a lot of people have about that vendor is that the informatics support is perhaps not as good as it could be"; analysis tools are not as well developed and need attention.
- Support levels from all vendors were unchanged in Q3 2026 versus Q2 2026.

## Key takeaways and caveats

- Facts from direct experience: the lab's fleet, 100% Illumina short-read, ~90% short-read mix, Q3 2026 output growth of 10-20%, ~15% consumables growth, flat capex, unchanged pricing/lead times/support.
- Expert forecasts and opinions: ~15% output growth in 2026, at least 10% in 2027, a ~10% shift to long-read, Nanopore gaining share, and AXELIOS being a significant threat only if its claims hold.
- This is a single, unusually grant-funded, heavily discounted, high-throughput RUO customer, so its insensitivity to price and instrument cost may not represent smaller or clinical labs.
