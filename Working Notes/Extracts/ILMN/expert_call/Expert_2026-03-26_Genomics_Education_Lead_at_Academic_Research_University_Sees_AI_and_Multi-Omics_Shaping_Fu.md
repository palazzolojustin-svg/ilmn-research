# Channel check with a genomics education lead at a Maryland academic university on Illumina demand, long-read shift, AI and multi-omics (interview 2026-03-26)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-03-26_Genomics_Education_Lead_at_Academic_Research_University_Sees_AI_and_Multi-Omics_Shaping_Fu.md` · Date: 2026-03-26 · Channel check expert-call platform (AI Interviewer); expert is a genomics education lead at an academic research university in Maryland (unnamed); published 2026-03-31 · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer-side academic user (NovaSeq, PacBio, Nanopore) says their Q1 2026 Illumina sequencing volume and consumables spend fell versus Q1 2025 only because the planned work was finished in 2025 after funding cuts forced them to spend faster, not because of price or loyalty problems. They plan to stay with Illumina (service and benchmarked kits), drop Nanopore for PacBio, expect no instrument purchases, and see long-read plus short-read hybrid sequencing, AI analysis and multi-omics (Illumina's SomaLogic deal) as the key trends. They are non-committal on Roche's AXELIOS (SBX) and have heard it may be less accurate than Illumina.

## Sequencing volume and project pipeline

**Q1 2026 drivers.** In Q1 2026 the lab did no sequencing at all by design, because it had planned and carried out a lot of its sequencing in 2025, both Illumina and PacBio. Volume is "largely decided by the project need." There was a hold-up in PacBio sequencing [Expert, Mar 2026].

**Funding is the main external factor; staffing is not.** When funding was cut short (in 2025), the lab urged a sequencing facility to sequence faster, essentially trying to spend all of its sequencing-dedicated money in a shorter time frame. All Illumina samples were sequenced on time. Some PacBio work was still not done even though samples were prepared, because:
- PacBio sample prep is more involved and takes longer.
- They have no PacBio instrument on site, so samples must be sent off, whereas their core facilities have a diverse array of Illumina instruments, making Illumina easier.

**Illumina volume trend.** Q1 2026 Illumina output is decreasing versus Q1 2025. The expert's reason: "what we planned to do with our project with Illumina sequencing has already been done." Everything needed with Illumina was sequenced by design. They are considering sequencing deeper with Illumina at the end of 2026, since they multiplexed (pooled) many samples earlier and want more depth for improved de novo assembly of microbes (building genomes from scratch) and better quantification.

**Versus expectations.** Q1 2026 output is "probably around our expectations," because Illumina sequencing is done and they are still sequencing the remaining PacBio samples. They knew more PacBio work was coming in 2026; those samples are being prepared now.

**Full-year 2026 forecast.** They plan "much less" Illumina sequencing than in 2025 because most was done before. They plan more PacBio sequencing because it was not finished in 2025; Illumina had been used initially to benchmark before proceeding to PacBio. They are also in conversations about more Illumina sequencing for deeper metagenomic data, but this is unplanned and still in the planning phase.

**Change in outlook over the last 90 days.** More optimistic, for two reasons:
1. They hope the funding situation stabilises so they will not be cut short as in 2025, when they had to catch up using funds that should have been spread over more years.
2. They are optimistic about adding AI-based analysis products, for example Illumina tools for analysing mutations, possibly with the next round of (deeper metagenomic) Illumina sequencing.

## Consumables and pricing

- **Consumables spend (reagents and kits needed for each run).** Q1 2026 spending on Illumina consumables is decreasing versus Q1 2025, purely because the planned Illumina sequencing is already done. The expert said it was "no, just sequencing volume": not pricing, stocking or other factors.
- **Kit pricing.** The expert does not negotiate. They take whatever the core facility offers and presume it is fair because the core negotiates diligently and has competitive pricing. They assume (an assumption, not knowledge) that the core's many Illumina instruments earn a good discount. Kit prices do not affect near-term decisions; their impression is "the kit prices are already okay" and they will not need to seek cheaper options.

## Instruments, competition and platform shifts

**Competitive pricing for instruments.** The expert does not engage in negotiations. They are aware that other, cheaper instruments and cheaper sequencing are coming. They also know Element is "trying to disrupt the price for sample prep." But changing instruments and strategy is expensive, and they plan to stick with Illumina. A slight (even more competitive) price change would not make them switch, because "we rely on service and we rely on benchmarked kits, which Illumina can provide and other companies typically are not there yet."

**Shift toward long-read (expert's market observation).**
- In metagenomics and rare disease, people are pivoting somewhat toward long-read sequencing. Their own lab wanted PacBio and Nanopore to assemble de novo genomes. In rare disease, long-read is used to resolve difficult regions and haplotypes (which variants sit together on the same chromosome copy). Although Illumina has solutions for some of these problems, long-read is "common knowledge" the default for them.
- More and more labs run hybrid (short plus long read) sequencing, because Illumina offers accuracy and quantification that long-read and other platforms "might still struggle with."
- The shift is driven mostly by technical capability, not cost. PacBio is "clearly still more expensive than Illumina," but people choose it for the data they need. People are including more long-read in whole-genome analysis.
- The expert suspects clinical labs differ: they lack workflows for long-read platforms and probably still use short-read and targeted sequencing (speculation). In research areas such as metagenomics and genome assembly, a bulk of sequencing now uses long-read instruments, independent of cost.

**Their own platform changes.** They have dropped Nanopore; they now run only Illumina and PacBio (previously Illumina, PacBio and Nanopore). Over the next 12 months they expect to "lose Nanopore" because PacBio meets their needs for accuracy and throughput, and they suspect the market will see less Nanopore and more PacBio. They do not think this takes much from Illumina, since the platforms are used for different questions.

**Equipment purchases.** No plans to buy any new instruments in the next 12 months; no change in plans over the past 90 days (none pulled forward or pushed back); to their knowledge the core facilities have also made no new purchases.

## Products, technology and strategy

**Illumina AI products (expert's top excitement).** The expert is most excited by AI-based data analysis and believes there will be a need for AI products and new algorithms to interpret data. They said "Illumina is ahead of the game," citing SpliceAI (which Illumina already had in 2019), PromoterAI and later PrimateAI (tools to interpret genetic variants), and noting "Some companies are not doing that yet." They want AI to help scientists interpret variants and home in on specifics.

**Wish list.** Genome assemblers and help extracting meaningful information from metagenomes, specifically plasmid assemblers and viral assemblers.

**Competitor products of interest.** Proteomics (study of proteins), though they do not do it themselves. They see multi-omics (combining DNA, RNA and protein data) as a big future trend and think other companies might have interesting proteomics approaches for future multi-omics projects.

**Roche AXELIOS (SBX).** The analyst described it as expected to launch next year. The expert said it is "a little bit difficult to say" what the competitive effect will be because the product is not yet for sale. They find SBX (sequencing by expansion, which they described as based on Nanopore technology and aimed at the basecalling problem) "interesting and very promising." They are following it, given Roche's large diagnostics presence, and "if the chemistry is going to work well, then possibly people are going to pay attention." They do not know how it will perform on accuracy, but "I heard predictions that it still will come with lower accuracy than Illumina." This is secondhand opinion.

**SomaLogic acquisition.** Asked about Illumina's planned acquisition of proteomics company SomaLogic, the expert called it "a great acquisition" that enables multi-omics expansion and "improves the value proposition." They later added that it signals Illumina's interest in proteomics, "although traditionally it's not what Illumina is about."

**Illumina support.** The expert does not know directly of any changes. They infer satisfaction because their facility typically acquires the latest Illumina instrument.

## Outlook

- **Multi-omics.** The expert expects people to want DNA, RNA and protein data together rather than in three separate experiments.
- **AI and informatics.** More AI products will be needed because more data is being generated than academic labs have expertise to analyse. They see the next two to five years as heavy on software, informatics and omics.
- **Partnerships and acquisitions.** Partnerships drive demand somewhat, especially in rare disease, where PacBio partners with rare disease initiatives, and they reveal what companies care about and are planning. Acquisitions matter too, as the SomaLogic deal shows.
