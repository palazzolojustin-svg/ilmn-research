# Expert call with VP of International Business Development at Veritas Intercontinental (NIPT lab operator, Europe/MEA): limited NIPT growth, commoditization, internalization risk, shift to preventive genomics

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-01-28_VP_at_Veritas_Intercontinental_Sees_Limited_Growth_in_NIPT_Market_Shifts_Focus_to_Preventi.md` · Date: 2026-01-28 · Third-party expert network transcript (interview 28 Jan 2026, published 28 Apr 2026); expert is VP International Business Development and Sales Director EMEA at Veritas Intercontinental; interviewer is a buy-side investor (client) · expert call*

**Summary.** A senior executive at a large European genetic testing lab (a consultant-perspective expert, current operator) explains that the market for non-invasive prenatal testing (NIPT, a blood test screening a fetus for chromosomal conditions) has matured into a commodity with capped volume growth. Basic-test prices to labs have fallen 40-50% in five to six years while premium tests hold their price, so growth comes from mix shift toward premium tests, acquisitions of struggling small labs and new areas such as preventive genomics. Illumina is the dominant supplier of the sequencers and reagents (the chemical kits used for each run) used by Veritas, and the expert views Illumina as indifferent to customer fragmentation and as having made no major NIPT product update since 2019.

## Expert background and how to weigh the evidence

- The expert is Vice President of International Business Development and Sales Director for Europe, the Middle East and Africa (EMEA) at Veritas Intercontinental, reporting to the CEO. They lead global expansion, strategic partnerships and the EMEA sales team [Expert bio, interview 28 Jan 2026].
- Before Veritas, they were Product Manager of Genetic Services at BioRep, leaving in March 2021. They previously worked in an "NIPT-only" lab [Expert bio; transcript].
- The expert is classified as a "Consultant" perspective and the interviewer as an investor-led (buy-side) analyst. The call was held 28 Jan 2026 and published 28 Apr 2026.
- Relationship to Illumina: the expert is a current customer-side operator. Veritas sequences on Illumina machines and buys reagents from Illumina. The expert is not an Illumina employee, and the market views are first-hand from running a large lab, though some country details (Belgian and Dutch arrangements) are recalled from memory and flagged by the expert as uncertain ("if I remember correctly").
- The bio says the expert knows Synlab, Cerba Healthcare and Geneplanet well and also knows Sonic Healthcare and Health in Code. It also says they know Eurofins' Italian operations and its monogenic disorder testing and hold a moderate view of its overall quality. Cerba uses Illumina-based systems. Synlab has advanced technology. Geneplanet uses varied methods across Sweden, Serbia and Croatia, which makes standardization hard, although it uses the reliable Illumina system.
- Veritas scale (direct experience): about 55,000 NIPT tests a year, samples from more than 65 countries, revenues of "nine figures," and a central lab in Barcelona (the interviewer's description, which the expert did not dispute). The expert says NIPT is roughly 35% of revenue (the interviewer's figure, which the expert confirmed).

## Market growth and drivers

- The expert says NIPT is no longer growing as fast as it did five to six years ago. The market "exploded officially in the beginning of 2018 before COVID." The biggest recent growth comes from public health systems starting to offer NIPT under specific criteria.
- The expert expects NIPT eventually to be offered to all pregnant women as pricing improves [Expert bio]. They cite the American College of Medical Genetics position that NIPT should be offered to every pregnant woman, from age 20 to 45. Scientifically, "NIPT is for everyone... no doubt on that." How the market changes depends on each country, and national finances currently prevent it.
- Differentiation is now price, certification (the European equivalent of US CLIA/CAP lab standards), workflow efficiency ("clean and lean" from enrollment to results), and personalization and customization. Test quality is consistently high across the industry [Expert bio].
- Veritas's growth over the next five years is not expected to come from NIPT. NIPT is "important, probably will be an organic growth." Management does not ask sales to grow NIPT revenue 20% a year because "the market is really capped." The cause is the market itself, which has many labs and players, not internalization or Veritas's ability.
- Last year (2025) Veritas grew NIPT at a double-digit rate, which the expert called "incredible." The expert clarified that this was organic growth. Acquisitions added no more than 6,000 to 7,000 samples in 2025.
- In Middle East markets NIPT is less recognized than in Europe, and Veritas grew very well there in 2025. Turkey is already crowded and was not a growth area.
- Penetration (the share of pregnancies tested) is not expected to "grow like crazy" in Europe over the next three to four years because doctors are the gatekeepers and the same doctors are already prescribing. The expert says about 90% of gynecologists are willing to offer NIPT.

## Demographics and country-specific volumes

- Uptake depends on health systems and maternal age. It is lower in the Nordics because mothers are younger (first children well before age 30). It exceeds 50% in Italy, where the average age of a first-time mother is above 33 (33-35 in the bio).
- Italy: about 120,000-130,000 NIPT samples a year, of which only about 10,000 come from the public system. More than 90% is out of pocket. The expert does not expect Italy to move from 130,000 to 200,000 tests a year.
- Norway: Veritas holds about 70% of the market (expert's estimate, direct experience). Norwegian penetration is about 10%, and the expert does not expect it to jump to 25% from a price cut alone. Some gynecologists do not suggest NIPT to women aged 20 or 25.
- The expert believes out-of-pocket growth is more plausible in the Nordics than in southern Europe, where out-of-pocket share is already high.

## Technology, Illumina and competing platforms

- Illumina released the VeriSeq NIPT solution version 1 in 2017 and version 2, with a genome-wide approach, in 2019. The expert says no big update has come from Illumina, "the main company," since 2019, while other producers are creating new systems.
- Illumina released a smaller 24-sample batch in 2021 (versus 48 or 96), which suits small labs but is "much more expensive." Per-sample cost "almost double" at 24 versus 48 samples.
- Element Biosciences, with Medicover, released a system running about 284 samples per run (the expert is unsure of the number). The expert says few European labs have the volume for a batch that large, though larger batches cut cost sharply.
- Thermo Fisher technologies are "really flexible," able to run 16 samples or fewer, eight at a time, versus Illumina's scalability.
- The market is therefore splitting. Big labs want high throughput and efficiency. Small labs want flexibility.
- BGI's cost is "a little bit less than Illumina, below €100," which "doesn't change so much."
- On the call's Illumina strategy theme, the expert says large vendors, including Illumina, "don't care about the partners." Illumina can sell the same CE-IVD (European-approved diagnostic) flow to neighbors 100 m apart because "their main activity is to sell machine and to sell reagent." Illumina is better off when the market is fragmented, because small labs pay high reagent prices while a big lab can negotiate better ones. The expert called this "a little bit strong to say."
- Veritas's equipment (direct experience): NIPT runs on NextSeq 550 (low-output Illumina sequencers). It has seven sequencers and five Hamilton liquid-handling robots dedicated to NIPT (earlier in the same answer the expert said eight sequencers linked to five library-prep stations, then corrected to seven). Library prep runs 96 samples, and sequencing splits into two runs of 48. For genomes and exomes it has five NovaSeq X Plus machines. One or two Hamiltons are shared across both uses.
- Scale of machines: one NovaSeq X Plus has output equal to roughly 30 NextSeq 550s. A single NovaSeq X Plus run can do about 140 genomes (70 + 70). A NextSeq 550 could run only two or three genomes at a "crazy" cost. Using big machines can cut production cost by up to 70%, but needs volume.
- Data: a genome produces about 90-100 Gb per sample, versus about 1 Gb for 48 NIPT samples. Cloud storage (Amazon) and IT infrastructure can cost more than sequencing itself. The IT burden is "the main challenge," and public hospitals underestimate it.
- NIPT interpretation is "super easy." AI will help considerably with genome and exome interpretation and already does.
- Alternatives named for targeted panels: SOPHiA GENETICS, Arrogenetic, the Illumina TruSeq system. SeqCode is called a competitor. BillionToOne is named by the interviewer as a kit provider.

## Economics: scale, COGS and cost trends

- Break-even scale (expert opinion): a lab must run at least twice weekly, meaning 96 samples a week for four weeks, about 400-500 tests a month, and at least 5,000 NIPT a year. Below that, outsourcing is probably better. A lab doing about 2,000 a year could use the 24-sample plate, but costs would be higher than sending samples out to a big lab.
- Many hospitals and private labs internalize with too little volume, which the expert calls "a huge mistake." Fixed costs (IT, infrastructure, NGS maintenance, which is "quite high") are large.
- COGS (cost of goods sold, i.e. direct cost per test): net cost is mainly reagents. Little technician labor is needed. Production cost at a high level is "in the hundreds" of euros as a lab, though the expert elsewhere implies about €100 at scale, and the interviewer summarized "around €100." The special collection tube costs under €10. Logistics is among the biggest costs for Veritas, which pays for DHL medical express first class.
- Size difference: Veritas (55,000 tests, five systems) versus a small lab (5,000 tests, one system) use the same sequencers. The difference is scale only.
- Cost outlook: sequencing costs have fallen, but the expert does not expect another 30-40% drop soon. No CE-IVD instrument today could cut cost 50%. Genomes and exomes are different, with more room to fall.
- Invitae (still active, US) sold NIPT at below $100 excluding logistics, betting sequencing costs would fall fast enough to reach profitability. The expert says this bet "failed completely."

## Pricing and mix

- Patient (B2C) price: about €600 for a basic NIPT in 2016, about €500 now. This is a modest decline.
- Lab-to-lab (B2B) price for basic tests fell from €350-400 to €220-250 in five to six years, a 40-50% drop. "NIPT became really a commodity."
- Premium tests (patient price) remain about €700-900 or up to €1,000. The B2B price remains higher too. The market does not demand reductions, and the expert expects premium prices to stay flat.
- Production cost for basic versus genome-wide on Illumina differs by only a couple of euros, so premium carries much more margin for physician, lab and producer. "All the actors involved... want to earn much more money."
- Veritas's definition of premium: copy number variants (gains or losses of DNA over 7 Mb) and microdeletion syndromes (smaller losses or gains, under 3-4 Mb). Other firms add monogenic tests (checking DNA sequence for carrier status, e.g., spinal muscular atrophy, cystic fibrosis).
- Premium share by region (expert's estimates):

| Region | Standard | Premium | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| Nordics | 80% | 20% | Complete premium panels not allowed |
| Southern Europe (Spain, Italy) | 20% | 80% | Out-of-pocket patients want complete panel |
| Latin America | 30% | 70% | |
| Middle East | 70% | 30% | More standard-oriented |
| Eastern Europe (Romania, Poland) | 50% | 50% | Low-income countries want large panels but price is too high |

- Public systems favor basic tests, with growth in out-of-pocket being premium. Italy's public test (€200 to the patient) was only the three trisomies and not even fetal sex. The Netherlands and Belgium offer genome-wide (premium) panels from day one.
- The interviewer's summary, accepted by the expert, was that the main growth driver is rising premium mix, with flat volume and flat premium prices.

## Public sector, reimbursement and internalization risk

- Trend: producers push labs to internalize. If government reimbursement starts, internalizing makes sense, since reimbursement usually covers costs. An NIPT-only lab is no longer competitive, because the market is global and labs must offer more than NIPT.
- Reimbursement levels: Belgium pays three accredited private labs (one-third of samples each) about €260 per test, and pregnant women pay €80 to the government. The expert calls this "bad" but says a good price is about €250-300 and "a good business" if cost is €100. Italy's draft plan proposed paying hospitals €280. Entering Belgium would be "impossible."
- Italy: a draft project promised an eight-figure budget covering over 75,000 patients. For about three years it has not been approved because money is lacking. Italian public health structure is "dramatic." The expert's own accredited-lab example: a €10 million government budget at €280 per test equals about 35,000 tests.
- Spain: finances improving ("probably now Spain is the new Germany" industrially), but adding NIPT to public coverage when the private market works well is probably not happening.
- Netherlands: sequencing done by two big universities (TRIDENT NIPT study, in the expert's recollection Rotterdam and another), with high penetration.
- Lost business (direct experience): Lombardy ran a trial offering NIPT free in three leading Milan hospitals, each with 5,000-10,000 births a year. Veritas lost "at least 30%" of its Lombardy market that year. When funding ended and offers narrowed to at-risk patients at reduced prices, Veritas returned to prior revenue in the region.
- Earlier, in 2016-2017 many commercial NIPT resellers (white-labeling a lab's test) existed. They have almost disappeared in high-income countries because they were acquired or built labs.
- The expert agreed with the interviewer that a hospital copying a lab is technically easy ("they pay millions to Illumina and Illumina is building the lab"), and that the barrier is the system and funding, not capability.

## Mergers and acquisitions

- Large labs acquire small ones because it is easier and cheaper than winning an additional 5,000 tests. Subscale labs "cannot survive" at current costs and genomics is too expensive for small labs.
- Valuation: typically about 10x EBITDA, but often there is no EBITDA, so the buyer covers debt and pays a little extra to save the company.
- Integration: redirect testing to the central lab (Barcelona), cut non-operating cost, merge or remove duplicate management. Full integration (IT, reporting, marketing, sales) takes six months if fast, about a year if slow.
- Preferred targets are countries where Veritas is already strong (e.g., Italy, where it has no lab but is "quite strong") or where a lab exists. Weaker markets such as Greece are harder. Cross-border deals bring legal complexity, since hospital contracts must be fulfilled as written (e.g., samples sent from Greece to Spain). The interviewer noted odd commercial practices in Eastern Europe, notably Serbia.

## Veritas business mix and preventive genomics

- Veritas has three areas: perinatal (prenatal), diagnostics (patients with symptoms, via hospital contracts in oncology, cardiovascular risk, immunodeficiency and neurological disorders), and preventive (healthy people testing to stratify risk, sold via insurers, clinics and private hospital checkups, no direct-to-consumer).
- Preventive is currently the biggest trend ("the market is growing"), and a big part of revenue but below 50%. The interviewer's split: prenatal slightly under 50% (NIPT about 35%, other prenatal about 10%), with the rest diagnostics and preventive.
- Reimbursement: diagnostics is mostly reimbursed, including by public systems. Preventive is 100% out of pocket (or insurer-paid privately), since most health systems treat only urgent needs.
- Veritas runs the entire flow: receives samples, sequences, and interprets with its own medical team issuing signed reports. It can also provide sequencing only and return raw data to hospitals. It uses exome or genome (non-targeted) sequencing rather than its own kits.
- Example customer: Clínica Universidad de Navarra, which includes preventive panels in checkups. Veritas has been its main provider for nearly 10 years.
- Illumina link: sequencing is on Illumina and reagents are bought from Illumina.
- Synergies: NIPT and genome work use different sequencers and library prep, though DNA extraction machines can be shared. Senior technicians and supervisors are partly cross-trained, with separate supervisors for NIPT and genome/exome. Adding genomics to an acquired lab requires new machines, training, and strong IT.
- Marketing: NIPT is the most aggressive channel. Cross-selling is possible (e.g., a woman tested at 30 may buy a breast and ovarian cancer panel or pharmacogenomics at 35), but synergy is "not so strong" because different physicians are targeted. Gynecologist-targeted products include exome-based carrier screening and myNewborn, a newborn panel checking over 450 childhood-relevant conditions, which Veritas is opening and books under prenatal.

## Expert's overall conclusion

- "If I have €10 million in my pocket, I will not invest in a NIPT lab at the moment." The expert would invest in other genomic services or run a commercial (non-lab) company, calling new NIPT entry "a really high risk activity" in a business that "is not a moving business."
