# Genomics core facility director (Ivy League university) on sequencing demand, Illumina vs. Roche/Ultima/PacBio, pricing and Q3 2026 trends

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-12_Director_of_Genomics_Core_Facility_at_Research_Center_Sees_Stable_Growth_in_Sequencing_Tec.md` · Date: 2026-10-12 · Expert call on an AI-interviewer channel-check platform (published 15 Oct 2026; interview 12 Oct 2026). Speaker: unnamed director of a genomics core facility (customer perspective). Interviewer: AI. · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer-side channel check with the director of a genomics core facility at an Ivy League university, a research-use-only lab that runs about 18,000-20,000 gigabases a year, roughly 85-90% on Illumina short-read and 10-15% on PacBio long-read. The expert reports Q3 2026 output and consumable spending up about 5% year over year, expects about 5% (up to 10%) growth in 2027, and expects Illumina to gain slight share on single-cell and spatial growth. They see Roche AXELIOS one as a promising but niche competitor they are still evaluating, and Ultima (with falling cost per genome) as the bigger long-term threat to Illumina.

They described it as a core facility "within an Ivy League university." They oversee advanced sequencing operations supporting academic research plus some external users from other institutions and companies. They have experience evaluating and operating both short-read and long-read platforms. [Expert: genomics core facility director, customer, Oct 2026]
- **Relationship to Illumina.** The expert is a customer, not a former employee or competitor. The lab runs 100% of its short-read work on Illumina and 100% of its long-read work on PacBio.
- **Recency.** The interview was on 12 Oct 2026 and the questions focused on Q3 2026 and the outlook for the rest of 2026 and 2027, so the experience is current.

## Lab profile

- **Annual output.** The expert described annual output as equivalent to about 200 whole human genomes at 30X coverage (30X means each DNA base is read about 30 times on average). A 30X genome is about 90-100 gigabases (Gb, billions of bases of sequence data), so the interviewer's confirmed total is roughly 18,000-20,000 Gb per year. The expert confirmed this ("Right").
- **Instrument fleet (confirmed twice).** Short-read: one Illumina NovaSeq X (high-throughput production sequencer) and one Illumina MiSeq i100 Plus (small, low-throughput benchtop). Long-read: one PacBio Revio. The lab also has 10x Genomics single-cell systems and 10x spatial transcriptomics (measuring gene activity while keeping track of where in a tissue it occurs). All single-cell sequencing and the sequencing-based spatial work run on the Illumina short-read platform.
- **Regulatory status.** Research use only, no CLIA certification (the U.S. clinical-lab certification), not used for clinical diagnosis.

## Demand drivers (tailwinds and headwinds)

### Tailwinds for short-read (Illumina) sequencing

- **10x Genomics Single Cell Flex assay v2** (called GEM-X Flex v2 by the interviewer), released this calendar year. The expert says it "dramatically lowers the per sample or per cell library prep cost" (library prep is preparing DNA or RNA for sequencing), which "drums up the sequencing demand."
- **On-chip multiplexing (OCM)** for fresh living-cell RNA-seq, applicable to 5-prime and 3-prime assays. The expert said it "really increased the single-cell sequencing RNA demand."
- **Visium HD 3' assay (10x Genomics).** A sequencing-based spatial method that supports all eukaryotic poly(A)-based mRNA without pre-designed, pre-synthesized probe panels limited to the human genome. It greatly broadened the supported organisms' genomes and transcriptomes.

### Headwind: imaging-based spatial

- The lab has a 10x Xenium In Situ imaging system, which has very high resolution and is heavily used. The expert says it is pulling work away from sequencing-based Visium HD 3', which "from the perspective of a short-read sequencing provider… is a negative impact."
- Reasoning: imaging systems like Xenium (and most probe-based systems) need pre-synthesized oligoprobes (short DNA detector sequences) that mostly cover human and possibly mouse genes. In the lab's experience, human or mouse sample owners prefer Xenium for better resolution.
- Visium HD 3' is chosen mainly by researchers with non-human, non-mouse animals (and maybe plants in future), who "don't have a practical other choice" because custom Xenium gene panels are "too expensive."

### Forward-looking tailwind: Illumina StrataMap

- The expert says Illumina has just released StrataMap Spatial Solution, an instrument-free, sequencing-based, Visium-like spatial system.
- Features cited: a capture area of 15 mm x 50 mm, "several times larger" than the current Xenium In Situ slide area and larger than the latest 10x Artera imaging-based system; it supports all eukaryotic tissue sections.
- Expert's view: very attractive; Illumina is promoting use of its own sequencers with a "highly competitive sequencing-based in situ spatial transcriptomics system."
- Lock-in observation: the expert says Illumina openly admits it will not allow the spatial location file to be decoded on other sequencing platforms; StrataMap must be analyzed with Illumina software to decode physical location.

## Short-read vs. long-read mix

- **Current mix.** The expert first said short-read is about 80% of output and PacBio 15%; when the interviewer noted that adds to 95%, they said that was rounding and PacBio is 10-15%. The agreed figure is 85-90% Illumina short-read and 10-15% PacBio long-read, by gigabases.
- **Why the split.** The research question determines it. Long-read on PacBio is needed for (1) de novo whole-genome assembly (building a genome from scratch) for human and especially non-human, non-mouse organisms, and (2) Fiber-seq, which detects chromatin openness (how accessible DNA is) and methylation (chemical marks on DNA) together from long DNA stretches in one assay. The maturation of Fiber-seq is "one of the biggest positive driving force[s]" for PacBio usage. Short-read is driven mainly by single-cell and spatial.
- **Next 12 months.** The expert expects single-cell and spatial growth to outpace Fiber-seq and genome assembly growth, so PacBio's share will "probably slightly decrease," helped somewhat by RNA isoform applications (distinguishing different versions of a gene's transcript) that need long reads. The interviewer paraphrased this as long-read absolute volume still growing.
- **PacBio cost cut.** PacBio recently released a new reusable SMRT Cell (PacBio's sequencing chip) that the expert says can cut sequencing cost by "more than half," letting PacBio claim a $500 human genome at 20X. The expert expects this to help use of long-read, especially full-length mRNA Iso-Seq. Drawback: total spending on PacBio may not rise much because reagent prices were lowered.
- **Iso-Seq.** PacBio's Iso-Seq kit can generate 60-80 million full-length mRNA reads for isoform quantification, which the expert calls the one variable that may raise PacBio usage, alongside Fiber-seq.

## Competitive landscape

### Short-read share at this lab

- Currently 100% Illumina. Q2 to Q3 2026: no obvious change in vendor share, short-read or long-read.
- Next 12 months: the choice in this core is "simply between the Illumina short-read versus the PacBio long-read"; the expert expects Illumina to "slightly gain the share" of the lab's output, mainly because of single-cell and spatial growth ("Exactly. This will be the main reason.").

### Roche AXELIOS one (SBX)

- **Status.** The lab is actively evaluating it but has not adopted it. The expert wants real-world data from regular users rather than what Roche shows "in their own expert's hand" in webinars; performance "may or may not be as high as the broadcast data."
- **Attractions.** Fastest speed and very high read throughput, a natural fit for single-cell and spatial. The expert expects it to open up "read greedy" applications not previously feasible. They described it as the most likely next non-Illumina system, ahead of Element. (In the transcript the expert also calls SBX a "nanopore sequencer," which appears to be a misstatement; treat with caution.)
- **Concerns.**
  - Reusable sensor: it must gradually lose activity or sensitivity, has a finite life, and could cause gradual decline in yield or read quality. This could introduce "systematic bias" between sequential runs, which is hard to mitigate for a service provider.
  - Cost: the expert hoped for about $100 per human genome (close to Ultima's level) versus Illumina's roughly $200; "I think Roche priced the per-run cost a little too high. That will reduce its adoption rate."
  - Maturity: "very promising" but needs stabilization and optimization soon after release; the expert prefers to wait rather than be "the early pioneers."
- **Threat assessment.** The expert sees AXELIOS one as "more like a niche competitor."
- **Bar to purchase.** (1) Stable yield and read quality over the whole life of a reusable sensor module; (2) openness, meaning how much users can control sequencing time and configuration rather than it being pre-locked by the manufacturer.
- **Timing.** A purchase depends on technical performance and funding; if not in the next 12 months, then likely the following year, and "this new acquisition got to happen within the next two years." (The interviewer summarized it as a potential 12-24 month acquisition.)

### Element Biosciences

- Next most likely platform to be acquired after Roche, considering both the existing AVITI24 (with in-situ sequencing capability) and its new higher-throughput systems, but "much less likely." Element has been "quiet" on incentives.

### Ultima

- The expert calls Ultima the real long-term threat to Illumina. Ultima's run cost is already about $80 per human genome, and the expert believes it will approach half, about $50 per human genome.
- At that level, "the Illumina $200 per human genome sequencer will gradually… shift to the Ultima system, especially starting with the big genome centers or NGS providers."

### Oxford Nanopore and PacBio

- The lab trialed Oxford Nanopore with a few samples about three or four years ago, before buying the Revio. From its own experience and the community, the expert says PacBio is "much more reliable and stable" for consistent data as an NGS provider. All long-read output is PacBio.
- The expert says PacBio's yield claims are often the maximum rather than guaranteed, which disadvantages service providers, in contrast with Illumina (below).

### Instrument incentives

- Q3 2026: the expert "heard" higher incentives, without acting. Sources: Ultima and Oxford Nanopore (much less from Element). They want to compete against Illumina; the expert roughly summarized their discounts at about 15-20% off list price.

## Illumina products and roadmap

- **NovaSeq X.** "Very stable." Illumina's official specifications are conservative (guaranteed low side); the lab always gets yield above the maximum specification, "up to 50% higher."
- **MiSeq i100 Plus.** Used for amplicon (targeted PCR product) and very small genomes. Its advantage is very long reads with high accuracy: it can sequence 1.2-1.5 kilobase libraries using a 1,000-base read length, "unmatched by any other competing systems, even including the new Roche AXELIOS one and nanopore sequencers." The mix between low- and high-throughput instruments is stable because they complement each other.
- **NovaSeq X yield roadmap.** The interviewer cited Illumina's commitment to raise flow cell output by up to 40% and speed whole genome sequencing by about 30%. The expert says a 40% yield increase means fewer runs for the same data output: data output stable, slightly fewer NovaSeq X runs. It probably will not change spending, since the upgrade is mostly software and internal procedures; users could save money or use the same spend to get more reads. "The bottom line is this probably will not drive up the spending on NovaSeq runs."
- **TruPath Genome.** Launched February 2026, removes library prep from whole-genome workflows at about $395 per sample list (interviewer's figure). The lab has not adopted it: it is "a niche application"; as a synthetic long-read, it cannot assemble a de novo mammalian genome, only provides phased reads, and has limited research value in their community. It cannot provide methylation status. The expert compared roughly $3,000 for TruPath with about $1,000 for PacBio, now only $700; both capability and cost favor PacBio Revio, so TruPath gives no incentive to shift long-read work to Illumina for labs with a Revio. (The $3,000 comparison figure is as stated by the expert and is not otherwise explained.)
- **SomaLogic (acquisition closed January 2026, per interviewer).** The expert calls it a smart move giving Illumina tighter control, and suspects Illumina might lock SomaLogic's SOMAmers (aptamer protein-detection reagents) to Illumina sequencers, as with StrataMap. They still prefer Olink's antibody-based detection over "the aptamer approximation." Overall they think it will probably increase Illumina sequencer usage. For this lab: neutral, since it does not use SomaLogic.
- **StrataMap.** See the spatial section above.

## Instrument purchasing

- No sequencer purchases in Q3 2026, so no placement plans changed.
- Capital spending on instruments in 2026 vs. 2025: no change (flat).
- Possible next purchase: Roche AXELIOS one within about two years.

## Consumables and pricing

- **Spending.** Q3 2026 reagent spending up about 5% versus Q3 2025, comparable to data output growth, so mostly volume.
- **Price.** Only a slight pricing factor: Illumina lowered the price of its 5-billion-read and 10-billion-read flow cell kits to be close to the 25-billion-read unit price. Prices for comparable consumables across platforms in 2026 vs. 2025: "the same." Discounts and terms were about the same versus Q2; instead of discounts, list prices get slightly lowered through technical improvements or new technology.
- **Inventory and lead times.** The lab holds little stock because delivery is quick; inventory is "sufficient with little overstock." No stocking or destocking in Q3. Lead times unchanged, "very short, very quick."

## Vendor support

- All vendors' sales and technical support are "working really hard and at high proficiency"; differences are in product features and price. Support levels in Q3 were unchanged and "near perfect, cannot improve much."

## Emerging technologies and closing
