# Cancer center core-facility director on Illumina NovaSeq X, Roche AXELIOS, Oxford Nanopore and TruPath (channel check, Q3 2026)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-03_Director_at_Cancer_Center_Optimistic_About_Sequencing_Growth_and_Evaluates_Emerging_Techno.md` · Date: 2026-10-03 · Channel check platform (AI Interviewer); expert is a customer, director at a cancer center sequencing core facility; interview 2026-10-03, published 2026-10-09 · expert call*

**Summary.** A director of a research-use-only core lab at a cancer center, running essentially 100% Illumina short-read plus a new Oxford Nanopore instrument, reports Q3 2026 output above expectations (about +25% year over year, driven by one large RNA-Seq project) and expects 10-15% growth in 2026 and at least 20% in 2027 as a new NovaSeq X Plus cuts cost per sample. The expert sees Roche's AXELIOS as a real threat to Illumina if it delivers, but doubts its reusable-module model suits core labs, has not adopted Illumina's TruPath, and notes Illumina reagent prices rose roughly 2-8% in 2026 including a 6% tariff surcharge.

## Expert background and reliability

The expert is a **customer** (a director at a cancer center) who runs a sequencing **core facility** (a shared lab that sequences samples for many researchers). They have about 15 years in this role. The lab has eight people. It is research-use-only (RUO, meaning not a CLIA-certified clinical diagnostic lab). It has visibility into Illumina and Oxford Nanopore instruments. [Expert: cancer center core facility director, interview 2026-10-03]

Most users are internal to the cancer center. There is also a significant external business from other universities and cancer centers that send samples in. The interviewer was an AI. The expert mostly speaks from direct experience on their own lab, but gives estimates ("best guess", "I can guess if I have to") on year-over-year percentages, and speculates about Roche's AXELIOS, TruPath and future share. They have not run AXELIOS or TruPath. The expert has no exposure to Roche, Element or Ultima short-read systems.

## Lab scale and fleet

- Annual output is not tracked in gigabases. The expert says the lab runs about **100 NovaSeq flow cells** per year (a flow cell is the consumable slide on which a run happens). Mix: about 25% the largest type (they say "like 20 S4"), about 50% S1, and about 25% SP.
- Short-read fleet: Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (recently bought), NextSeq 2000, MiSeq i100, and an "old MiSeq". The lab previously ran a NovaSeq 6000 for eight to nine years. The expert also says "we are using NovaSeq 6000" this year and "I would say 90% usage will be on the 6000 or X Plus."
- Long-read: Oxford Nanopore PromethION P2 Solo, bought about four months before the interview (a couple of months before using it). It is the smallest Nanopore model.

## Demand drivers: tailwinds and headwinds

**Tailwinds** [Expert, Oct 2026]:
- New interest in genomics and big grants.
- Falling sequencing cost, which the expert calls the biggest driver of more sequencing. Sample volumes have increased significantly over the expert's 15 years.
- Newer assays such as single-cell sequencing need more sequencing per sample.

**Headwinds**:
- A harder funding environment in recent years, with reduced funding leading to reduced sequencing need.
- The constant need to renew sequencers. A million-dollar-plus sequencer every four to five years is challenging for a core facility. The NovaSeq 6000 became not cost-effective versus the NovaSeq X Plus, forcing the upgrade. The expert says it is "challenging for us to keep pace with buying new sequencers."

## Funding mix and Q3 2026 conditions

After correcting an initial answer (60% grants, 25% institutional, under 15% industry, which summed to 95%), the expert settled on:

| Funding source | Share of sequencing |
|---|---|
| Federal and foundation grants | 60% |
| Institutional funds (e.g., development funds PIs use to generate preliminary data or start projects) | 30% |
| Industry-sponsored research | 10% |

The expert said the majority of research sequencing is grant-funded. In Q3 2026 versus the prior quarter, grant funding was **unchanged** and pharma/biotech-funded work was **as expected**.

## Short-read vs long-read

- About **99% short-read, under 1% long-read** (long-read is R&D to learn the technique and optimize assays; volume "is nothing right now").
- Why short-read dominates: cheaper, faster, easier to use, higher throughput (thousands of samples quickly), and all assays are already optimized for it. Long-read is for niche projects hard to answer with short-read.
- Outlook: long-read should gain some traction, but at best about **5% of total sequencing** in the next 12 months. Barriers: cost (still very expensive), low throughput (the P2 Solo does two genomes at a time and takes three to four days), and lingering concern about long-read quality and accuracy versus short-read. The expert says long-read "is never going to be at a scale like short-read sequencing." To scale, it needs lower cost and faster speed.

## Q3 2026 output and outlook

| Period | Expert's view |
|---|---|
| Q3 2026 vs expectations | Exceeded. A very large project (thousands of RNA-Seq samples) started earlier than scheduled (it had been planned for early 2027). |
| Q3 2026 vs Q3 2025 | Increased; best guess **at least +25%** (no exact figure) |
| Full-year 2026 vs 2025 | Increase of **10-15%**, mainly because the new sequencer lowers cost and people will sequence more |
| 2027 vs 2026 | Increase, at least **20%** (conservative minimum), expected 25% or more |

For 2027 the expert expects the new sequencer to make sequencing about **50% cheaper**, hoping that someone planning 20 RNA-Seq samples now does 30 or 40. The 2027 acceleration versus 2026 is attributed to cheaper sequencing and recent new funding; it is conditional on an improving funding environment and further cost cuts through automation. The expert is optimistic for the rest of 2026, not because of sample trends but because a few researchers recently received new funding.

## Competitive share

- Short-read output is **essentially 100% Illumina**; the lab has no Roche, Element or Ultima systems, so share did not change in Q3 2026.
- Over 12 months, Nanopore should gain a little from a tiny base as new assays come online, but the expert expects it to be **additive, not substitutive**: "I don't see Nanopore really taking any sequencing away from Illumina." Illumina volume should stay flat or keep rising. The two platforms "don't compete directly" in what they can do.
- Instrument mix within Illumina: about **90% of use on NovaSeq (6000 or X Plus), 10% on smaller instruments** (NextSeq 2000, MiSeq i100, MiSeq), which are used for small or medium projects, quick turnaround on few samples, testing and optimization. The expert expects the mix to stay the same.

## Roche AXELIOS (opinion, no hands-on use)

- The lab has **not adopted** it but is interested. The expert saw the instrument at a conference and calls it "really powerful" for speed, cost and reusable modules. They said it "will become an absolute powerhouse for clinical genomics lab", and could be a good addition in coming years, perhaps next year or the following. They want to wait until it is more mainstream and other labs use it.
- Biggest limitation: so far Roche has only shown whole-genome data and some assays. A core lab runs about **50 different assay types**, and whole genome is a small fraction of its work. The expert is hesitant about how fast Roche can adapt the other assays.
- Threat to Illumina: "It is actually a pretty big threat if they can deliver what they are promising. They can eat a lot of Illumina's business."
- Illumina's moat (expert's reasoning): its long time in market means assays were all developed with Illumina in mind, so any new sequencer forces users to adapt assays. Illumina is also more flexible: four or five flow cell types let users run from five samples to thousands, whereas Roche has so far shown one module whose output is controlled by run time.
- Reusable-module concern: the module has a limited window of use (the expert says roughly a month) to maximize cost benefit. Core labs have unpredictable workloads, batching, and weeks spent just making libraries, so they could "lose money" on it. Clinical labs and companies with predictable volume and schedules (every day or every three days) would find it easier. The expert says they "still have difficulty imagining that machine in a core facility."

## Illumina roadmap and products

- **NovaSeq X roadmap** (interviewer stated Illumina has committed to raising NovaSeq X flow cell output by up to 40% and whole-genome speed by about 30%): the expert says this certainly helps. In academic research cost is the main driver, and higher flow cell throughput lowers cost per gigabase and motivates purchases. Speed is not always the main purchase driver but makes turnaround faster.
- **TruPath Genome** (launched Feb 2026 per the interviewer; removes library prep from the whole-genome workflow): **not adopted**. The expert calls it a cool concept, but it cannot multiplex (one sample per lane), so it is slightly more expensive than standard whole-genome. They are waiting to see how others like it. They doubt it will replace long-read because it is not truly sequencing long continuous DNA stretches. If it can resolve complex DNA structures, it might help move some work from standard whole genome. The expert admits they are unsure about the product.
- **SomaLogic** (interviewer: acquisition closed January 2026; it is proteomics, measuring proteins): the lab does little proteomics and it does not change their motivation to use Illumina. But it helps Illumina's multi-omics portfolio, and people in the protein field who were not thinking about Illumina now will, so the expert expects more business from the proteomics community.

## Instrument purchases and capex

- No plans to buy, replace or upgrade sequencers in the next 12 months (X Plus just bought). No immediate plan to expand Nanopore within a year; a larger Nanopore model could be considered later.
- No acceleration, delay or cancellation in Q3 2026; the purchase plan had run for about a year.
- 2026 instrument capex versus 2025: "a huge increase", about **+100%** in the expert's framing (two sequencers bought in 2026, none in 2025). Purchases usually happen every three to five years.
- Instrument discounts or incentives from Illumina and Nanopore in Q3 2026: **same** as the prior quarter.

## Consumables and pricing

- Q3 2026 consumables spend versus Q3 2025: no exact figure, but the expert says it likely rose by the same 20-25% as sequencing volume, driven by **volume, not price**.
- Pricing: Illumina changes prices every January or February. To make NovaSeq X Plus reagents cheaper, Illumina raised NovaSeq 6000 reagent prices, by about **2-3%** per the expert. Illumina also charges a **6% tariff surcharge** across all its reagents. Combined: "at least 8% increase" versus last year, but the expert is unsure when the surcharge began. If it began around May 2025, the 6% is already in last year's base and the net increase is only 2%. If it began December 2025 or January 2026, it adds to the increase. The interviewer summarized the net range as **2-8%**, and the expert agreed.
- Reagent commitment terms or discounts in Q3 2026: same as prior quarter.
- Inventory: "sufficient as needed", no overstocking; lead times in Q3 2026 unchanged.

## Vendor support

- Sales and technical support is generally good for both Illumina and Nanopore, and unchanged in Q3 2026.
- Bioinformatics support is weak at both vendors for non-bioinformaticians: they provide a document and assume expertise instead of walking users through end to end. The expert says "we get frustrated."

## Emerging technology watch

The expert is watching sequencing companies expanding into **spatial profiling** (measuring gene activity while keeping tissue location) and beyond plain sequencing data. They note Illumina is trying to get long-read-like capability from short-read via TruPath, Illumina is coming up with a spatial platform, and Element is doing spatial sequencing. They are curious how these platforms yield other meaningful data.
