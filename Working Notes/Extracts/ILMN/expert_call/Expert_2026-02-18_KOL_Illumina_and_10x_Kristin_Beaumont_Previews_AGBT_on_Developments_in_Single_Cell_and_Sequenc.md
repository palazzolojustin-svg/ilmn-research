# Academic KOL (Mount Sinai genomics core) previews AGBT 2026 and updates on Q4 2025 / Q1 2026 sequencing, single-cell and spatial spending

**Summary.** A core-facility director at Mount Sinai who is a customer of Illumina, PacBio and 10x Genomics describes a weak Q4 2025 (spend down at least 20% versus a normal year) followed by a much stronger start to Q1 2026, helped by better federal funding sentiment. She previews AGBT (Roche's new sequencer, Element's surprise high-throughput instrument, PacBio's reusable flow cells and SPRQ-Nx chemistry), explains why she is not shifting work from Illumina to PacBio yet, and discusses 10x Flex v2, spatial growth, methylation and protein. Her view is that Roche and Element are worth watching but it is too early to call them a threat to Illumina.

# Expert call notes: Kristin Beaumont, Mount Sinai (Guidepoint, 18 Feb 2026)

## Expert background and basis of views

- **Who:** Kristin Beaumont (KB) is an Assistant Professor of Genetics and Genomic Sciences at the Icahn School of Medicine at Mount Sinai. The moderator introduced her as associate director as well as assistant professor. She is described as an academic key opinion leader (KOL), a physician scientist viewpoint. The call was moderated by Andrew Carmen (AC) of Guidepoint and lasted about 51 minutes. It was a repeat call ("once again", "since the last time we spoke").
- **Relationship to Illumina and peers:** She is a **customer**, not a former employee. She runs a community-resource genomics core: multiple projects come in from multiple principal investigators (PIs, the lead scientists who hold research grants). The core buys Illumina sequencing reagents, PacBio long-read sequencing, and 10x Genomics single-cell and spatial products. Her stated expertise is single cell and spatial.
- **Instruments:** The core still runs the **NovaSeq 6000** (Illumina's previous-generation high-throughput sequencer) and mostly uses S4 flow cells (the largest-capacity flow cell for that machine). It has Revio (PacBio's long-read sequencer) and has worked with PacBio's new SPRQ chemistry. It does its own library sequencing for single cell and spatial work.
- **Recency:** Her observations are first-hand and current, covering Q4 2025 and roughly the first weeks of Q1 2026. She cautioned that January is an unusual month for her group's purchasing, so she could not estimate Q1 reliably until most of February had passed.
- **Direct experience versus speculation:** Spending, utilization, Flex v2 handling, Xenium tissue-array tactics and PacBio chemistry testing are direct experience. Views on Roche, Element, PacBio's strategy, 10x's clinical push and 2026 growth areas are her opinions or predictions. She had not yet seen the AGBT presentations.
- **Compliance:** The moderator read the Guidepoint statements asking the advisor to avoid commentary on stocks, valuation and material nonpublic information.

## Funding environment (NIH and federal)

- **Opening view:** As of the start of 2026, KB sees "general increased positivity around federal funding" [Expert, Feb 2026]. She notes a "surprising number of increases in project submissions, project interest, people looking to really kick off big efforts". This contrasts with early 2025, when there were worries about grants "running out or being pulled".
- **Cause unclear:** She does not know whether more grants are actually being funded or whether researchers are simply ready to move ahead with money they already have.
- **Remaining problems:** Collaborators still report some budgets cut by 15%, delayed grant reviews, delayed paperwork, and requests for applications (RFAs, NIH funding announcements) not going out when expected. By and large, though, PIs now have access to grants, and money that had not been released is accessible. More people are starting projects and spending, rather than hoarding grant money out of worry. She says people are still cautious and are "still submitting grants like crazy".
- **Stabilizers she cites:** The NIH budget has not been slashed substantially; there is bipartisan support for keeping it in place; and indirect rates (the overhead portion of a grant that covers costs such as rent) have been stated by government as not to be touched, with caveats.
- **Caveat on indirect rates:** There is discussion of applicants stating where they are located (New York, California, Oklahoma), which could feed into funding calculations. She calls this a roundabout way of affecting indirect rates, since rent is higher in New York and California.
- **Human models over animal models:** NIH has communicated that it will no longer support grants where animal-only models make up the bulk. Her PIs are pivoting from mouse models to human systems such as iPSCs (induced pluripotent stem cells, human cells reprogrammed to behave like stem cells). Mount Sinai has a strong iPSC effort, and she sees many more iPSC-based submissions. Diseases that iPSCs model well are attracting more grant applications.

## Utilization and spending: Q4 2025 and Q1 2026

### Q4 2025
- October and November are normally busy for her core, but were not in 2025. She was concerned that expected projects did not materialize. There was activity, but at a much lower rate than a typical October and November.
- Little uptick appeared until right before Christmas, when people began asking whether they could submit large projects after the holidays.
- Spending: Q4 2025 was "pretty comparable" to Q3 2025, and both were "definitely lower than what we would expect in a normal year... down by minimum of 20%". There was little change between Q3 and Q4. Customers were not spending at the end of Q4; they were preparing to spend in Q1 2026.

### Q1 2026
- The first couple of weeks of January were substantially busier, in submissions and project organization, than Q4 2025. She was "very surprised" by the activity.
- Q1 2026 is looking "much closer to a normal Q1", which she compared to Q1 2024. Q1 2025 she called "all over the place". She said she would speak with more confidence in a couple of weeks.
- Her conclusion: Q1 looks much better than she would have projected from Q3 and Q4 2025.

### By vendor
| Vendor | Q4 2025 | Q1 2026 |
|---|---|---|
| 10x Genomics (single cell and spatial) | Down the most, "substantially lower" than expected | Rebounding the most |
| Illumina | Down, but less than 10x | Up, but less than 10x |
| PacBio | Down, but less than 10x | Up, but less than 10x |

- **Her interpretation:** Illumina projects cost much less per sample, and PacBio falls in that cheaper category relative to 10x. Her less expensive assays fell less than the more expensive single-cell and spatial work. In Q1 the pattern reversed: everything is up, but 10x is up more than PacBio and Illumina.
- **Within 10x:** Even in Q4 2025, spatial was down less than single cell. In Q1 2026 she has some "quite large" spatial projects coming.
- Her go-forward budget "definitely" looks more normal this year.

### Capital equipment (capex)
- People are "still going to be a little conservative", per her group and others she talks to. Some instruments they considered buying this year will probably wait until the second half of 2026.
- They are considering applying for S1 grants (supplements for equipment) rather than drawing on their own budget. If the first half of 2026 is "absolutely outstanding", they may loosen up.

### Pricing, tariffs and inflation
- Reagent prices have risen slightly. She is less affected because the core buys in bulk and often passes costs on to clients. It is "something people are aware of" but "hasn't necessarily been a problem or been prohibitive".
- Pricing with major vendors is discussed yearly, which fixes costs for the year barring extenuating circumstances. She is not aware of dramatic new pricing coming.

## Illumina-specific points

- **Utilization:** Her NovaSeq 6000 utilization is "definitely" back up. Part of it comes from 10x demand, because the core sequences its own single-cell and spatial libraries, so "when 10x is up, Illumina is also naturally up".
- **Why not NovaSeq X:** Under the 10x workflow it takes several projects with the same sequencing configuration to fill an S4 flow cell. Historically, high submission rates filled one within a couple of weeks. That math is why NovaSeq X and NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina's newer, higher-throughput sequencers) were not something the core planned to buy.
- **Fragmentation of configurations:** With 10x offering more assays, each with its own optimal configuration, fewer libraries match, so flow cells fill more slowly. Waiting too long for data is unacceptable to clients, so the core splits libraries onto smaller flow cells. This changes per-sample cost "a little bit, not terribly substantially". The optimization is under way, especially for Flex and Flex v2 experiments.
- **Possible diversification:** She said that if budgets allow, the core may consider adding a Roche or Element instrument. She confirmed that a NovaSeq X would not solve the fill problem, and that this is why the core has its eye on Element's HT instrument and possibly "the affiliates for Roche" (her phrasing). Testing samples on both will show which, if either, fits.
- **SomaLogic:** Asked about Illumina's purchase of SomaLogic assets, she said protein is underused in her genetics-focused group, historically a "gut check" (for example confirming T cells or B cells) and the domain of pathology departments. She wants to build protein capabilities, had hoped to do so in 2025 before budget disruption, and has recommitted this year. She will explore the new assays. Protein work currently skews to immunology, some cancer; she would like neuroscience and broader uses not matched by commercial products.
- **5-base methylation:** Methylation sequencing has been very expensive, so it must be targeted or is price prohibitive. She gets about "a handful of inquiries" a year, and has always found some workaround, though not at single-cell or spatially resolved level. There is "a little bit more traction" this year, but assays need optimizing for sample types, cost reduction and bioinformatics. She said "genomics is a very cart and horse field" in that cost-effective methods unlock new questions. She does not know offhand whether Revio gives methylation data, then recalled one project where methylation came from Revio. She thinks 10x's acquisition of Scale was partly motivated by Scale's methylation assay. Distinguishing 5mC from 5hmC (two chemical forms of methylation) arises in one or two projects, but she says the methodology is not proven enough for people to ask those questions confidently.

## AGBT 2026 preview and competitors

- **Mood:** The prior couple of years were "low key"; this year has interesting developments.
- **Roche (short-read challenger):** Until a few weeks before, the short-read conversation was Illumina versus Roche. She wants technical specifications and more data. She cites released information such as the "Q39 score" (a very high accuracy measure) and early datasets. She says Roche's approach is "slightly different than what we're used to with Illumina" and that 2025 "showed a lot of teasers". She plans to attend all of Roche's sessions. She cautions that vendor metrics come from cell lines; real-world patient-sample performance will emerge later. She is curious whether Roche's Xpandomers (the molecules its chemistry uses) will help data accuracy.
- **Element:** Its announcement of a high-throughput sequencer was a surprise to her and "a lot of people". Its "Beyond" event was due the next day (19 Feb 2026) with more detail. Earlier Element buzz was about cyto-profiling rather than NGS. She asks how much detail and access Element will offer, and whether it changes the Illumina-versus-Roche conversation.
- **Is it a threat to Illumina?** "A little bit too early to tell." Illumina has a foothold, brand recognition and loyalty from users who have run its instruments "forever". She said "there has to be so much more beyond just potential cost savings" to make a dent. But "I don't think it's beyond the realm of possibility", as there seems to be advancement every few months. She will watch post-AGBT adoption in later quarters.
- **PacBio refocus:** She sees PacBio refocusing on long read after offloading some short-read work to Illumina (the Omniome assets). She thinks it "very much" looks like the end of PacBio's short-read effort, speculating that, given budgets and the economy, PacBio might say "we're done with short-read sequencing for now" and focus on long read. This is her prediction, and she hopes AGBT clarifies it.
- **Other AGBT item:** Stellaromics will discuss 3D spatial transcriptomics.

## PacBio long-read experience

- **Strategic view:** She likes PacBio's push to make long read accessible. The perception has been that long read is too expensive or hard to get quality data from. She believes long read answers unique questions.
- **SPRQ / SPRQ-Nx on Revio:** The core has run a few projects on the chemistry. Certain projects still make sense on Illumina and others on PacBio. They are still evaluating accuracy and depth on real patient samples and the cost per sample, noting that company metrics are optimized and often not from real samples. Some projects always go to PacBio because of the biological question.
- **Switching:** "Nothing is shifting." They are not moving work from Illumina to PacBio yet. Many short-read projects are long-term and would not be moved. But some long-read-suited questions have been run on Illumina for cost reasons, and could move once cost and experimental design are understood. A lower price from Nx would "certainly help".
- **Reusable SMRT cells:** She is excited from a cost and resource standpoint but it is early. PacBio has recommended a range of reuses, from twice to a few more, probably application dependent (perhaps two for one experiment, four for another). The key issue is data degradation. Even 5% or 10% degradation matters. For a community core serving multiple PIs, it would be hard to tell the PI on the second project that data will be slightly compromised. Degradation might be 10% in some cases and 30% in others, unclear. The core has done the cost math several ways: sometimes reuse makes sense, sometimes not, depending on recovery.
- **The 30% figure:** PacBio's general guidance to her is about 30% less data on the second use compared with the first (she believes, "if I'm not mistaken"). She wants hands-on testing. She may reuse a flow cell multiple times within one large project, and plans to sort this out in 2026.

## 10x Genomics: Flex v2, spatial and clinical

### Flex v2
- Flex v2 decouples parts of the bench workflow to make it modular. Instead of a few recommended designs, users can dial the number of cells, sequencing depth, and the number of samples multiplexed (pooled together) to fit budgets and goals.
- Upside: done well, per-sample cost can fall "much closer to a bulk RNA-Seq type of price". Caveat: the cell numbers and multiplexing that give that price may not apply to every project, so pricing must be worked out project by project. The core is testing limits on real samples (how many to multiplex, minimum cells) against 10x's suggested ranges.
- Her view: it is more complicated and will take getting used to, but gives more tools to fit a client's budget, and "it's going to open up single cell to potentially some users that have been a little hesitant to use it in the past because of cost".

### Spatial versus single cell
- She does not see spatial replacing single cell; they address different questions (data depth, analysis). More people are doing both, for example mostly spatial with some single-cell experiments to help annotate cell types.
- On Xenium, most of her users do not use commercial panels but customized ones. Custom panels designed from single-cell data (both gene lists and expression levels) greatly improve value, yielding cost-effective, "spectacular data".
- She says spatial projects are hitting the same inflection single cell did: pilot studies are done and larger-scale projects (Visium HD or Xenium, both platforms) are being submitted.

### Cost tactics
- Visium: little can be done beyond creative mounting to get two samples per assay instead of one.
- Xenium: the capture area is about 12 x 24 mm on a standard slide, analyzed with one reagent kit. She fitted **21 tissue punches** into one area in a recent project (tissue microarray approach). Her grant strategy: propose a Visium HD or single-cell pilot, use it to design a custom Xenium panel, then scale with a tissue microarray. PIs have received it well and some funding has come through.
- 10x provides white papers and tech support but not sample preparation products for microarrays, which is pathology territory.

### Clinical push
- 10x's collaboration with **Dana-Farber** to push into clinical applications is, in her view, "really exciting", a shift the field has awaited. Clinical use (CLIA-certified assays billable to insurers) needs much greater rigor and approval on an assay-by-assay basis, which is difficult but could substantially widen 10x's market and share.
- Groundwork: 10x has worked to handle FFPE samples (formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded, the standard preservation for most biorepository tissue). Basic science is now producing interpretable disease insight at single-cell and spatial levels, so she thinks we may be at a convergence point. Oncology is a logical first area. This is her opinion; she wants to see if 10x says more at AGBT.

### Clinical sequencing generally
- She does not do clinical sequencing and hesitates to predict. The clinical market is driven by insurance reimbursement and the state of healthcare more than science. She sees solid preclinical submissions, which is encouraging for developing clinical assets, but would not predict gap-filling for research funding.

## Growth areas in 2026 (her view)

- **Spatial:** The strongest. Bulk sequencing gave way to single cell; now spatial is reaching beyond early adopters, with investigators returning with papers and grants asking for spatial data, and more creative designs beyond proof of concept. Stellaromics' 3D spatial is a trend.
- **Protein:** Everyone knows it is needed and it is getting easier, for example with panels introduced by 10x.
- **Integration across methods:** Single cell with spatial, and bulk sequencing with single-cell deconvolution through bioinformatics, rather than separate datasets.

## Closing view

- KB is "cautiously optimistic" for AGBT and the rest of 2026. "Q1 started off really well for us", and the core is "in a much better place this year than we were last year". She hopes for steady growth and that "people can get excited about science again".
