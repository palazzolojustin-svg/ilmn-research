# Expert call with a Vanderbilt University Medical Center sequencing core manager (customer) on adding Roche, Ultima or Element alongside Illumina

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-04-02_Next_Generation_Sequencing_Manager_at_Vanderbilt_University_Medical_Center_Sees_Roche_as_S.md` · Date: 2026-04-02 · Expert-network transcript (published 16 Apr 2026); interviewer from an investor-led (buy-side) firm; expert is the Next Generation Sequencing Manager at Vanderbilt University Medical Center (name not given) · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer who runs an academic sequencing core with only Illumina machines says their leadership wants a second sequencing vendor to cut costs and add options, and that Roche's new sequencer is the leading candidate because of Roche's brand, clinical reach and very fast turnaround. Ultima and Element are also on the table. The expert does not expect to drop Illumina within five years, criticizes Illumina's product releases, support and customer service, but calls Illumina's DRAGEN data analysis a strong point that competitors lack.

## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

**Role and relationship.** The expert is a customer, not a former Illumina employee. They are the Next Generation Sequencing Manager at Vanderbilt University Medical Center (VUMC), where they run the sequencing core (a shared lab that sequences samples for researchers and outside clients). They have been there almost eight years as of the interview [Expert: Vanderbilt sequencing core manager, 2 Apr 2026]. The expert's profile says they have hands-on experience with Illumina, Ultima Genomics and Roche instruments. The interviewer was a buy-side investor, and the interview was conducted on 2 Apr 2026 (published 16 Apr 2026).

**Prior role.** Before Vanderbilt, the expert was Senior Scientist/Assistant Director of Sequencing Operations at HudsonAlpha Institute for Biotechnology, leaving in December 2018 per the bio. The bio says they worked hands-on with 8-12 Illumina sequencers. In the call the expert describes HudsonAlpha as having 10 NovaSeq X sequencers, working under Dr. Shawn Levy, and having "every platform": PacBio, Element and Oxford Nanopore. They describe it as "very high-level production sequencing." (The call mixes the timeline: it says eight years at HudsonAlpha and almost eight at Vanderbilt, while the bio says HudsonAlpha ended in December 2018.)

**Current Illumina fleet at Vanderbilt.** Two NovaSeq 6000s, one NovaSeq Xp, one MiSeq i100 and one NextSeq, all Illumina. The expert says the lab is "at almost max capacity" at six 25B flow cells per week on the NovaSeq Xp. (The 25B is the largest, and per the expert the cheapest-per-read, NovaSeq X flow cell.)

**Experience base.** Most of the call is direct experience (current fleet, costs, spending, past Illumina problems, HudsonAlpha experience, 10x workflows). Statements about the Roche instrument's chemistry, speed and quality are mostly secondhand impressions or expectations, since the lab has not bought one. The expert says they have seen data quality from Roche and from 10x's early Roche testing, and have a Roche salesperson. Statements about Illumina's future and about the market as a whole are the expert's opinion.

## Why Vanderbilt wants a second sequencing vendor

- Leadership's strategic goal is to introduce a competing sequencing technology into the core "for innovation, for different chemistry, to help drive down costs, to provide other options to investigators" [Expert, 2 Apr 2026].
- The expert says leadership told them: "We are tired of Illumina. We don't want to deal with Illumina anymore. Illumina is done." They also say the core will continue to offer Illumina, which is trusted and "a foundation for our sequencing." The expert is tasked with deciding where the strategy goes.
- Precedent: the expert did the same with single-cell sequencing. They call 10x Genomics "the mafia of single cell" and "still the gold standard," with very high-quality assays. Adding other single-cell platforms helped drive down cost and gave options for different budgets and quality needs. Leadership wants to repeat that in sequencing.
- Budget context: the core is 100% cost recovery (it must charge users enough to pay its own costs). The expert says it is hard for academic institutions, small labs or even for-profit companies to get capital equipment approved or to find about $1 million for a piece of equipment.

## The expert's view of how competition arose

- Illumina's patent on its chemistry expired "a few years ago." Before that, short-read sequencing (reading short DNA fragments) had only Illumina, apart from BGI in another country. Long-read had PacBio and Oxford Nanopore. The expert calls Illumina "the mafia of sequencing."
- After the patent expired, smaller competitors emerged. It took time to build reputation and confidence given Illumina's size.
- Ultima broke in by collaborating with the Broad Institute and others with large resources that did not have to worry about equipment costs. "The Broad is unlimited money."
- The expert lists viable competitors as Element, Ultima and now Roche.

## Roche: the leading candidate

**Why Roche is viable.**
- Mainly its name: "Everybody trusts Roche." Roche has been in the expert's field for years, and the lab uses various Roche instruments and reagents, including KAPA library prep reagents (kits that prepare DNA for sequencing).
- Roche's sales reps and technical support staff stay with the company, unlike people who job-hop between Covaris, Thermo Fisher and Illumina, which the expert says does not build confidence.
- The long development time is "a good and a bad thing": it created anticipation and, in the expert's view, confidence that Roche was taking the time to get it right.
- Roche has a clinical presence, which the expert says sequencing needs. Clinical sequencing has been hard to expand because of insurance reimbursement. The expert sees Roche as a possible bridge.
- The expert says "I think Roche is a heavy, heavy presence for sequencing. I think they're going to do really, really well." They also note having heard "good and bad things," mainly questions about quality and pricing.

**Pricing expectation (opinion).** To compete, Roche must at least match Illumina's price per gigabase (a unit of sequencing output) or per whole genome. "I don't think they're going to be dumb enough to make it more expensive." The expert says customers claim to want quality but "really want to save money. That's number one on their list," and will sacrifice slightly on quality for lower price.

**Chemistry and quality.**
- The interviewer asked about Roche's new hairpin chemistry (SBX) versus Illumina's SBS (sequencing by synthesis). The expert says everyone has been "hungry for a new chemistry" after 18-20 years of SBS. Even Ultima's chemistry is a different version of SBS.
- Most people trust Roche's name and chemistry and expect top-notch quality. Some "naysayers," whom the expert calls "grumpy old men," are waiting to see if quality holds up.
- The expert says they saw data quality from the Roche platform and was "very excited about the quality of the data that we saw."
- Most see it as a clinical instrument because of the quality of the chemistry. Even Vanderbilt leadership asked if it was the right one to evaluate because it "seems more on the clinical side." The expert wants to combine clinical and research use, since leadership has asked them to help grow clinical sequencing.

**Turnaround time and run speed.**
- The expert says Roche's fast sequencing is "very, very significant." They confirm Roche is "the fastest" in turnaround. Other sequencers run faster than Illumina but not as fast as Roche.
- The interviewer cited a four-hour Roche run versus about a day for Illumina. The expert did not dispute it.
- Why it matters: the expert wins big contracts on fast turnaround. They do sequencing for UAB and commercial firms such as Cofactor Genomics, which act as middlemen. They say they cannot match the pricing of Novogene (a large China-based sequencing service provider) because they lack its throughput, but they compete on speed.
- The expert says turnaround is "almost equal with cost" and "more important than quality." People "will sacrifice a little bit of quality for a faster turnaround time," for example 95% over 98%. They compare it to not wanting to wait 48 hours for Amazon packages.

**Mid-run loading ("batching").**
- The interviewer described being able to start a run, then open it and add samples. The expert says it is similar to starting a different runtime but is "not a big plus" for them. They plan runs Monday, Wednesday and Friday with two flow cells each.
- They see opening an instrument mid-run as "a potential source of error" (software could freeze, wrong samples could be added). Possibly useful for clinical, and with Roche's speed one could just wait for the next run.

**Compatibility with single cell and spatial.**
- The interviewer asked about duplex versus simplex modes and counting applications. The expert did not discuss modes directly but said 10x Genomics' development team is like Roche's (top-notch, tests well, "slow because of that").
- 10x has collaborated with Roche and released initial testing on Roche's platform that the expert calls "high, high quality." The expert says the Roche instrument is compatible with 10x single-cell and spatial samples.
- The expert says they would never buy a sequencer incompatible with all their sample types. Single cell is about 40% of their sequencing.
- Spending: low seven digits per year with 10x Genomics, a couple hundred thousand dollars with BD Genomics, and mid six digits with Illumina's PIPseq (a single-cell product the expert said they weren't sure of the current name).

## Vanderbilt's possible plan with Roche

- A clinical use case: Vanderbilt developed a clonal hematopoiesis custom assay (a test using custom probes to look at blood-cell mutations) priced at $12 per sample. The core ran 180,000 samples last year. The clinical lab wants to port it. Each sample needs only about one million reads.
- A Roche instrument could be validated for clinical use and also used for research, and the clinical lab could later buy its own Roche, with Vanderbilt having taught them to run it.
- The expert's tactic: run the clonal hematopoiesis samples on the Roche without telling customers, using a bundled price. Pulling these off the 25B flow cells would not affect cost or turnaround for others. It would help the team learn the instrument and library creation. Then they would advertise data quality via seminars and validation runs and move everyone over.
- Eventually, with Roche (or Ultima) the expert expects "everybody will move over" and Illumina could be faded out or used for legacy projects, though they say the process must be careful because "we don't want to lose money."
- Risk: two library types with different adapters is "a wet bench nightmare." Mislabeling could waste a roughly $13,000 flow cell.

## Ultima Genomics

- The expert calls Ultima "a true competitor for Illumina for large-scale sequencing" and met with them the day before the interview (1 Apr 2026). They describe Ultima as "smart, very strategic."
- Concerns: "People have major concerns about quality with Ultima," which Ultima addresses daily. Ultima has only single-read sequencing and is introducing paired-end soon.
- Ultima has historically been high throughput. It now has 5B flow cells and is scaling down offerings to be accessible to small and medium throughput labs.
- Labs that have Ultima machines have very long turnaround: the expert sends libraries out to such labs and is told data takes six months.
- Ultima would "pretty much put an instrument in my lab for free" in exchange for a reagent purchase commitment.
- The expert's idea: move all 10x single-cell work to Ultima, using 10B flow cells at the same price as 25Bs, hoping Illumina responds to competition by cutting prices. Data comparisons would show quality stays high.
- Weakness: Ultima's smaller sequencer comes with a server but no preloaded analysis pipelines. The expert would have to buy licenses for BWA and others and link them to their LIMS, which is "a problem."

## Element Biosciences

- The expert notes Element's AVITI is modular: buy the sequencer at a flat price (about $300,000 for the small sequencer) and add or upgrade components, including spatial, over time. This is "very appealing to a lab that's limited or has capital equipment" constraints.
- Element could support a multi-omics approach (multiple assays on one instrument). The expert says multiomics, single-cell and spatial are "the future," moving beyond standard base-by-base sequencing.
- Element generates FASTQ files (raw sequence output files) that load into the lab's DRAGEN server, so analysis is not a problem.
- The expert also considered moving 10x workloads to Element, which would take them off the 25B.

## Economics of introducing a second platform

- A new sequencer takes capacity from the NovaSeq. Because the 25B flow cell is cheapest only when full, diverting volume raises costs or turnaround time (which the expert says is the same as raising cost) for Illumina users.
- The aim is to avoid raising costs on one platform while introducing another. "Illumina is going to come right back and give me a discount too." The expert wants to make both competitive, with each platform suited to certain assays.
- The expert believes every core will end up with a non-Illumina provider and that "they can't all be just Illumina." Most have already.

## Five-year outlook for Illumina (opinion)

- In five years, the expert does not expect to remove Illumina fully. "There are always going to be legacy projects." Illumina will still be "a big part" in five years, but ten years from now "Illumina may just be a really small sequencing, a part of sequencing."
- The expert is unsure Illumina can keep up technologically.

## Criticism of Illumina (direct experience)

**Product releases.** The expert says Illumina has "really good ideas, their constellation, their spatial," but development is slow, products are insufficiently tested in-house, and after release "there's all sorts of upgrades and issues." They say Illumina is secretive, scared of idea theft, and avoids alpha and beta testing with customers.

**Examples given.**
- HiSeq X patterned flow cells (a new SBS chemistry layout): HudsonAlpha was an early adopter. Index hopping (samples being misassigned between barcodes) caused contamination between samples. Illumina sent a team of 20 people to HudsonAlpha. The fix was dual indexing. The expert says Illumina should have tested this in-house.
- NovaSeq Xp software and hardware issues on release: doors would not open, screen would not lift, software froze. A camera timeout issue shut instruments down because imaging large flow cells took long. Since reagents are one-time use (the barcode cannot be re-read), failed runs were costly. At HudsonAlpha, with nine NovaSeqs in production running 36 flow cells weekly, "at least four or five of those would fail" until a software fix.
- Spatial genomics: the expert says "they're having major issues with that spatial genomics" in software and that Illumina's multi-assay vision is "very, very far into the future." Their team will not support it, and it is not going to happen at Vanderbilt.

**Service.** "Their tech support isn't optimal. Their customer service isn't optimal." They say Illumina "got too large and forgot who the customer is," focusing on big commercial customers (naming "Nova") and forgetting those who "buy the juice" (reagents).

## Library prep

- The expert had limited information on Roche-specific library prep and suggested the interviewer find someone else for it. They call library prep "very technically challenging," requiring highly skilled staff, and say there has been "no progress in 18 years." Sequencing and data analysis are now simple (load a kit; load FASTQs). They say a library-prep-on-a-chip would make its inventor "a billionaire."
- The interviewer said Roche library prep reportedly takes several hours longer. The expert said extra time would cost money but they could mitigate by batching, robots and liquid handlers.
- Cost: the expert says library prep costs about $70 per sample for Illumina reagents, tech time and robot time, including adapter ligation, and assumes Roche is similar. Adapter ligation alone is about $7.25 per sample.
- They call Illumina's claim of a $200 whole genome "a lie" because it excludes sample prep and QC. Reaching that would require 72 whole genomes on a 25B flow cell.
- Plan if buying Roche: move all library prep, including for Illumina sequencing, to Roche kits (e.g., KAPA HyperPrep), splitting only at the final PCR/adapter step into Illumina or Roche adapters, with separate storage. This minimizes errors, increases discounts and efficiency. The expert says it "wouldn't be a deal breaker, but it is more money."
- The interviewer inferred Illumina's sample-prep revenue from Vanderbilt could drop ~90% ($70 to $7). The expert said library prep kits come from the front end being Roche, with only the ligation as the Illumina portion.
- The expert would also move extraction kits (blood, FFPE) and hybridization kits to Roche. Currently they use Twist Bioscience for hybridization capture and spend low seven digits a year there, and would move that to Roche (NimbleGen) for single-vendor simplicity.

## Data analysis: Illumina's strongest advantage

- Illumina acquired DRAGEN from Edico (a fast processor that runs analysis pipelines). It is available in BaseSpace, Illumina's cloud application that streams data from the instrument.
- Vanderbilt used BaseSpace but spent about $500,000 a year, and IT "was really mad" at the data volume. They bought their own local DRAGEN server, which is "super-fast and super cheap."
- Vanderbilt is not an analysis core; a separate bioinformatics group does secondary and tertiary analysis. The core does data QC on every sample (e.g., aligning mouse samples to a mouse genome, checking 30x human whole genome coverage).
- One 25B flow cell generates 12-14 TB of data. Samples carry tags in a custom LIMS (laboratory information management system, a database linking sample IDs to data files, alignment and variant calls) built by a developer. One flow cell can hold 70 to 5,000 samples. Commercial LIMS such as Clarity exist but are limited.
- QC metrics: ≥98% alignment to the right species, 30x coverage (40x for clinical), contamination screen, sex check against phenotype, insert size. Failures are flagged to the expert.
- Competitor readiness: Element's FASTQs load into DRAGEN easily. Ultima's pipelines are unpackaged. Roche's salesperson "keeps just circling around," saying "We might have some solutions for you," with nothing prepackaged. Onboard solutions would not handle the planned data volume.
- The expert says this is "a weak point for all of the sequencing competitors" and a "very, very strong point for Illumina." "On the data analysis side, they are strong. That DRAGEN is really good." They say Illumina's instruments "aren't great and their customer service is bad." They will plan and budget for analysis before buying a competitor instrument, but many labs "are going to be stuck."
