# Channel check with an academic medical center NGS manager (Tennessee): cost drives shift from Illumina to Ultima and Element; TruPath too expensive; Axelios not compelling

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-09_Manager_at_Academic_Medical_Center_Believes_Cost_Efficiency_Drives_Sequencing_Platform_Sel.md` · Date: 2026-10-09 · Channel check on an expert-network platform (AI interviewer); expert is a Next Generation Sequencing Manager at a medical center in Tennessee (published 14 Oct 2026, interviewed 9 Oct 2026) · expert call*

**Summary.** A research-use-only sequencing core lab that runs about 1,000 gigabases a year, currently 100% on Illumina short-read instruments, plans to lease an Ultima instrument (over $1 million) and add an Element benchtop sequencer in June 2027, because their sequencing reagents are roughly 50-80% cheaper. The expert says Illumina's TruPath Genome is not cheaper than their own library-prep approach, Roche's Axelios is not cheap enough to be a competitor, and Illumina raised consumable prices 5-8% in 2026. Q3 2026 output fell about 8% year on year on weaker funding and a multi-omics shift, but they budget about +15% for 2027 on single-cell and spatial projects.

## Expert background and how to weigh the evidence

- The expert is a Next Generation Sequencing (NGS) Manager at a medical center in Tennessee. NGS means high-volume DNA/RNA sequencing. Their focus is on Illumina and Oxford Nanopore platforms. The platform labels them an "Expert Perspective: Partner" and the interviewer was an AI [Expert: NGS manager, academic medical center, 9 Oct 2026].
- The relationship to Illumina is that of a **customer** (channel check). This is current, direct experience, since the interview took place on 9 Oct 2026 and the questions cover Q3 2026.
- The lab is a **research-use-only (RUO)** lab, not a CLIA-certified clinical diagnostic lab ("Research only at this time"). It is a service core: about 60% of work is for internal investigators at the institution. The expert also said about 20% of samples come from external customers, of which 5-8% is pharma. They also described internal funding as "almost 100% of that 80% internal" being grant funding. The expert also mentioned external for-profit and nonprofit investigators. (The 60% internal figure and the 80% internal figure are both stated in the call and are not reconciled.)
- Most statements are direct experience (the lab's own volumes, prices, budgets, plans). Views on the wider market (Axelios's eventual position, long-read adoption, methylation assays) are the expert's opinion.

## Lab profile and demand backdrop

- **Output:** about 1,000 gigabases (Gb, billions of DNA letters sequenced) per year.
- **Service lines:** a very wide range, with the lab on the sample-prep side. Methylation, single-cell, and whole-genome work all support output [Expert, Oct 2026].
- **Headwinds and tailwinds:** The expert named grant funding, limited sample availability and limited assay availability as the main factors. Service breadth is a tailwind. Funding is a slight headwind: it has not had a huge impact, but it is grant-writing season, causing a pause in sample submissions that lasted until the last couple of weeks before the interview.
- **Funding mix:** funding for internal work is almost entirely federal-style grants. Pharma is 5-8% of the external 20%.
- **Grant funding in Q3 2026 versus Q2:** "Increase." The expert's explanation is that researchers "got nervous" and applied for more grants and more types of grants (an opinion about researcher behavior, not an actual increase in awards).
- **Biopharma:** Worried about grants, the expert deliberately targeted biopharma. They contacted collaborators of academic institutions, ran literature searches to find relevant sequencing assays and collaborators, and aimed probe and assay development at those areas. Once assays were built, people reached out and collaborations followed. The result met their expectations, and they think the institution's biopharma funding target was exceeded (the expert was unsure).

## Output trends and outlook

| Period | Expert's figure | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Q3 2026 vs plan | Met expectations and budget | n/a |
| Q3 2026 vs Q3 2025 | Down about 8% | Mainly lower funding, plus a shift toward multi-omics (spreading money across sequencing, proteomics and other technologies) |

- The expert said they are neither optimistic nor pessimistic: "the market is changing towards a different type of approach to the science." They cited vendors adapting, such as "Illumina adopting the spatial genomics."

## Instrument fleet and short-read versus long-read

- **Short-read (all Illumina):** one NovaSeq X Plus, two NovaSeq 6000, one NextSeq, one MiSeq i100. **Long-read:** one Oxford Nanopore (ONT) PromethION.
- **Split:** roughly 99% short-read, about 1% long-read. Reasons: cost, the technology, and the fact that short-read usually suffices for research goals. "There are very few cases at my institution that you have a need for long-read technology."
- **What would move work to long-read:** lower cost, better technology and chemistry, and published papers with cost-benefit analyses, for example for the Undiagnosed Disease Network (UDN) project, showing that deeper long-read sequencing adds coverage and finds what was missed.
- **Expert's view of PacBio and Oxford Nanopore:** instruments and reagents are too expensive and the genomic benefits "are not there yet." Long-read can complement short-read, but most investigators do not want long-read only. Over 12 months the long-read share "might increase a little bit" but will not overtake short-read.

## Competitive landscape (short-read)

### Current and planned vendors
- **Today:** 100% Illumina, and no vendor share change in Q3 2026.
- **Ultima:** The lab is in the process of leasing/purchasing an Ultima instrument. The driver is the expected increase in single-cell and spatial genomics work. All single-cell and spatial work will move to Ultima, because its sequencing is "much cheaper" and pairs well with the very expensive wet-bench assays (the lab-chemistry steps before sequencing). Cheaper wet-bench assays stay on the more expensive Illumina sequencing.
- **Split after Ultima arrives:** By budget, 60% of the annual sequencing budget (single-cell and spatial) goes to Ultima. By sample count, only about 8-10% of samples go to Ultima, because single-cell and spatial assays process far fewer samples than custom capture, whole-exome or whole-genome work.
- **Element Biosciences:** The lab does about 150,000 samples a year on a cheap, fast, high-throughput assay now sequenced on Illumina. It wants a lower-throughput benchtop platform, such as Element, to run high sample counts needing little sequencing depth at lower cost. Budgeting for Element is in **June 2027**.
- **Three-tier strategy (confirmed by the expert):** Element for high-volume, low-read-depth samples, Ultima for expensive workflows needing many cheap reads, and Illumina for workflows in between.
- **Share outlook, next 12 months:** Ultima gains. Illumina shifts from the higher-throughput 25B flow cells (25 billion reads) to lower-throughput 10B flow cells. Element later takes some Illumina volume.

### Cost as the driver
- Of the share shift, the expert said cost is "100% the reason." "Most investigators will sacrifice a slightly lower quality, which is definitely the case with the Ultima and the Element, for a lower cost, a much lower cost, 50% lower cost."
- Ultima reagent cost reduction: about 80%. With instrument costs included, about a 50% reduction in total per-sample sequencing cost.
- Closing remark: the expert was "shocked by the price difference with Ultima on the reagent cost" and by the push from institute leadership and stakeholders to bring Ultima in. Instrument cost is "the same. There is no difference," but the reagent difference is "huge."

### Roche Axelios (Axelios one)
- The lab considered and investigated it but concluded the price difference was not low enough to be a competitor to Illumina.
- Method: they priced the instrument, service contract, depreciation and reagents, and modeled which workflows (10B flow cells, single cell/10x, captures, whole genome, RNA-seq) could move. Because they still owe depreciation on their Illumina instruments, they cannot move everything. Reagent and instrument costs were not much lower, and throughput did not justify the purchase.
- Threat view: "At first, I thought they were going to be a very big threat." Maybe in the clinical world they could hold some ground eventually, but pricing is not competitive enough and they have been very slow releasing instruments, so the expert is unsure where they will land.
- Overall landscape view: instrument competition is "flat right now," with Ultima, Element, Roche and Illumina "all fighting for the front."

## Illumina products and roadmap

### NovaSeq X roadmap
- The interviewer cited Illumina's commitment to raise NovaSeq X flow cell output by up to 40% and speed whole-genome sequencing by about 30%. The expert said it does **not** make them use NovaSeq X more.
- Reasons: the faster flow cells are the 35B (35 billion) cells, which are very difficult to fill. Most labs mix other samples with whole genomes to balance flow cells, so pure whole-genome runs are rare. Run times of 42-48 hours are already fast enough. Data processing and demultiplexing (sorting reads by sample) takes about 8-10 hours per flow cell anyway, with about 13 terabytes of data per 25B flow cell.

### TruPath Genome
- Per the interviewer, launched February 2026; removes library prep (the step that prepares DNA for sequencing) from whole genome workflows at a list price of about $395 per sample.
- The lab adopted it and was initially excited because staff no longer had to prep samples. But the all-in cost must include instrument depreciation, service contracts and lease: "It's never going to be a $400 genome."
- Comparison: a standard prepped whole genome costs less than $100 (Illumina-related instrument costs not described further). Pooling 72 samples on a 25B flow cell comes to less than $300 per genome. So it is cheaper not to use TruPath. The lab has run a couple of flow cells, easy for the lab but "not cheaper for the investigator."
- Share of output through TruPath: less than 1%. Quality was no different from the lab's PCR-free whole genome prep. No incentive to shift long-read work to Illumina via TruPath. "Illumina is going to have to reduce costs... even further if they want us to sell these."

### SomaLogic acquisition
- Interviewer: closed January 2026. Expert: no shift to Illumina. Some people use the "IPP" prep (as transcribed), but most investigators still use Olink (a competing proteomics product), possibly due to trust or Illumina not yet having built customer bandwidth. The expert is not sure.
- View on Illumina: it is "making the right moves towards the multi-omics world"; "I don't know if that was the right acquisition, but I do think that moving into broader areas is a good move."

### Methylation and epigenomics
- The expert flagged Illumina's methylation-based assay as up-and-coming, "very significant," and something that "could really benefit Illumina," with a large crowd of interested people. Such tests need to be validated by the lab. Beyond that, they track no emerging sequencing technologies.

## Capital spending and instruments

- Plan: purchase/lease an Ultima, for large expected single-cell and spatial projects, to expand capacity, and because Illumina is more expensive. No instrument plans were accelerated, delayed or cancelled in Q3 2026.
- 2026 instrument capital spending: higher, "double what it was last year," driven entirely by the Ultima lease, which is "over $1 million."
- Instrument discounts, incentives and placement terms: unchanged in Q3 2026 vs Q2.

## Consumables and pricing

- Q3 2026 consumables spend vs Q3 2025: about the same in dollars, with lower volume offset by price increases of 5-8% (attributed to tariffs or a vendor increase).
- The increase was from Illumina. The expert believes it was a general price increase, but when they asked, Illumina said it was due to tariffs and reagents and solvents shipped from overseas for manufacturing.
- Comparable consumable prices in 2026 vs 2025: about 5-7% higher.
- Reagent discounts, incentives and commitment terms: unchanged in Q3. Inventory: sufficient. Lead times: unchanged, no issues.

## Vendor support

- Illumina: informatics support (DRAGEN, BaseSpace) has always been excellent ("They go above and beyond"). Sales and technical support vary but have increased, particularly for this institution, which the expert thinks is likely because Illumina knows a competing technology is coming to their lab (speculation).
- Overall support rose slightly in Q3 2026.
- Ultima: very good support during the purchase process, including training, protocol support, and library preparation for Ultima instruments (the lab prepares libraries and sends them to Ultima to support its investigators).

## Key risks and takeaways for Illumina, as stated by the expert

- Cost is the dominant factor in platform selection, and Ultima's roughly 80% lower reagent cost (about 50% lower all-in per-sample cost) is shifting single-cell and spatial work off Illumina, with Element expected to take high-volume low-depth work from mid-2027.
- Illumina's 2026 price increases of 5-8% (tied by Illumina to tariffs) come as customers are cost-sensitive.
- TruPath is not cost-competitive for this lab at about $395 list.
- Axelios is not a competitive threat to this lab at current pricing and pace.
- Offsetting positives: strong informatics support, a methylation assay seen as promising, and Illumina's multi-omics direction viewed favorably.
