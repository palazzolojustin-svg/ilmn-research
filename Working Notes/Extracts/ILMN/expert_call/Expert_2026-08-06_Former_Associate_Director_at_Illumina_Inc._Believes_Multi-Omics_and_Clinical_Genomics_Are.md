## Why labs choose Illumina (the "IBM" analogy)

- The expert opens with the saying from the 1980s (possibly 1990s), "no one got fired for buying IBM," meaning leaders who chose the dominant vendor were safe, while those who cut corners with someone else risked their jobs. They say Illumina is "almost the IBM" of sequencing: dominant for almost two decades. [Expert, Aug 2026]
- **Installed base:** "Depending on whose number you look at," the expert thinks **80%+ of installations** (instruments in labs) are Illumina. This is an estimate, not a precise figure.
- **Service and support:** Illumina's size in service and support helps customers succeed, and reinforces the analogy.
- **Familiarity of leaders:** Almost everyone now in a leadership position in the field has worked with or been exposed to Illumina sequencing at some stage (in a lab, or as a principal investigator using the data). The expert says this creates an emotional and perceived-risk bias in Illumina's favor.
- **Decision factors that interleave:** confidence in risk and scalability, price, and whether the solution "will grow as you grow." Cost is "a major driver," but a cheaper option raises questions: is it sustainable for growth, and might the small vendor be out of business or acquired by a competitor in two years?
- **Illumina's positioning:** Not the cheapest, but "your dependable partner." The expert says Illumina is trying to avoid a race to the lowest dollar per base because it "can't win in that space" with a bigger burn rate (higher cash spend) than rivals. Its pitch is the totality: support, scale, structure and test menu that most competitors cannot match.
- **Customer maturity matters:**
  - Novice users focus on price and pick technology because it is "cooler, better, faster, cheaper," without thinking about sustainability.
  - Experienced users often already own Illumina and decide whether to invest in more Illumina or **diversify** platforms. That is a different conversation for Illumina.
- Net view: in most scenarios Illumina is "one step forward" (elsewhere "one, maybe two steps") and "it works well for Illumina right now."
- **Cautionary note:** The expert points out IBM's later decline: "Domination doesn't mean much if you are not able to keep up with the new players in the new field."

## Competitive landscape and market share

### How the landscape has changed
- Five years earlier Illumina enjoyed "almost pure dominance." PacBio and Oxford Nanopore (long-read sequencing, which reads much longer DNA stretches) were small niche players aimed at de novo assembly (building new genomes from scratch) and were "not really taking any market share." They also competed with each other, which "didn't really work well for either one."
- New entrants are different: Ultima and Element **do not compete with each other**; each is positioned to compete with Illumina in a distinct segment.

### Segment-by-segment
| Competitor | Where it attacks (per expert) |
|---|---|
| Ultima | The high end: big sequencing centers running hundreds of genomes a day and deep sequencing, i.e. the NovaSeq 6000 / high-throughput space. The expert calls this "probably the biggest space where there is lack of competition." |
| MGI and BGI | Also went predominantly after the high end, then diversified into smaller sequencers. |
| Element | The intermediate space, roughly the NextSeq portfolio: small and medium labs doing clinical and many applications at once, needing a flexible platform. |
| Roche | The "newer entrant"; expected to target pure clinical, with highly regulated products, leveraging its pharma and clinical orientation. Expert's view of intent, not confirmed fact. |
| PacBio / Oxford | Long-read niches (see below). |

- **Clinical now larger than research:** The expert says the clinical segment is growing fastest and has overtaken research. Illumina's mix used to be "50-some and 40-some" research versus clinical; **by the time the expert left (Sep 2024), clinical was 52%** of revenue. Afterward, with "the government change and research revenues," the business is "becoming predominantly clinical."
- **Roche's logic:** Roche would bring "a portfolio of highly regulated products" to compete where Illumina is growing revenue, again targeting areas of Illumina's strength not tackled by others.

### The "predators" framing and Illumina's structural disadvantage
- Illumina is in the middle with predators "biting the leg… the head… the arms," i.e. pressure from multiple angles. The expert says Illumina's responses show it sees this.
- **Strategic disadvantage:** Illumina fights many companies on many battlegrounds while each competitor fights only Illumina.
- **Pricing:** Newcomers can control their own destiny and "go below cost" to build installed base. Illumina must defend on cost against all of them, which pressures its margin on revenue. A race to the bottom "will only make things worse for Illumina" because of its bigger burn rate.
- **Market share outlook:** The expert gave no numeric share forecast beyond the 80%+ installed-base estimate; the thrust is gradual carve-outs from multiple directions.

## Short reads versus long reads

- **Original premise failed:** Long reads were expected to replace short reads. The expert says "we've seen that it was a false statement." At current technology, long reads cannot reach the scale and price point of short reads. Short reads are "good enough" for most things.
- **Short reads are getting longer:** Illumina is pushing read length (the expert mentions going to **500** with chemistry improvements; they refer to MiSeq chemistry) and developing an intermediate technology called **Constellation** (the expert says it descends from "molecular reads back in the day"). Tens of kilobases (long-read territory) are unlikely for short reads.
- **Validation point:** Illumina going after long reads "almost validates what PacBio has always tried to say," that you eventually need long reads. But scale and cost mean long reads will not replace short reads.
- **Why Illumina won despite not being first:** It was not first to market with short reads but scaled, improved the technology and cut prices significantly, widening the market. Long-read vendors lacked scale, size and cost point for a broad audience; Oxford and PacBio are "not bad at what they do."
- **PacBio's trajectory (expert opinion):** PacBio's early success came from the genome-reference era (sequencing new species to build reference genomes, sometimes in hybrid long plus short mode). Only the first one to three genomes of a species need this. Now the business is "plummeting" because that market is saturated, with "no new customers, no new genomes."
- **Clinical niche for long reads:**
  - Expert's yield framework for rare disease: panels get about **20%** of answers, exomes **40%–60%**, genomes about **80%**, leaving **10%–20%** unresolved with a regular Illumina genome.
  - The question is whether to add a long-read genome, or instead RNA-seq, epigenomics and other multi-omic assays.
  - In clinical practice some genes are hard to cover with short reads and are simply not reported; moving those to a new technology is "not clinically viable" on cost, turnaround or scale.
  - Expert's foreseeable-future view: "I did my best and got 90% of the genome" and many will be comfortable with 90%. Long reads will be stuck in that niche, "not enough to grow big numbers."
- **Hybrid mode will exist**, but the market is too small to make any long-read platform dominant; platforms compete with each other.
- **PacBio–Oxford merger chatter:** The expert saw LinkedIn chatter about a combination, calls it a "brilliant idea" since they cannibalize each other, but doubts any US regulator would approve it because they compete with each other (anti-competitive concerns). Speculation.
- **Roche:** Roche claims it will pursue long reads. The expert says "let's see if they can really get to a price point where it's a long-read genome with short-read prices." If you get the extra 10% for the cost of the 90%, most people would accept; otherwise it is a cost issue.
- **Bottom line:** "Long and short is a never-ending odyssey."

## Multi-omics strategy (SomaLogic, Fluent BioSciences, spatial)

*(The interviewer framed: Illumina acquired SomaLogic for proteomics, Fluent BioSciences for single-cell, and is building spatial biology solutions.)*

### The sample-saturation problem (direct experience)
- **NovaSeq X vs NovaSeq 6000:** The expert says NovaSeq X was priced similarly as an instrument but delivered the output of **four NovaSeq 6000s** at a run cost of "almost half, if not 1/3." The market initially loved it and bought it, but it **replaced many NovaSeq 6000s** and "cannibalized the business" because customers ran samples more cheaply.
- The core problem was not cheapness per se. At full capacity revenue would still be higher, but customers ran **out of samples**, so instruments were not used at capacity and Illumina did not realize the theoretical revenue it projected.
- This coincided with leadership change at Illumina. Leadership assumed a new machine would unlock more samples; instead the industry hit "a saturation point of incoming samples."
- **Attempts to find samples:** "We actually did sequence everything in the freezer. There's nothing left." Clinical samples arrive only when patients show up, and most patients don't get tested because they aren't reimbursed. Sequencers sit idle longer than they should.

### Is multi-omics a long-term driver?
- Expert's answer: "Is it a short-term strategy? 100%." It opens new sample types, diseases and use cases at price points not previously realistic. Illumina needed technologies it lacked in-house, so it acquired. The expert finds "both of the acquisitions pretty clever, especially SomaLogic."
- **Thermo response:** Thermo Fisher acquired Olink (a proteomics company) as "a really direct response" to Illumina's SomaLogic deal, solidifying a Thermo–Illumina fight already happening "with pretty much everything… in the clinical space," i.e. head to head.
- **Long-term doubts:**
  - Proteomics is a valid use case; the expert argues proteins do most biological work and thinks proteomics "is going to take off and supersede genomics."
  - Genomics generates less recurring revenue: "You do it once and you forget it. DNA doesn't change" (exception: cancer and MRD). Proteins change over time, requiring repeated measurement (through life, disease or even a day), implying larger revenue growth but more room for tailor-made and point-of-care solutions.
  - **Proteomics via DNA sequencing is like microarrays** (fixed-content chips): "You see what you see, but you don't see what you don't see." It also misses protein modifications that change activity and interactions.
  - The expert expects a newcomer with **native protein sequencing** could "easily just kill that market in one day," is "almost certain" such efforts are underway, and says it may come from Illumina itself, an acquisition, or an "IBM killer." Sequencing instruments would not be needed.
  - AI accelerates innovation, possibly shifting from single-vendor platforms to niche products that work from a drop of blood and deliver results fast.
- Summary: short term "definitely viable and most likely will succeed"; long term "more doubts."
- Follow-up: multi-omics is also a short-term bullish signal that should drive good revenue and potentially good margins, alongside clinical, but all segments face competition including from newcomers; the field is "ripe for takeovers."

## Growth outlook and clinical genomics

### Time horizon
- Short term = **two to three years at most**. After about 2.5 years the view moves into "long-term territory" and becomes "more questionable." The field grows exponentially and technology and adoption in three to five years are unknowable.

### Short-term bullish case
- More samples, integration, and Illumina's **end-to-end solution including informatics** (software for analyzing data), which eases entry for new labs.
- **Clinical penetration still tiny:** Genomics is used on only a small number of patients; not close to saturation "for a while."
- **NIPT (non-invasive prenatal testing, a blood test for fetal chromosomal conditions):** The nearest to saturation. The expert recalls recent figures of about **70%–80%** of pregnancies tested ("if I remember correctly"), meaning roughly **one in five** pregnancies still lacks the test despite coverage and reimbursement. Cancer testing numbers are "really, really low."
- Clinical revenue at Illumina is growing; the expert "always projected that it will take over." Room to grow especially in cancer.

### MRD (minimal/molecular residual disease) in cancer
- MRD preferred over Grail-type early detection as nearer to full penetration. Repeat testing creates recurring revenue; recurrence means retesting.
- Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP) is a great use case but done once, like the DNA problem.
- Multiplier: MRD can mean **three to four tests in a year** versus one test locking in revenue for years.
- Ultima has made public announcements about tackling MRD; Roche and others will likely follow. "Almost infinite potential to grow that market."
- Illumina's edge: installed base, scale, support, cancer product innovation, including **approval for TruSight Oncology 500** (comprehensive genomic profiling). It is going after liquid biopsy and, using that technology, into MRD.

### Risks
- **Illumina is only a supplier:** Clinical drivers are customers such as **BillionToOne, Natera, Guardant, Exact**, dominant in their fields and driving most revenue generation. The expert sees "two levels of risk": Illumina does not control the market and does not capture big margins, while customers have big bargaining power and probably press Illumina on margin and earn much more margin themselves.
- **Top-heavy customer concentration:** Illumina's clinical revenue is "very dominantly" from a few large customers. A competitor could pitch "cut your cost by half," faster and better. Losing top clients "would be a really big impact… on the budget."
- **Counterpoint (expert's lean):** Executives who switch and fail "will lose big" (IBM logic), and with tight margins, high risk and global uncertainty, the expert thinks most won't risk it, supporting the short-term bull case. "Debatable."
- **Dominance as risk:** "Illumina's position of dominance could be a risk factor for them with the top-heavy clinical clients potentially jumping ship."
- Overall: "There is enough room for everyone to grow."
