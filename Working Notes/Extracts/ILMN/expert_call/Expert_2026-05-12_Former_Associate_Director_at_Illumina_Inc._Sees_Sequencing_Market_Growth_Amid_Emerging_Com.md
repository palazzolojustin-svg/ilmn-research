# Former Illumina Associate Director (Portfolio Strategy), now at Bio-Rad, on sequencing market growth, Roche's new platform, Ultima/Element competition and Illumina's strategic choices

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-05-12_Former_Associate_Director_at_Illumina_Inc._Sees_Sequencing_Market_Growth_Amid_Emerging_Com.md` · Date: 2026-05-12 · Expert-network transcript (published 14 May 2026; interview 12 May 2026); expert is a former Associate Director, Portfolio Strategy at Illumina (left April 2024), now Director, Global Product Marketing - Oncology at Bio-Rad; client analyst unnamed; "Business-Led" analyst perspective · expert call*

**Summary.** A former Illumina portfolio-strategy lead describes the sequencing market as two segments, research (driven by multi-omics and pharma) and clinical (driven by oncology, especially MRD). The main risks are research funding and non-sequencing technologies. Illumina's dominance rests on its broad platform range, installed base, ecosystem and the high cost of switching in clinical settings. Roche's new nanopore sequencer is seen as promising but not an "Illumina killer" in the next few years (maximum ~5% share loss in two years), while Ultima is making inroads in high-throughput, cost-sensitive work. The expert says Illumina must choose between research and clinical, build end-to-end clinical solutions, and may have to cut prices to defend MRD volume.

## Outlook for sequencing market growth

The expert frames the market as two segments.

### Research / translational research
- Growth will be driven by multi-omics: combining genomics with transcriptomics (measuring gene activity), spatial and single-cell data, and multimodal data from non-sequencing sources, then applying AI to extract insight.
- Pharma is a big driver, trying to use this large volume of information in the translational phase (moving discoveries toward drugs) to shorten drug development timelines.
- In their words, research growth will come from "beyond genomics kinds of applications."

### Clinical
- Clinical oncology is expected to be "the main driver of growth," particularly MRD (monitoring a patient's response to therapy and whether intervention is needed).
- There is "emerging clinical utility" showing that liquid biopsy (blood-based cancer testing) and ctDNA (circulating tumor DNA) detection, by sequencing or other methods, can have better clinical impact than standard-of-care imaging. The expert calls this "a huge growth market."
- **Market size:** Depending on the estimate, total addressable markets (TAMs) are "somewhere between $10 million, $20 million, $30 billion" (as transcribed; context implies billions).
- **Repeat testing:** MRD requires serial monitoring of a patient rather than one test, which increases test volume.
- **Early cancer screening/detection** (finding cancer before symptoms) is seeing more adoption and will also drive the market.
- **Decentralization:** Today much clinical sequencing is centralized at labs such as Natera, Foundation and Guardant. Pharma wants tests broadly available, which is not possible without decentralized testing. The expert is "not quite sure which way that market is going to go" but says it will drive sequencing demand.

## Risks that could slow sequencing growth

- **Research funding (the biggest risk, per the expert):** The environment is "very uncertain," with headwinds "not just in the U.S. with NIH funding, but globally we're seeing a slowdown in research investment." The research funding environment "will really dictate" research-side growth.
- **Non-sequencing modalities in research:** Proteomics (which partly depends on sequencing) and new platforms. The expert cites 10x Genomics' recent launch of a spatial biology research platform that is "highly sensitive, highly precise and with high resolution" and does not depend on sequencing. If such technologies gain traction, that could slow sequencing adoption generally.
- **Clinical cost substitution:** Sequencing costs are falling but it is "still expensive." Labs seeking lower cost may choose PCR or other testing instead of sequencing.

## Long-read versus short-read sequencing

**Terms.** Short reads read small DNA fragments (the expert cites a limit of around 300 base pairs, the "letters" of DNA) with high accuracy; long reads read much longer stretches, useful for structural variants (large rearrangements of DNA).

- **Long term:** If a low-cost, easy-workflow long-read solution exists, the clinical market "would want that." Long reads have advantages for long structural variants in oncology and some genetic diseases. The expert thinks the market "might settle on long read" eventually, helped by advances: Illumina launched its own long-read technology, other companies exist, and Roche's new nanopore sequencer can do long reads.
- **Short to medium term:** Short reads will still dominate "because the quality of the sequencing is so much better with the technologies that we have right now." They do not expect long reads to affect the market in the short-to-medium term.
- **"Medium-read" idea:** The expert flags that Roche's sequencer can do roughly 600 to 800 base pairs, compared with ~300 for short reads. This is "not necessarily really long reads, but longer than 10 short reads" (as transcribed). They call this an interesting possibility.
- **Why long reads have been held back:** Lack of simple workflows and insufficient data accuracy. Clinical oncology needs very high accuracy to detect somatic mutations (acquired tumor mutations), especially very rare variants, and long reads have not provided it. PacBio and Oxford Nanopore have tried to enter oncology through collaborations and partnerships, but "it hasn't really succeeded yet." The expert believes accuracy is a large part of the reason.
- **How accuracy could improve:** The expert says they do not know. They suggest a nanopore sequencer reaching medium-read lengths with higher accuracy could solve it, but "it still remains to be proven."

## Illumina's market position and why it persists

**Strategic framing (expert opinion):** Illumina started as a tools and platform company but "has to move more towards a solution provider space." Eventually "sequencing is definitely going to become commoditized" (meaning products become interchangeable and compete on price), which is why Illumina is repositioning toward end-to-end solutions for sequencing labs and specific clinical solutions such as MRD, tumor testing and genomic testing.

**Reasons for dominance:**
1. **Breadth of platforms:** MiSeq (lower throughput), NovaSeq (factory/population scale), and mid-throughput instruments for clinical markets.
2. **Global commercial footprint** and wide adoption in research and clinical.
3. **Ecosystem:** Many third-party solutions, especially bioinformatics (data analysis software), are built around Illumina.
4. **Clinical switching barriers:** Once an assay is validated clinically, moving to a second platform requires revalidation, proof that the platform is stable, and enough support, because "you can't have downtime in a clinical world." Downtime can be tolerated in research but not in clinical. The expert calls this "a much higher barrier" to change.

**Challengers' limits:** Element and Ultima "have been successful to some extent" in chipping away at share, but neither covers the full range. Ultima is good for high-end sequencing but lacks lower-throughput solutions; Element is more for lower throughput but not higher throughput.

## Competition by throughput segment

| Segment | Competitive picture per expert |
|---|---|
| High throughput (sequencing-intensive, cost-driven, e.g. MRD, whole genome) | Pressure from Ultima and, "to some extent," BGI. Ultima's lower cost per GB (gigabase, a unit of data output) is giving it "some foothold," and it is "the name that keeps coming back, starting to make inroads." |
| Medium throughput (clinical) | Illumina "pretty well-established." Competition is mainly over library-prep solutions, reagents and assays, while the platform "more or less" stays with Illumina. Element has made some inroads, "but not to that extent." |
| Low throughput | Element competes by saying it is not just sequencing: spatial, single-cell and other proteomics applications can run on the same flow cell. At the very low end (iSeq 100, MiSeq), instruments are often used just to quality-check library preps, and this is "still very much dominated by Illumina." |

## Roche's new platform

**Basis:** Opinion from outside the company; expert is "carefully bullish."

**What is differentiated:**
- It is nanopore sequencing, with much more automation: library prep and sequencing sit on one instrument in the expert's description.
- **Turnaround time** is the biggest advantage, important in clinical use. Nanopore lets you sequence until you have enough information, then stop.
- Accuracy is "getting close to where Illumina is" and "will probably be as accurate as Illumina."
- Cost has not been released, but nanopore "should have a lower cost" in the end.
- It has "the potential to disrupt the market" technologically.

**Obstacles:**
- **Commercial:** Roche's commercial team is not used to selling into research; it is a clinical company with hospital and clinical-lab relationships. The expert repeatedly doubts how well Roche can sell in research. Past Roche sequencing efforts, including IP from labs for liquid biopsy assays, "have not really gone anywhere."
- **Clinical/regulatory:** Needs extensive clinical validation, product registration and decisions on which applications to pursue. Open questions include whether the library prep, auto-extractor, sequencer and data analysis all need separate FDA or IVDR (EU in-vitro diagnostic regulation) clearance or can be one system. The expert says regulatory pathways take "at least five years, if not 10," so they do not see huge clinical adoption soon.
- **Evidence:** Data shown so far comes from technologically sophisticated centers such as the Broad Institute and the Hartwig Institute, not from "the wild." Missing: data on liquid biopsy and FFPE (preserved tumor tissue) samples, the main clinical oncology sample types. Practical unknowns include instrument downtime, breakpoints and dependencies among the three units.
- **Comparison:** The expert says early Illumina also had imperfect instruments and library preps and needed heavy field support to drive clinical adoption.

**Threat to Illumina:**
- **Short term (next ~two years):** "Not an Illumina killer application." Expert's number: Roche would not take 20-30% of the market; "maximum 5%." It "could put some pricing pressure and some margin pressures" on Illumina if labs move.
- **Medium term (five to six years):** No huge impact expected.
- **Longer term:** If Roche leverages its diagnostics leadership (including owning Foundation) and moves clinical testing onto its platform, that "would put significant pressure on Illumina."
- **Research market:** Labs could switch quickly. Core labs might adopt it given lower sequencing cost and long-read capability for structural variants.

**Illumina's response options (expert opinion):** Offer more integrated clinical solutions. Today automation, library prep, assays and tertiary interpretation/reporting come from multiple companies (Illumina covers primary and secondary data interpretation). A true end-to-end solution would add stickiness that "might become difficult for Roche to overcome."

## Illumina's growth strategy (expert's recommendations and observations)

The client noted Illumina has struggled to post higher revenue growth in recent years. The expert's view:

1. **Clinical end-to-end solutions:** Offer clinical labs a single integrated solution.
2. **Keep high-intensity applications like MRD sticky:** Point to the fact that much current clinical data was generated on Illumina, so it is a reliable, trusted platform. These are about leveraging existing clinical position rather than technological advances.
3. **Monetize data:** Illumina already has a consortium with six or seven pharma companies to gain insights from whole genome sequencing data, and launched Illumina Connected Insights to build a cancer cell atlas.
4. **Multi-omics:** Focus on multi-omics, data generation and insights in research/translational work.
5. **Price defence:** With Ultima saying "Our cost is lower," "Illumina might even have to just bite on that and come under price pressure, lower the price," accepting lower margin to keep share.

**Strategic indecision (expert opinion, as an outside observer and former insider):**
- When CEO Jacob Thaysen first arrived (expert says "a couple of years ago"), the sense was a return to roots: great instruments, software and cutting-edge products for research.
- After NIH funding cuts, the expert saw a pivot into clinical, including a recently hired new GM of clinical, which they attribute to market pressures.
- Illumina says about 60% of its applications are clinical, but the expert says "clinical" is used very broadly, including clinical trials and translational work. "True clinical" means patient testing and day-to-day testing (genetic disease, NICUs, oncology).
- The expert says Illumina must decide between true clinical growth and research innovation: "that's a struggle that Illumina has always faced."

## Proteomics acquisition

- The expert calls it "more strategic" right now. Proteomics (study of proteins) is a growth area, especially large-scale proteomics, because "the proof is in the protein": it confirms something is biologically happening beyond what genomics shows. Pharma is beginning large-scale proteomic projects.
- Strategically "a great investment," but they are "not 100% sure" about immediate revenue impact over the next couple of years.
- Gut view: it is still a growing market and Illumina must get the right solutions out. Long term, discovery proteomics should evolve into focused clinical applications once key proteins for a disease are known.
- No quantitative estimate offered; the expert admits limited familiarity (speculation, not direct experience).
