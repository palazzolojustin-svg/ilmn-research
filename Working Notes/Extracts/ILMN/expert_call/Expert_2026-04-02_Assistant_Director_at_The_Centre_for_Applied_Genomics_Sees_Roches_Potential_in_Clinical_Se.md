# Expert call: Canadian genome center director on Roche SBX, Illumina switching thresholds and cost per genome

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-04-02_Assistant_Director_at_The_Centre_for_Applied_Genomics_Sees_Roches_Potential_in_Clinical_Se.md` · Date: 2026-04-02 · Expert network transcript (published 16 Apr 2026); Assistant Director at The Centre for Applied Genomics (Canada), interviewed by a buy-side investor; expert perspective "Consultant" · expert call*

**Summary.** A long-time Illumina customer running a large Canadian research genome center says Illumina is deeply entrenched and would only be displaced by a system that is much cheaper with equal or better data quality. He would switch to Roche's new SBX sequencer at roughly half the current fully loaded genome cost (about CA$200 versus somewhat over CA$400), but remains skeptical Roche can make its Xpandomer chemistry cheaply. He sees Roche's real opening in fast-turnaround clinical diagnostics, not research, and expects Illumina reagent prices to come under pressure.

## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

- **Who:** Assistant Director at The Centre for Applied Genomics, a genome center in a children's hospital in Ontario, Canada. PhD in molecular biology and molecular genetics (human genomics). Responsible for infrastructure grant writing and reporting, sitting on purchasing committees, and scientific strategy. The informatics and computational staff (a couple of dozen people) report to the expert. [Expert: Assistant Director, genome center, interview 2026-04-02]
- **Relationship:** Current Illumina customer (direct, hands-on experience), and a core facility that sequences for internal research and for external clients. Not a former employee. Perspective is that of a buyer and decision-maker. Experience is current (still running Illumina instruments in April 2026).
- **Illumina history:** The center has run Illumina systems since the Genome Analyzer, about 2008. The relationship has been "very long and abiding," though "sometimes it's been a little contentious back and forth."
- **Installed base:**
  - Two NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina's latest high-throughput sequencers).
  - Two older NovaSeq 6000s, "gradually sunsetting"; kept for small projects and for users who started projects on them and want to finish on the same instrument.
  - One MiSeq (a small Illumina system for small targeted experiments).
  - Two PacBio Revios (long-read sequencers), used "all day, every day."
  - One Oxford Nanopore PromethION 24 (not their highest-throughput model; a 48 exists), which is not used much.
- **Size:** About 100 staff (about half in the lab, a couple of dozen informatics, a couple of dozen admin and finance). Serves about 800 labs a year and touches about 45 countries. About 90% of clients are in Ontario; the other 10% is split roughly evenly between the rest of Canada and the rest of the world (weighted to the US and Europe). One of three large Canadian genome centers (with McGill in Montreal and Michael Smith Genome Sciences Centre in Vancouver), which together form a nationally funded sequencing network. The expert says it is not as big as the Broad Institute but "not a small genome center."
- **Research only:** The center does no clinical diagnostics because of how regulation works in the jurisdiction ("Research is research and clinical is clinical never the twain shall meet"). It works closely with the hospital's clinical genome diagnostics lab next door, which has its own NovaSeq X Plus for clinical work.

## Is the center looking for an Illumina alternative?

- The expert has no hands-on experience with Roche's new system. Everyone is interested in it; the center is sending someone to Roche show-and-tell events (one in Santa Clara, "in a couple of weeks," and one in Mannheim, Germany, two or three weeks after that).
- Knowledge so far comes from Roche's releases and AGBT (a genomics conference) in Florida a few weeks earlier, where real-world users including the Broad Institute presented. The expert is "always very suspicious" of manufacturer-generated data and prefers data from client labs. As a cautionary example, Complete Genomics "ran the thing 100X and cherry picked the best 10." Based on real-world data the Roche system "seems like it's pretty good."
- **Element:** Not the right size or shape; seen as a mid-throughput instrument, "a bit more like maybe an Illumina NextSeq." Does not solve any problem for the center. The expert does not say the technology is bad.
- **Ultima:** Colleagues in Montreal have one instrument and found the data good, but have not done much work on it (secondhand, not direct).
- **Bar to displace Illumina:** The system would need to be "significantly cheaper" and of at least equivalent, "probably better," data quality. The expert calls this "a high bar" because Illumina has spent years refining its instruments.
- **Why Illumina is entrenched:** Scientists worldwide understand the technology (fluorescent dye bases added in a single-base extension reaction), the data are well understood, and many analysis tools exist.

## Clinical sequencing and Roche

- Regulatory validation is a barrier to replacing an older technology for clinical use.
- A recently published paper using Roche technology for clinical diagnostics was "really impressive," with extremely fast turnaround ("we got the sample this morning and we got the result this afternoon"). The expert says "That really doesn't happen with Illumina sequencing."
- Expert's view: rapid turnaround is the big advantage. Conventional wisdom says speed doesn't matter for genetic syndromes because they are long-lasting, but the expert calls that "a fallacy." Stephen Kingsmore and some Oxford Nanopore groups have done very fast clinical diagnostics before. If Roche performs as the paper indicates, it "could be really important for the clinical diagnostics market."
- The hospital's clinical lab would buy its own instrument; if a system were "faster, cheaper, better, more accurate" the hospital "would look very carefully." Today they run only Illumina sequencers.
- The client suggested Roche may be targeting clinical (for example MRD, minimal residual disease testing) rather than research workloads. The expert agreed ("I would think so"): Roche has long had a diagnostics business, whereas Illumina started in research and has "made many attempts over the years to sneak into the clinical diagnostics market." Expert speculation: Roche likely sees genome sequencing becoming a frontline diagnostic test, and "where's the money. It's in the clinical diagnostics."
- **Roche history:** Roche previously acquired 454 (formerly CuraGen) and it "became one other thing in Roche's catalog of 50,000 products" and "died because they didn't support it." The expert does not know if SBX will be similar, noting many sequencing technologies are gone, "with the obvious exception of Illumina." Roche could succeed if it puts weight behind it (for example "if they want to put $30 billion behind something, they can").

## Counting applications

Asked whether Roche suits "counting" applications (such as measuring gene expression by counting how often a gene appears), the expert said "there may be a little bit of marketing hype/misdirection." Any sequencer can count; the difference is the analysis software. He finds it hard to believe Roche would be better or worse than others and does not expect "a jump step improvement." Caveat: no hands-on experience.

## Cost per genome

### Current economics
- The center's cheapest retail price for a genome on Illumina is "somewhere over CA$400," fully loaded (QC, sample prep, some labor, sequencing, informatics, data delivery). The expert puts the real cost around $300 to $400. A couple of years earlier the same genome was about CA$999 or CA$1,000.
- The largest single cost component is Illumina reagents (flow cells and chemicals), and most of the price is spend with Illumina.
- Expert's understanding (secondhand): library prep (preparing DNA to go on the sequencer) is roughly $100 on Roche versus $150 on Illumina, "not a big delta." Roche sequencing cost is said to be considerably lower; he has heard "a couple hundred bucks" per genome, with various numbers circulating. This matters over hundreds to thousands of genomes a month.

### Can Roche get there?
- The expert read the original technology paper and called it "really cool." Roche's SBX uses Xpandomers: heavily modified nucleotides, "enormous molecules with lots of fluorescent tags." He finds it "really hard to believe" they can be made cheaply, but "the proof will be in the retail pudding." Self-assessment: "I remain skeptical, but maybe I'm being a little bit too skeptical."
- The client suggested Illumina's reagent margin is huge and Roche may accept lower margins (for example 20% versus Illumina's 80%). The expert agreed: Illumina's reagents likely do not cost nearly what they sell for, and margins on sequencing reagents may be "a little bit more inflated than they really need to be." Illumina knows how to manufacture at scale, but so does Roche, and the chemistry may not be that expensive for Roche.
- **Flow cell reuse:** The expert notes the low price per genome claims depend on reusing the nanopore-type chip about 20 times; "If you just ran it once and threw it away, you would never achieve that price per genome." He would be fine with reuse if validated (a clinical lab would verify by proficiency testing, under CAP, CLIA, or OLA in Ontario, wash the flow cell, and retest). He sees it as "just another step," not "a big impediment."

### Instrument price and runtime
- Roche is said to price its instrument at about $750,000 to $800,000 (the expert "heard"). An equivalent Illumina instrument with extended warranties is "probably double that." At around 300,000 tests a year, capital cost is not material to price per genome.
- The expert's center buys instruments from infrastructure grants and does not amortize them into service prices; hospitals may use capital budgets too. Instruments also have short lifespans ("might be out of date by two or three years").
- **Runtime:** Roche's roughly four-hour run versus about a day for Illumina "doesn't matter one bit" for research or for cost (power is negligible, labor does not change). For a clinician in a stat lab with a sick child, a four-hour versus one- or two-day turnaround "might become extremely important." Research example: a 2,000-person cohort, "one month or three months" does not matter.

## Data quality and analysis

- **What "equivalent" means:** The benchmark is a 30X genome (each position read about 30 times). If a 30X genome from Roche gives confident, real variant calls comparable to Illumina, the researcher is happy; clinicians may want higher accuracy. Differences like 25X versus 30X versus 35X do not matter; needing 100X because of noise would.
- **Signal-to-noise:** The center's sequencing facility manager reviewed SBX and said "Wow, this is really cool." Xpandomers increase signal without raising noise, giving more accurate reads. The expert is "definitely on the enthusiastic side" about the technology, though puzzled how such large molecules fit through enzymes and the pore.
- **Errors:** Random errors are removed by depth of coverage via consensus ("the other 29 say that"). Error-rate debates (including those about PacBio and Oxford Nanopore) are taken "with a grain of salt."
- **Simplex vs duplex:** Roche currently reads in simplex (one direction through a molecule). Illumina, MGI/BGI, Complete Genomics, Element and Ultima read both ends of a fragment (paired, described by the expert as "duplex"). This helps with mapping and assembly. It does not materially change error rates, but allows fewer reads (the expert agreed with the client's example, and corrected the numbers to roughly 25X versus 40X). This is "a computational problem, it's not a biological problem." The expert says this is the one place Roche "may fall down a little," but is not worried: others have solved it, and Roche could instead make longer simplex reads (thousands of bases, like PacBio).
- **Software:** Illumina's DRAGEN is "great" and is based on Broad tools (Genome Analysis Toolkit, SAMtools), optimized, packaged and licensed. Open tools would work on Illumina data. The expert does not know what Roche outputs; he hopes for standard flat files that standard pipelines can analyze, and notes the question is one "not enough people are asking." Past examples exist (microarray era) of vendors not providing tools. He sees no need for Roche to build proprietary tools yet.

## Switching threshold and pricing dynamics

- **Threshold:** The expert would switch at about half the cost: a fully loaded genome around $200 (the transcript says "maybe half" and also "$300 fully loaded instead of somewhere over $400" as a hypothetical; the stated conclusion is that half would do it). At around 75% of Illumina's cost "I don't think so but maybe." Reason: switching means retooling liquid-handling robotics for library prep and retraining staff.
- **Why half works:** Clients could do twice as much work with the same grant (for example NIH or CIHR). "What does scientists want? More data."
- **Competitive pressure:** The center's mission is to provide good research data at the best price; it is publicly funded. A halving in price would not hurt revenue much since clients would simply do more work. But if Montreal, Vancouver or others adopted Roche at half the cost and the center did not, it would be hurt because "scientists are cheap." The expert: "If our competitors bought Roche instruments and they ran at half the cost of our Illumina I think we'd probably go and buy a Roche instrument."
- **Glide path:** Prices fell from about CA$1,000 to somewhat over CA$400 in a couple of years. The expert expects further declines in a year or two, as Illumina reagent costs probably can fall and Illumina is "experiencing significant pressure from everybody talking about Roche and a little bit from the Ultima, which supposedly is much cheaper per genome." Lower Illumina reagent prices "play into our hands happily."
- **How clients choose providers:** Some clients are cost-driven and always go to the cheapest. Most choose on comfort and prior relationships. Jurisdiction also matters (for example the Ontario Ministry of Research and Innovation funds spending expected to stay in-province; same for British Columbia and Quebec). The three Canadian centers work together and prices differ slightly.
