## The lab's scale and workload

- The lab produces roughly 100 whole genomes' worth of sequencing a year, which the expert put at about 10 TB of data per year. The interviewer translated this to roughly 10,000 gigabases (billions of DNA letters read) per year, and the expert confirmed ("Yeah").
- The main focus is single-cell sequencing (reading the DNA/RNA of individual cells) and next-generation sequencing (NGS, the current high-volume, short-read approach). The lab also does vector sequencing, smaller whole-genome work and bulk RNA sequencing. Different sample types each need their own protocols.
- The lab prepares its own libraries (the prepared DNA/RNA fragments loaded onto a sequencer), does its own indexing (barcoding so many samples can be pooled in one run) and does its own data analysis. It can rely on a core facility (a shared institutional sequencing service) for extra capacity.

## Headwinds and tailwinds to sequencing output

### Sample quality and lab operations (direct experience)
- Output depends on the quality of extracted cells (good viability and enough cells), which depends on how well technicians, post-docs and students run extraction protocols. Reagent quality must be "top-notch," or experiments must be repeated or give poor data.
- Sample preparation is a critical, lengthy step before sequencing. The expert said early steps are sometimes overlooked by people from different backgrounds, which affects the whole project.
- More lab members and projects raise output ("more ideas, more data") but also raise variability, because people come from other labs with different experience. The challenge is standardizing protocols so data quality is consistent.

### Funding cuts (the dominant theme)
- Many labs are losing or having grants reduced. The expert says this affects the quantity of sequencing, not quality, and describes "a lot of federal cuts and a lot of also private cuts" affecting all labs, including theirs. There is "a more conscious outlook of how much time it should go towards sequencing."
- The expert's reasoning: more funding lets labs try more projects, and more projects raise the odds that one is a "winning" result. Less money means fewer projects tested.

## Funding mix and Q3 2026 funding trends

- **Funding mix of the lab's sequencing (expert's estimate):** about 40% philanthropic or institutional, about 30% NIH or federal grants, and about 30% (the expert said "33%") pharma companies and other non-governmental entities. The expert called the question "very difficult." The interviewer's restatement (roughly 30% federal, 40% institutional/philanthropic, 30% biopharma and other non-governmental) was confirmed as correct. The shares as spoken add to slightly over 100%, so treat them as approximate.
- **Seed funding.** Money circulating within the university, applied for every other year, helps cover high expenses. The expert counts it as quasi-governmental because institutional money partly comes from states.
- **Effect of cuts on the lab.** Because only about a third is federal, the expert says the effect on output is "limited," since pharma, philanthropic and NGO money continues. The lab must still choose the most promising projects, with an eye to publications that show funders their money is used well.
- **Q3 2026 grant funding vs Q2 2026.** Unchanged ("pretty much stable"). There had been an increase before the prior quarter that helped, and it now looks like it is "stabilizing and... evening up."
- **Forward funding view.** The expert is "pretty sure" the next period will be about 10% less. Funding is fluctuating with the economic and political situation, "the wars and all those things." The expert sees heavy funding attention on AI and in some healthcare areas, which can help single-cell and RNA sequencing.
- **Pharma and biotech-funded work in Q3 2026.** Slightly stronger than expected from philanthropic and pharma sources, but "not statistically significant, in my opinion." These funders are cautious because they expect a return and cannot afford to give money away for free.

## Instruments and workflow

### Fleet (direct experience)
- **In-house:** Illumina NextSeq 500 and 550 (benchtop sequencers).
- **Via core facility:** Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (a high-throughput sequencer). The lab has no immediate access, so it sends pooled libraries and the core loads them.
- **No long-read instruments** (PacBio or Oxford Nanopore) in-house or via the core. Essentially 100% of output is short-read.
- The lab has no NovaSeq X (Illumina's newest high-throughput system) in the lab or core.

### When each instrument is used
- NextSeq 500/550 for urgent projects, such as a paper returned for revision at a journal (the expert named EMBO). It gives data the next day and suits one or two samples. The expert said a high-output cartridge costs about $2,000-$2,500 for about 400 million reads.
- Cost trick: single-cell RNA sequencing needs about 200 million paired reads per sample, and Illumina cartridges carry about 20% extra reads, so one cartridge can cover two samples. That cuts the cost per sample from about $2,000-$2,500 to about $1,000-$1,500. The expert called this "very intriguing" information gathered for the lab.
- NovaSeq 6000 for larger batches (10-15 samples run immediately by the core; sometimes five or six samples). It is cheaper per sample because more samples share a run and fewer cartridges are used, but results can wait about a month. The expert's framing: the NextSeq buys fast data that could win grants (a few thousand dollars spent could lead to a million-dollar grant), while the NovaSeq is for basic research and saving a few thousand dollars.
- Quality check: after the run, the expert checks indexing; a good run has around 89-90% up to about 95% of reads assigned correctly to samples.
- Reagent bench life is about four to six months, so cartridges are used only when needed to avoid expired reagents.
- The expert noted that newer instruments will bring higher precision and quality checks.

### Illumina customer service (direct experience)
- Illumina customer service was very helpful with indexing and reagent issues, and offers free reagent replacement if the fault is Illumina's. Service contractors also diagnose, maintain and fix instruments, at a cost.
- Later in the call: "Illumina has great customer service. They have a great reputation." The expert described them as talkative, candid, open and friendly, and said it is "the best experience customer service-wise." Support levels were unchanged in Q3 2026 versus Q2 2026.

## Short-read vs long-read

- **Current state:** The lab has no interest in long reads (technologies from PacBio and Oxford Nanopore that read very long stretches of DNA, useful for repeats, indels, duplications, transposons and non-coding regions that standard NGS can miss). The expert understands the technology well from the book chapter and says long-read work is impactful in non-coding RNA, which the expert says was long neglected but now shown to silence genes or raise cancer-gene production.
- **Why the lab stays short-read:** Research has focused for years on protein-coding regions (exome sequencing), which the expert says mainly drive drug resistance. Funding and recognition follow established research models, so "it is very hard to move out from a way of thinking to another one." Research in the expert's experience "doesn't favor much non-coding RNA." Single-cell, T-cell receptor (TCR) and spatial transcriptomics work are described as key to understanding immune responses to drugs.
- **12-month outlook:** No change in the read-length mix. Moving to long reads would need a major breakthrough showing non-coding RNA has a revolutionary impact (the expert's example: a Nobel-level immunology finding). The expert put the chance at "maybe... 5%, even less," but is not "hopeless." The expert noted that mRNA vaccines raised general interest in RNA and cancer RNA research funding.

## Competitive landscape

### Vendor share (direct experience for Illumina; second-hand for others)
- Essentially 100% of short-read output is on Illumina. Share did not change in Q3 2026 and the expert expects no vendor to gain or lose share over the next 12 months ("None of them"). The lab has not used Roche, Element Biosciences or Ultima.
- **Why:** The expert said it is "mainly inertia," plus institutional knowledge, publications and reliability. The expert also likes Illumina's software.
- **Switching conditions:** Already owning Illumina instruments means no need to buy a new one (Roche's quoted price is $750,000) unless one breaks beyond insurance replacement. The expert wants to see publications in high-impact journals (above impact factor 12; the expert cited a recent paper at 14) before trusting Roche: "I trust more results than words and promises."

### Roche Axelios 1 (SBX, sequencing by expansion)
- **What the expert read:** Axelios 1 is Roche's new instrument, quoted at $750,000, which would cut human-genome sequencing cost to $150 per genome, which the expert described as 25% less than Illumina per genome. The technology is called SBX.
- **Concerns:** It is very new, has no publications, no track record, and is research-use only, unlike Illumina, which spans research, diagnostic and therapeutic use. The expert noted Roche had "been out of the game for too many years" and has not shown real-world data. The lab and its institutions have not bought it.
- **Throughput comparison:** The expert says NovaSeq X can make about 80 genomes per flow cell run, versus about 16 for Axelios 1, and that Illumina's track record is a major plus even if it costs "a little bit more."
- **Threat level:** The expert called it "minimal, maybe like 25% threat in comparison against Illumina," but said it "could actually potentially reverse the whole thing" if publications appear in a year or two, since publications take time. Roche's possible advantage is fast, small cartridges and smaller, faster batches for projects that need only a few genomes rather than 80. The expert has not adopted it and is only studying it.

## Illumina products and corporate actions

### NovaSeq X
- **Adoption:** None in the lab or core, and no plans to buy. The expert's needs are small (for example about 15 single-cell RNA samples; the expert said 18 genomes is like 40 single-cell RNA samples), so 80-genome capacity is not needed. Larger institutions and core facilities would benefit from the time and cost savings.
- **Roadmap:** The interviewer said Illumina has committed to raise NovaSeq X flow cell output by up to 40% and speed up whole-genome sequencing by about 30%. The expert said this does not change their outlook, mainly because it costs more and because sample accumulation, not instrument capacity, is the bottleneck: the NovaSeq 6000 already takes one or two months to gather 30-40 single-cell samples, and double that would take about four months, so speed matters more. That is why the lab keeps the NextSeq 500/550 for faster data and quicker grants.

### TruPath Genome
- The interviewer said Illumina launched TruPath Genome in February 2026, removing library preparation from the whole-genome workflow at a list price of about $395 per sample. The expert said it gives no incentive to upgrade, because it is useful for epigenetics-type projects the lab does not run.

### SomaLogic acquisition (opinion)
- The interviewer said Illumina closed the SomaLogic deal in January 2026. The expert called it "just a way to avoid the competition with Roche," a defensive diversification into proteins (SomaLogic is a protein company) in case genome sequencing loses value. The expert said SomaLogic is small without much hold, "I don't think it will help much," and it "looks like survival mode." It did not shift any of the lab's work toward Illumina.

## Capital spending, discounts and pricing

- **Instrument plans:** No plan to acquire, replace or upgrade sequencers in the next 12 months ("We are fine with our current setup"). No instrument placement was accelerated, delayed or cancelled in Q3 2026.
- **2026 instrument capital spending vs 2025:** Expected slightly lower, about 10% (the answer was phrased as "sequencing volume"), because of less grant funding.
- **Instrument discounts and incentives, Q3 2026:** First said a 10%-15% decrease, then corrected: discounts were unchanged ("zero").
- **Reagent prices:** The expert said prices have remained about the same, "if not increasing by 10%," and noted vendors typically raise prices about 10% a year but have held flat given weaker demand. The expert's opinion is that vendors try for small increases to show "the market is still good" while "deep down... it's not performing as well as it used to before," referring to Illumina and the sequencing industry broadly.
- **Consumable spending (Q3 2026 vs Q3 2025):** Unchanged. Volume has been the same. Spending is roughly half volume-driven and half cost-driven.
- **Inventory:** The lab cut reagent stocking by about 25% to avoid waste, and describes current inventory as "sufficient."
- **Reagent discounts or commitment terms (Q3 2026 vs Q2):** Unchanged.
- **Lead times for consumables:** Unchanged versus the prior quarter.
- **Vendor support (sales, technical, informatics):** Very good; no change in Q3 2026.

## Technologies and trends the expert is watching

- **Publications from Roche's Axelios 1**, which could change how the expert views Illumina.
- **SomaLogic's protein-based discoveries**, for the same reason.
- **NovaSeq X publications.**

## Key takeaways for tracking Illumina (from this one customer)

- Academic demand is soft but not collapsing: Q3 2026 was flat to down 5-10% year over year for this lab, with the expert expecting 2027 to be 10-15% lower, driven by US federal and private funding cuts.
- Illumina's position in this lab is secure in the near term (100% share, inertia, support, track record), and Roche Axelios 1 is viewed as a limited threat until publications arrive.
- Newer Illumina products (NovaSeq X upgrades, TruPath) did not move this lab, which is small and bottlenecked by sample accumulation. The expert expects larger core facilities to benefit from NovaSeq X.
- Reagent pricing and discounts were flat, and the lab is deliberately running leaner inventory (down about 25%).
