# Natera oncology lifecycle-management leader on emerging sequencing competitors to Illumina, MRD market growth and long-read sequencing

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-22_Associate_Director_at_Natera_Inc._Believes_Emerging_Sequencing_Platforms_Pose_Competitive.md` · Date: 2026-07-22 · AlphaSense expert call (interview 22 Jul 2026, published 24 Jul 2026); expert is a current Associate Director, Oncology Lifecycle Management at Natera (a customer of Illumina); interviewer is an investor-led (buy-side) client · expert call*

**Summary.** A current Natera executive, who is also a physician and former academic researcher, explains why Ultima, Element and Roche are credible challengers to Illumina on cost and flexibility, but argues that switching away from Illumina is very hard for established clinical assays, especially deep-sequencing MRD (minimal residual disease) tests. He expects Illumina to lose share gradually rather than abruptly. He also covers MRD market size and growth, MRD accuracy debates, Roche's new SBX platform (research use only, so far unproven), long-read sequencing use cases and structural variants.

# Expert call notes: Natera executive on competition to Illumina in sequencing and the MRD market

## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

**Who the expert is.** The expert is a physician by training, with years of academic research in molecular oncology and translational medicine (research that moves lab findings toward patient care). They worked at Johns Hopkins and Ohio State, in a lab studying immunotherapy, RNA and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA, tumor DNA fragments floating in the blood). [Expert: Natera associate director, 22 Jul 2026]

**Current role.** The expert has spent the last four and a half years at Natera, which they describe as "the leader in ctDNA." They started as translational lead for gastrointestinal (GI) and genitourinary (GU) cancers, feeding into clinical trials, data analysis and strategy. They now hold a leadership position covering strategy for clinical trials and commercial success in GI cancers, including new test launches, integrating technology and bringing tests to market. The AlphaSense bio gives the title as Associate Director, Oncology Lifecycle Management at Natera, reporting to the Senior Director of Oncology. The bio says the role has P&L (profit-and-loss) responsibility for specific product segments and a focus on MRD (minimal residual disease, testing for leftover cancer after treatment). The bio says they previously held a Senior Manager role at Natera, which ended in August 2025 (a promotion within the company). The expert also spent six months at a Boston start-up before joining Natera.

**Relationship to Illumina.** The expert is a customer-side user. They have used Illumina platforms in academia and now at Natera. They say Natera also uses aspects of Element Biosciences for ctDNA. Their sequencing experience with Element comes from the Boston start-up, which ran Element's benchtop machine. The call is labelled "Customer" perspective, with an investor-led (buy-side) interviewer.

**Recency and direct versus speculative knowledge.** The experience is current (a sitting Natera employee). The expert speaks from direct experience on MRD, Natera's use of Illumina and Element, and clinician discussions ("hundreds and hundreds of physicians every year"). They speak from reading and conference attendance (not hands-on use) on Roche's new platform and on Ultima. They are openly "biased for MRD" on deep-sequencing needs. They say they are "not taking sides" between Natera and Guardant. They know little about Illumina's diversification: they had to be prompted on Illumina's single-cell acquisition and referred loosely to the "Q1 call."

## Overall view on Illumina's competitive position

- For "20+ years" there was no serious rival in short-read sequencing (reading short DNA fragments; the most common, high-throughput method). "This recently has changed. It has changed a lot." [Expert: Natera associate director, Jul 2026]
- The expert names the main challengers as Ultima Genomics (because of cost), Element (because of its VITARI platform, which they say is "almost equal with NovaSeq X," Illumina's newest high-throughput machine) and Roche, whose "AXELIOS" (as transcribed; they also call it the new nanopore-based platform) they see as "the main disruptor." The expert says Illumina will "start really, really feeling it very soon with Roche," and notes Roche's deep knowledge of diagnostics and lab equipment.
- Evidence of impact so far: the expert says competitors "haven't infiltrated so much." As a sign, they cite that "last year," after many years, Illumina had a 2%-3% loss in total revenue (a figure from the expert's recollection, not verified in the document).
- Bottom line: Illumina's future is "good," it will remain dominant, and it will lose share, but gradually. "It's not going to be in six months, they will lose 30%." The loss will be gradual, giving Illumina time to catch up. Illumina "adopted first and quickly," and big players have built their success on its platforms. The expert says "maybe something super disruptive will come up in two years, something AI-based," but "as of now... the barriers to bypass, to change Illumina, are very, very high."

## Ultima Genomics

- **Capability.** Ultima can now do whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing (WGS, the full genome; WES, only protein-coding regions) with "relatively high quality." The expert has seen some results and presentations that were "not as good."
- **Price.** The expert says the cost is "$80-$110" per sample for applications that do not need great depth, such as oncology, fetal health and women's health. They see an opportunity with smaller labs, local labs and clinical uses such as transplant sequencing. For these large markets, they say you do not need the depth and cost of Illumina, so Illumina "will start losing some market share."
- **Solaris chemistry.** This is the product the expert likes most. It needs about 50% less DNA input and lowers cost by 30%. Cost per million reads is "somewhere below $0.40," which supports what the expert called "somewhere $50 genome." The expert explains that Ultima found a way to amplify specific DNA regions without needing so much input, which matters because clinical samples are limited. They told the interviewer to look this up, and the figures are their recollection of a recent review.
- **Product line.** Three products, from cheapest to most expensive: the UG 100, the Solaris panel and the UG200. The expert says the UG200 is "comparable with Illumina" in price, so "I don't think that there's any reason" to use it. Solaris is "very, very promising."
- **Interviewer's summary, confirmed by the expert.** For many applications the result needs to be "good enough," not top of the line, at roughly 30% of the cost with smaller samples. [Expert: "Exactly."]

## Where and when switching away from Illumina happens

- **New versus established.** A new company can integrate a new technology far more easily than an existing large genomics company can switch. A big company will not "throw away all the Illumina platforms." Expanding on the same Illumina platform makes operational sense and preserves internal calibration and validation. Firms do not want the same commercial product run on two different sequencers, and "FDA is not going to love it."
- **Where diversification happens.** Companies that diversify their assays and want a new product that does not need deep sequencing are a good use case. Likely switchers are new applications, diversified portfolios, start-ups, academic labs, new labs and local labs, where "the barrier to entry... is going to be way lower." Start-ups would have a strong incentive to pick something cheaper that is "good enough." The interviewer's restatement, confirmed by the expert: a company building a new panel could start with Ultima and validate from scratch, while an established, validated, clinically applied test faces a much bigger barrier.
- **Used Illumina machines.** The expert notes that many Illumina machines can be bought in the aftermarket from closed labs. Several people told the expert they would still prefer an Illumina machine even after "five, seven years" of use elsewhere, because of the brand and the cheap secondhand supply. This is another source of Illumina stickiness against cheaper newcomers.

## Element Biosciences

- **VITARI (high throughput).** The expert says the machine costs about $700,000, against "more than $1 million" for the NovaSeq X. It has "pretty good baseline coverage" starting at 50X (each position read about 50 times) on a genome for "somewhere over $70." At 50X you can do women's health, transplant, infectious disease, large-scale whole genome, patient screening and cancer screening, which covers "the majority of the oncology customers," but you "cannot run Signatera or ctDNA with 50X." Signatera is Natera's MRD test.
- **AVITI (benchtop).** A small sequencer suitable for academic labs and small facilities, costing "somewhere over $100 per genome." It can also run single-cell sequencing. The expert compares it to "having an espresso in your house." They worked with it at a Boston start-up for six months and were "extremely satisfied": "You could run basically anything."
- **Limits.** Element cannot do comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP, broad tumor-tissue mutation testing) or MRD. CGP "usually needs 150X or deeper, plus you need RNA sequencing." Tissue-informed MRD tests usually recommend more than 80X or 120X. The 30X-50X range, depending on machine, is not suitable.
- **What the shallow depth is fine for.** The interviewer's framing, confirmed: applications like prenatal screening (NIPT, non-invasive prenatal testing), transplant and infectious disease use preset DNA probes that go "super, super targeted," like a GPS, so more than 30X is "an overkill." MRD, by contrast, is a "shotgun approach," seeking the "tiny fish in a big ocean," and wants to be as deep as possible. "It's a different technology."
- **In MRD.** Element is not used by the big MRD players. "The big four, plus Natera, they're just using Illumina" in the U.S. The expert does not know what very small European labs do. (Earlier in the call they said Natera uses aspects of Element for ctDNA, which is a loose inconsistency within the transcript.)

## Why MRD players stay on Illumina

- The expert says the main reason is the barrier to switching, not just quality. MRD-guided trials change treatment based on an MRD result and "take five to seven years," so "you cannot switch an assay or a sequencer middle of a trial. It's a no-no." "Maybe this will change."
- **Reimbursement and geography.** Reimbursement is mainly through Medicare, "a very small component," with private reimbursement limited. No company has reimbursement in Europe. In Japan, only Natera has a PMDA (the Japanese regulator's) approval. In Europe, local labs "can do whatever they would like," which gives new sequencing companies an opening to run MRD tests off Illumina.

## MRD and diagnostics market size and economics

- **MRD market.** About $5 billion now, expected to reach $30 billion by 2030. Consulting reports project $30 billion-$50 billion. Barriers include adoption, reimbursement, cost and clinical utility, because many physicians "are using MRD, but they don't know what to do with it" after a positive result. The expert says they face this daily at Natera.
- **Other testing markets.** NIPT, transplant, infectious disease and regional-lab panels (viruses, cancer screening panels) together are "more than $40 billion right now." The expert says MRD will be "equal with everything" in five years.
- **Consolidation.** Many big companies are acquiring smaller MRD firms. The expert cites "Tempus just bought Personalis two days ago" (i.e., about 20 Jul 2026, per the expert) as one example.
- **Growth drivers.** Physicians and patients are asking for MRD, Medicare and private payers are reimbursing more tests and indications, accumulating data, and falling testing cost.
- **Margins.** As sequencing gets cheaper and better, margins "automatically will increase." Many companies are knowingly eating high upfront operating costs and marginal costs per test. They accept this because evidence generation and the FDA and NCCN (a US cancer treatment guideline body) attitudes toward MRD are changing, and "if you leave early, you are missing out long term."
- **First actionable use.** The interviewer notes the first actionable MRD proof of concept in muscle-invasive bladder cancer (MIBC); the expert confirms this refers to the IMvigor trial.

## MRD accuracy debates and gold standards

- The interviewer relayed reports from oncologists who ran two companies' MRD tests on the same patient and got different results (one suspected a false negative). The expert pushes back: single-patient (N-of-1) anecdotes "not science." They rely on large datasets.
- **Evidence cited.** Guardant ran "two huge studies, 2,000 patients each from Japan" with three-monthly ctDNA testing in colorectal cancer along with imaging, "quality of evidence 1b." Both Guardant and Natera showed sensitivity in the "high 80s percent or 90%" and specificity "more than 95%." The expert says they are "not taking sides" and would be skeptical of a 10-patient case series.
- **Biology explains many misses.** Lung lesions, brain metastases and peritoneal disease do not shed ctDNA into the blood, so negatives there are expected ("That's biology and physiology"). The expert says they are "more skeptical on how the physicians are using the test rather than the actual performance."
- **Technology trend.** More companies are moving to whole-genome rather than whole-exome sequencing, which the expert believes is "the future" and which should eliminate many false negatives in patients who stay negative then recur.
- **Different philosophies, consistent data.** Signatera is tissue-informed (built from the patient's tumor), while Guardant is tissue-uninformed, but "the data are very consistent for all the companies."
- **Gold standards.** For early cancer detection (GRAIL, Cologuard, Exact), the standard is colonoscopy, with the expert claiming "100% sensitivity and 99.9% specificity." Blood tests help triage toward colonoscopy and can find advanced disease at higher rates, catching colon cancer at stage II or IB instead of IIIC. For MRD, the NCCN standard is imaging (CT for colon, MRI for liver, ultrasound for breast), but imaging is imperfect. The expert states CT in colon has about 70% sensitivity.
- **ASCO guidance.** The expert reads ASCO's ctDNA guidance as saying ctDNA should never be used alone for decisions. It can be used for (1) liquid CGP to find mutations, (2) as part of clinical trials, and (3) when imaging and findings are ambiguous, which the expert thinks matters most. They call it complementary to clinical suspicion.

## Roche SBX

- **Expert's stance.** The expert read about it and attended AGBT (the genomics conference) where it was launched. In principle it is "very promising" and "very innovative." It "goes very deep," has nanopore-like capability for long sequencing "which is great for MRD," and the price is similar at $750,000. They are "not fully convinced that it will radically change the market."
- **Reasons for doubt.**
  - Illumina has been in the market for years. For deep-sequencing precision oncology, there are many alternative reagents and library-prep options (preparing DNA for sequencing) that cut costs on Illumina without compromising performance.
  - SBX launched at the end of June (less than a month before the call). A company would "have to go all in," and the expert doesn't know how it works at scale.
  - Roche claims consumables can be reused up to 20 times or up to three months after first use. The expert doubts a commercial MRD company would reuse something before testing it. (Interviewer notes others say the reusable cell takes longer per sample and loses capacity.)
  - Roche touts a four-hour turnaround for deep sequencing but with 150 ng of DNA, which the expert says is "nearly impossible from a real patient." Interviewer notes sample size is unclear versus Illumina. Expert agrees there are "inconsistencies": fast sequencing doesn't help if it needs "tons of DNA."
  - Launched as research use only (RUO), not for CLIA (US clinical lab certification) or commercial labs. The expert doesn't know CLIA plans but expects some "in the very near future," which would be a good test. For now it's for research and clinical trials.
- **Roche's leverage.** Roche has the name but "people are not going to mess with their diagnostics that much in order to experiment." If you run a "multi-billion-dollar business on sequencing," you won't change because of a name: "You need to prove it first." Barriers to entry remain "very, very high."

## Long-read sequencing

- **Illumina's position.** Illumina is short-read focused; the expert says "long-read, they're not part of it, basically." Oxford Nanopore and PacBio are the long-read players; Roche's SBX can also play in long reads, with principles like Oxford Nanopore's, and could apply to single-cell RNA sequencing and spatial genomics.
- **Size.** The interviewer's figure is that long-read is maybe 10% of sequencing; the expert did not give a number, but said applications are "more limited" and "specialized." Commercially limited, but "very important research-wise." One growing application is chronic kidney disease (a smaller market).
- **Use cases the expert listed.**
  - Tumor viral sequencing (tandem repeats): HPV, HCV, some COVID use.
  - Repeat expansion disorders such as Huntington's disease, Fragile X, Friedreich's ataxia (small prevalence).
  - Structural variants in MRD. Long reads are better, as short reads miss "almost 1/3" of structural variants. MRD applications are limited so far, so adoption is not wide.
  - Complex or homologous loci such as HLA typing for transplant and autoimmune disease.
  - Fusion genes and oncogenic rearrangements (glioblastoma, liver, cholangiocarcinoma, pancreas, gastric), which short reads miss. Long reads could detect both DNA and RNA features. An ideal future could combine CGP and MRD in a single long-read test, but that is "in the very distant future."
- **Adoption obstacle.** "Because something in theory could be used, doesn't mean that commercially and operationally, it makes sense." Big MRD companies are reluctant because it is "pretty expensive." Some suggest long reads are more sensitive for MRD, "but nobody is brave enough to use that."

## Structural variants, fragmentomics and related companies

- **Explanation.** The expert describes the idea as multiple variants (SNVs, single-letter DNA changes) within 50 base pairs that tend to occur together. Seeing them together is a cancer-specific signal rather than noise, which raises MRD confidence. (The expert labels these "structural variants," though the description matches closely clustered variants; the notes record it as spoken.) Many players now claim this boosts sensitivity versus whole-exome or whole-genome without it.
- **Foresight Diagnostics.** A Stanford start-up that developed CAPP-Seq and was strong in structural variants. The expert says Natera acquired it "on November" (presumably Nov 2025). The expert believes "the future is there."
- **SAGA (acquired by Roche, per the interviewer).** The expert was "not convinced" by its data shown at ASCO GI this year for colorectal cancer, which "were not better than Signatera or Guardant" (those using methylation or whole-exome), though they don't know why its cohort underperformed.
- **Illumina still dominant here.** People still use NovaSeq to detect structural variants. Nobody will "switch completely your whole army of labs" to find the extra third, especially with a large commercial presence.

Time ran short, so the topic was barely covered. The expert says Illumina is entering spatial biology and single cell (the interviewer says via the acquisition of Fluent), but "the presence is not great" and it is something they "will definitely have to do." On the Q1 call (as the expert recalled it), Illumina leaned into AI, multiomics and drug discovery, with AI-based launches and products for pharma development. The expert notes "everybody is" pushing AI. [Expert: Natera associate director, Jul 2026]
