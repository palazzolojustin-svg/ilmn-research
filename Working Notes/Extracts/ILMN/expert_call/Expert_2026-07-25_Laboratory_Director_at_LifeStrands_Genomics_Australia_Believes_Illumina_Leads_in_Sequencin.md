# Expert call – Laboratory Director at LifeStrands Genomics (Australia), an Illumina customer, on Illumina's tech leadership, NovaSeq X, liquid biopsy, switching costs and clinical-integration gaps

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-25_Laboratory_Director_at_LifeStrands_Genomics_Australia_Believes_Illumina_Leads_in_Sequencin.md` · Date: 2026-07-25 · AlphaSense expert call (published 29 Jul 2026; interview 25 Jul 2026); expert is a Laboratory Director at LifeStrands Genomics Australia (customer perspective; investor-led/buy-side client, unnamed) · expert call*

**Summary.** A laboratory director at a small, agile Australian precision-oncology lab, who is an Illumina customer, says Illumina dominates sequencing (about 95% of the market, in the expert's words) thanks to reliable technology, integrated software and strong customer support. The expert calls NovaSeq X a "game changer" but says Illumina lacks clinical expertise and is behind companies such as Natera in liquid biopsy. Switching away is costly (validation of $100,000-$200,000 or more), and competitors (Roche, Thermo Fisher, MGI, Ultima, Element) lag on integrated software. Sample preparation is the biggest remaining bottleneck, and blood-tube logistics and hemolysis could limit larger liquid-biopsy tests.

# Expert call notes: Laboratory Director, LifeStrands Genomics Australia (interview 25 Jul 2026)

## 1. Expert background and relationship to Illumina

- **Role.** The expert is a Laboratory Director at LifeStrands Genomics Australia. They are responsible for scientific oversight of the organization's clinical pathology lab, including administration of technical and scientific operations, supervising standard operating procedures (SOPs) and reporting scientific findings. Previously they were Laboratory Director and Technical Director at XING Technologies, an Australian biotechnology company focused on diagnosis and monitoring technologies for cancer and disease.
- **Earlier career.** Before XING they were Director of Research at BGI Australia (BGI Group), leaving in May 2019. BGI Group is a competitor of Illumina. In that role they oversaw research initiatives, strategic direction and efficiency efforts. They also spent many years in academia.
- **Experience.** About 15 years with Applied Biosystems, Illumina, MGI, and Ion Torrent/Thermo Fisher, with familiarity with Oxford Nanopore (ONT) and Roche's AXELIOS. Most experience is with short-read next-generation sequencing (NGS, technology that reads many short DNA fragments in parallel). Background is mainly bioinformatics (computational analysis of biological data); the expert describes themselves now as "a general scientist at a leadership level".
- **Current Illumina products used.** MiniSeq, NextSeq 550, NextSeq 2000, a DRAGEN server (Illumina's hardware/software for fast data analysis), and BaseSpace ICI/ICA (Illumina's cloud informatics software).
- **Relationship.** Direct customer of Illumina, with firsthand experience of the products. The expert has spent the last six or seven years in private diagnostic labs specializing in precision oncology (cancer-specific testing), and has worked in several labs, so can compare how different labs operate. Their current lab is small and innovative; clinicians call asking for new tests ("I really need this thing. Can you guys please offer it?"), and the lab builds a business case and usually offers it, or refers ("reflexes") the sample to another lab.
- **Recency.** Current, active user as of July 2026. Some knowledge of Illumina's newest products (MRD test, TruPath) is secondhand: the expert says they know the MRD test only "from what I hear anyway, from the public materials".
- **Perspective.** Customer; the interviewer is a buy-side (investor-side) client.

## 2. Illumina's market position and value

- The expert says Illumina has "something like 95% of the market" in sequencing (their estimate, not a sourced figure; a loose recollection, as signaled by "something like").
- Reasons the expert's lab does a lot of business with Illumina: "very solid products and very good customer support, so they're always reliable." Illumina also constantly offers new products and seeks early adopters and "evangelists"; such offers reach the expert often.
- Illumina sees value in partnering with agile labs like the expert's, which can implement and give feedback on new technology.
- Value is "huge" and "very general": Illumina offers applications across many fields.
- Expert's overall verdict: "At the moment, Illumina, for me, is always going to win because they take care of the customer pretty much throughout the whole testing process."

## 3. Liquid biopsy and the clinical-understanding gap

Liquid biopsy means testing a blood sample for tumor DNA, so cancer can be assessed without a tissue biopsy.

- Expert view: Liquid biopsy has become a big market item for Illumina, but Illumina is "a little bit behind the ball" compared with companies that are heavily active in the space.
- Illumina is "a technology company": good at sequencing machines and kits, but spends little time in clinical settings and "doesn't always have the best understanding of the clinical needs."
- Companies such as Natera rely on tech companies like Illumina for the technology but have clinical expertise and can deliver results directly to patients.
- Illumina "has amazing tech for this" but is "struggling a little bit" on clinical understanding. It seems to be putting in a lot of effort now, watching what others do and trying to build competitive options.
- Illumina has no clinical lab of its own and must rely on lab partners good enough to implement the technology and report whether it works and how to improve it.
- The expert's own lab started liquid biopsy because clinicians asked for it and "no one else has really been doing it or doing a good job." Demand typically comes from a new drug lacking a liquid-biopsy test for its predictive biomarkers, or from an upcoming clinical trial needing patients enrolled. It is "very focused on very new and very actionable information."

### Upcoming/current Illumina liquid biopsy products (expert's view)
- **ctTSO 500 DNA test** (available now): not whole genome; a large gene panel for liquid biopsy. "Seems to be pretty good." It generates a lot of data and people initially struggled because the software solution was poor; it now seems "a bit more integrated and seamless."
- **MRD test** (minimal residual disease, i.e. detecting tiny amounts of remaining cancer after treatment), planned on NovaSeq X. The expert says from public materials that Illumina has made it very sensitive, which is attractive, and expects costs "significantly better than what's out there right now." This is secondhand.

## 4. Decentralized versus centralized testing model

- Expert's view: Foundation Medicine and Natera rely on a central lab to process samples. Illumina is trying to decentralize, letting individual labs test in-house.
- The expert believes decentralization "is going to be the winner" because it is better for patients and makes diagnostic testing more sustainable; samples are processed faster, avoid shipping to somewhere in the U.S. (especially for non-US labs), cost less, and create competition among local labs that keeps costs low. Cheaper sequencing from NovaSeq throughput helps. This is an opinion/prediction.
- **Caveat on collection kits.** U.S. players with big share (Natera, Guardant, Exact) run centralized labs and supply proprietary blood-collection kits. For decentralized liquid biopsy, either blood collection must become generic and purchasable from any supplier, or kits must be supplied worldwide, which the expert thinks is "a bit prohibitive."

## 5. NovaSeq X and platform economics

- Interviewer framing: NovaSeq X lowers cost per genome; Illumina needs labs to scale testing volume to offset lower per-sample prices and drive long-term consumables (reagent kits needed for each run). The expert agreed ("Absolutely") and said "the NovaSeq X is a game changer."
- Expert's reasons: Illumina has developed kits that work well on it, and put effort into making software "easy and accessible"; it is "a one-stop integrated solution from putting a sample in to getting useful data out." Customers still need a clinical partner to validate and implement tests.
- The interviewer described NovaSeq X as the flagship ultra-high-throughput system and top of the hardware line; the expert confirmed ("Yes").
- **Multiplexing.** Samples can be pooled and run together on one flow cell (the consumable slide where sequencing occurs). Though built for huge volume (whole genome, very high-coverage liquid biopsy), it can suit small-test labs because many small tests can share one flow cell. Past hesitation to adopt big sequencers was due to not knowing how to use them for tiny tests; understanding is increasing and Illumina is getting better at helping. The expert does not know how well Ultima and Element sequencers multiplex.
- **Adoption in Australia.** Expert believes NovaSeq X was introduced "last year" (2025; the expert says "I think"). It is expensive, so not many Australian companies have bought one yet; the expert assumes it is "all over the U.S." You must be able to fill flow cells or it is a costly idle machine. Cheaper sequencing and more clinical applications make the business case easier. Smaller labs have avoided it partly because they were unsure how to use capacity efficiently for small panels (expert agreed).
- **Speed.** No analogy offered beyond "bigger, faster, cheaper." Previous sequencers are slower. Roche's AXELIOS is faster; Illumina is "still a little bit slower than that machine." But speed matters less: a good clinical product taking eight more hours than a competitor "is not really going to kill anyone." The expert is "happy enough" and doesn't need it significantly faster.
- **Improvement priorities.** Accuracy is already adequate. Speed is always helpful. The biggest improvement would be reducing sample preparation.

## 6. Sample preparation bottleneck and TruPath

- Expert: sample handling and library preparation (converting extracted DNA into a form the sequencer can read) is where labor, cost and risk of sample mix-ups arise. Illumina "haven't spent a lot of time" here; some companies are looking at it. It would require an automated handler ahead of the sequencer.
- **Thermo Fisher Genexus:** an almost end-to-end workflow; after extracting nucleic acids, you load them and it does the rest through to data interpretation. "It's complicated, and sometimes it falls over." Illumina hasn't introduced this; no other big sequencing company is close. MGI has tried, but the expert has little experience and says it sounds "not terribly well-integrated."
- Interviewer summary (expert agreed): Illumina lacks an end-to-end automated sample-to-report system, so sample prep remains a major labor-cost and error bottleneck.
- **TruPath** (new Illumina technology; expert only recently heard of it): currently for whole genome sequencing only. DNA is still extracted, but placed directly on the flow cell with no library prep and no liquid handling. "That's actually huge. I'm super-excited about that."
- Limitation: it relies on large DNA fragments, so needs fresh tissue or blood. Diagnostics normally use formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded (FFPE) tissue, which fragments DNA and makes it unsuitable. Possibly workarounds exist. Blood would be a good use.

## 7. Competitive comparison and software integration

- Illumina wins because of end-to-end customer care and integrated kits, machines and software. Lacking automated liquid handling is acceptable because others can supply it.
- **Software lock-in:** much analysis software is specific to each sequencing technology and company. Introducing a non-Illumina sequencer means finding software for it, "a huge headache." The expert did this personally ("it was terrible. I really regret doing it") and went back to Illumina. (Direct experience; the expert does not name the vendor.)
- Expert: Illumina is "hugely ahead" on integrated solutions; other companies are not spending much time on it.
- **Roche:** the sequencer "sounds really good" but has no good software package for an integrated solution; this "is going to hamstring them a little bit," leaving them "stuck in research labs" rather than reaching clinical use. (Opinion, based on expert's impressions.)
- **How far behind:** "a high mountain to climb." Roche took 10 years to get its sequencer out. Good software for all use cases (agriculture, population studies, etc.) takes many people and time. Expert thinks it will be "a couple of years before others can catch up." Caveat: AI makes writing software easier, so it might be less of a bottleneck. Speculative.
- **Competitor outreach:** competitors contact the expert "all the time." The expert was more interested in the past, but now with NovaSeq, falling prices and faster sequencing, "it's really hard to say no" to Illumina. The expert's lab is small but willing to try things. Smaller labs might prefer Genexus ("just put it in the corner and let it go"), whereas Illumina "still needs a little bit of handling."

### Amplicon versus hybrid capture
- **Amplicon sequencing:** you must know in advance what to look for and design the test for only those targets. Very sensitive and specific; finds the biomarker if present.
- **Hybrid capture:** you select genes with probes, not specific mutations; e.g. a gene fusion can be found by including only one partner gene. More flexible and better for discovery, but loses some sensitivity and is probably a bit more expensive. Expert: "I would always want hybrid capture if I can," calling it generally a superior technology; Illumina is "getting past that sensitivity block."
- Genexus can only do amplicon sequencing, which limits testing; Illumina does both.
- **Expert's lab example:** its liquid biopsy test is amplicon-based, a small panel of actionable hotspot mutations (targets for therapies). A good fit: sensitive and clear-cut.
- Hybrid-capture liquid biopsy needs a minimum number of probes, so it must target many genes, becoming larger, more expensive and less sensitive at very low levels. It suits comprehensive testing. NovaSeq X helps, but "to date, a hybrid capture-based liquid biopsy test on Illumina is prohibitively expensive and is really only offered for research." (No dollar figure given.)

## 8. Switching costs

- Switching is hard if you want only one sequencing technology; it is easier to run a second technology side by side and then retire the first.
- Each switch brings a huge validation cost: depending on test complexity, "$100,000, $200,000 just to test out the new technology and make sure that it is working." Larger sequencers and tests cost more. "It definitely pays to find the technology you want to use and stick with it for quite a while."
- The expert has not found flaws in Illumina over time ("I haven't experienced that with Illumina, but maybe with other companies. That's mainly the software analysis component.").
- The expert has discovered unintended uses and tweaks of Illumina technology (almost patented one), but experimental follow-through is costly and outside a clinical lab's purview.

## 9. AI

- **Regulation (Australia):** the TGA (Therapeutic Goods Administration, comparable to the U.S. FDA) is "very AI-averse"; the lab must do extensive testing to show AI is not used in its clinical tests, as it is "forbidden, effectively." AI can be used in precursors to testing. The expert personally uses AI (Claude, installed locally) nearly daily to write code.
- **Illumina:** the expert imagines Illumina's developers use AI to build analysis code but doesn't know and doesn't think Illumina would say. Illumina has "a very good software development team," so AI could be a game-changer by speeding new software and analytical methods. AI will be behind the scenes, not in the end product. (Speculation.)
- **Data advantage:** not on a large scale; Illumina sometimes asks customers to share small datasets but doesn't benefit from discoveries. It likely benefited from GRAIL, where over one million people were tested, a rich dataset but specific to blood methylation testing. "As we've all heard, Grail didn't really deliver on what they were offering." The expert thinks Illumina has access to that data.

## 10. Liquid biopsy sample logistics: a potential weakness for Illumina

- The expert's lab uses commercially available Streck blood-collection tubes (blood-preserving tubes). Cost: $26 per tube; two to four tubes per test. Making kits and sending them to patients is "a little more expensive and logistically complicated than I expected."
- Returning tubes has been "even a bigger disaster": tubes must be full or blood shakes, causing hemolysis (rupture of blood cells), which releases normal human DNA that contaminates the sample and dilutes circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA) signal.
- For the lab's small amplicon tests, this is manageable. For larger tests such as Illumina's ctTSO 500 and MRD test, and whole-genome breadth-based tests, hemolysis would be "a big problem." Cancer patients often can't fill the tubes. The expert doesn't know how large companies avoid this (perhaps better stabilization).
- Expert view: "I'm not sure that Illumina has thought about that yet." Lack of a regulated sample-input method, leaving labs to devise their own, "could be a bit of a weakness for them." (Opinion/speculation; the expert says "I don't think they've thought about that.")

## 11. Summary of expert sentiment (AlphaSense AI summary and transcript)

- Illumina is a technology leader with strong products and customer support but lags some competitors in clinical understanding.
- Decentralized testing is ultimately superior, but needs strong clinical partnerships.
- Integrated software and flexibility create high switching costs and make competitor adoption challenging.
- Remaining barriers: NovaSeq X's high cost and need for high utilization (smaller labs), sample prep automation, and liquid biopsy sample logistics.
