The 1.5B is used for 2x300 runs and occasional rapid-turnaround projects; the lab mostly runs 10B and 25B. The expert expects next year to say they mostly run 10B (stated as "15Bs" in the transcript, likely a transcription error) and 35B.

- Expected effect: "there will be a dip and then it will continue to go back up." Existing projects shrink (for example needing 2 lanes rather than 5) but, for the same price, new projects grow into the bigger flow cells.
- DRAGEN use is expanding. The lab has growing 5-base projects (Illumina's method that reads DNA bases plus methylation, a chemical mark on DNA) and does analysis for these. Offering analysis at no charge adds appeal to sequencing customers. The lab is starting to charge for 5-base analysis because it is charged for it; basic items such as RNA alignments are not charged.
- 5-base does not cannibalize PacBio's Revio, which can detect methylation natively: 5-base projects are 50, 100 or 300 samples, which the expert says would not go on Revio now.

## Single-cell and spatial (10x Genomics, Visium, Xenium, Atera)

Plant single-cell is growing: the lab has now done three plant projects (one tried at the time of the last call), including one rushed because of a grant deadline and another from a repeat user whose results were not yet in but "so far everything looks good."

- Spatial (technology that records where in a tissue gene activity occurs) work is consistent, fed by the sister core's Visium instrument, which supplies libraries to this lab regularly. 10x released rat probes (the lab got a rat project) and HD PolyA (the lab has HD PolyA projects). Spatial project volume has been "fairly steady" over a couple of years with a slight uptick.

The one being worked on is a plant, for which the hurried plant single-cell project generated data for probe design. Xenium utilization is "low right now" and "the machine is not the limiting factor." In situ platforms generate images rather than needing Illumina sequencing.

Cell-resolution data is something "nobody would turn down," and bioinformatics development for cell resolution on Visium data may help.

### 10x Atera

- The expert called Atera (10x's new whole-transcriptome in situ instrument) "an interesting machine" likely to be "very well adopted by places that have a large human demand and mouse probably coming right around the corner." For a lab with many non-model species it is "a bit limiting"; "any system that limits to human sort of makes us glaze over." The lab's species include honeybees, flies, cows, chickens and plants.
- It could cannibalize Xenium or other business if it expands. For a new instrument to come to the lab it must replace everything an existing instrument does, which Atera cannot (it does only part of what Visium does); the lab runs Visium HD, Visium HD FFPE, PolyA and all kits, and likes 10x for that breadth. The lab already has a Xenium.
- The expert has not heard UIC or Rush planning to add it. They note "10x Genomics develops things fast" and are interested in capturing as much as they can with additional probe sets. The lab is watching.
- Sequencing-impact view: the expert sees a progression ("this then that") in which projects do single-cell or Visium first and later move to targeted systems. Shifting whole-transcriptome work to Atera might begin to produce a significant shift "one or two years down the road" and "that might be optimistic." They do not see a huge shift off sequencing now, and projects are only rarely deemed better for Xenium.

### Illumina StrataMap

- StrataMap (Illumina's new spatial product) is being evaluated. One project is in discussion on campus, and a very large project from USDA off campus will come for the sequencing part. Slide and cDNA handling would be done by the sister core, after which cDNA comes to this lab for final libraries and sequencing.
- Features: a giant slide of about 7.5 cm, which requires a full 25B flow cell per slide, so projects need enough tissue and size. Samples can be divided into many smaller sections; the first project is a fish, with many fish larvae on the slide (it uses polyA capture).
- Positives: significantly larger field for people with many samples or large sections; priced "fairly low for what you get" on the spatial side.
- Negatives: sequencing cost is higher, needing about 4 times the sequencing per spot; a single, shorter read of 80-90 base pairs versus the 150-nucleotide cDNA read possible on PolyA Visium, which many non-model users want.
- Competition: it will compete where large tissue coverage is needed. Whether it takes share from 10x: "If it works really well, yes, it will. If it works like the fluent single-cell, it won't." The lab plans to report back later in the year.
- Multiomics link: StrataMap and Visium multiomics would increase sequencing demand; in situ platforms would not unless projects move from sequencing-based to in situ methods.

## PacBio (Revio) and long reads

- Utilization is rising on both Revio (PacBio's long-read sequencer) and NovaSeq X, more than last year, driven mainly by samples per project.
- Revio growth areas: "huge uptake" in bacterial metagenomic samples; many de novo assembly projects (building genomes from scratch), paired with Hi-C (chromosome-contact mapping); an internal discussion that day about moving Hi-C to PacBio's Sci-Fi library prep; and amplicons (targeted DNA fragments) via Kinnex kits. Fewer one- or two-amplicon projects remain on Illumina because full-length 16S and full-length ITS (marker genes for microbes and fungi) can be sequenced on PacBio and "people are going to do that." Multi-target work on the Standard BioTools (Fluidigm) system is also strong, with five projects in-house.
- Expected volume: "I would not be surprised if we do 50% more SMRT cells over this next year than we did last year." (A SMRT cell is PacBio's consumable sequencing cell.)
- Bottleneck: library construction. When the lab switched from Sequel II to Revio, run time fell from 2 weeks to 2 days, making library prep hard to keep up with. Quicker amplicon and bacterial preps and multi-cell projects help; another person moves in and out of PacBio to supplement. The lab joked it may need another Revio, and the balance "could flip."
- The lab is still a PacBio CSP (certified service provider).
- SPRQ-Nx chemistry (PacBio's newer, lower-cost chemistry): it lowers cost "a little bit," roughly $200 less, but is "not as massive a wow" as hoped; logistically challenging as the same project needs 3 SMRT cells that must be loaded right away. Most projects need one or even less than one SMRT cell, maybe two. A few large projects now too costly may shift to PacBio, but not a huge shift. Lower prices tend to make users do more (e.g., add two libraries and an extra SMRT cell).
- Sub-$300 genome claims: "not a reality." The expert says the company's website has an asterisk saying sequencing reagent cost only, excluding labor, service contracts, library prep, instrument cost and maintenance. It is "a pinhole look." The expert's example: a human genome might cost about $3,000 each, perhaps about $800 each when several are put on one SMRT cell on the new chemistry, "a 20% discount" (the expert's wording; the figures as stated are unclear). The expert would not use it on smaller projects.
- Versus Illumina: Illumina's synthetic long-read approach (ILCR) "never really took off here"; PacBio offers "better, cheaper, longer reads." What moved to PacBio were amplicons and microbial/metagenomic work, but because Illumina volume is up overall there is no dip: "We just have more overall."
- Constellation (Illumina's long-read mapping approach, human-focused): not tried; the expert was told multi-species is being considered, proposed it for one project that did not proceed, and is watching ("It's a true path now").

## Roche Axelios and other new entrants

- Roche's Axelios sequencer (now in the field) is "not a great fit": it has limited applications, would "split and divide basically our focus and our budgets," replaces nothing, and "just sort of takes 0.1 of what we do on NovaSeq and moves it to a different system." Data eight hours sooner or later "is also not a critical place for us."
- The expert would "put it at the bottom of an Ultima Element Axolotl lineup" (the transcript's wording, apparently referring to Ultima and Element sequencers). It might fit single-focus labs, not "a core that wears a thousand hats." Most cores the expert knows have many hats. The moderator suggested it may go into clinical human research.

## Multiomics

- The expert sees 5-base as the first approach combining genomic DNA and methylation, and it is of interest.
- Multiomics requires more front-end and especially back-end work (multiple datasets). Students want to finish their own projects and often only want what they are interested in. The lab sends many quotes for large collaborative multi-professor multiomics ideas, but those that arrive are "pared down quite a bit."
- Spatial platforms may do multiomics well, because single-cell is hindered by the difficulty of getting good cells or nuclei. Multiomics is also more suited to better-developed genomes; de novo sequencing is not common at the lab.

## Bottlenecks, AI and sample preparation

- The biggest bottleneck is DNA and RNA extraction, followed by library prep ("library prep, library prep, library prep"). The cause is unique sample types, qualities and quantities and people's handling choices, requiring labor, space and protocol development. Anecdote: a researcher arrived with almost 600 cockroaches in freezer boxes in a Panda Express bag. In a beetle project, a researcher used the whole insect instead of the legs and got mostly bacterial sequence.
- The lab QCs everything with Qubit and Fragment Analyzer, in proportion to sequencing volume. It tries cheaper, quicker library kits but insists on doing it right; some places want "cheap, fast, and dirty."
- AI: can help with project planning, discussion and bioinformatics once data exist, but "AI can't make libraries, it can't extract DNA and RNA." The expert: "Sequencing has gotten easier... but the bottleneck is getting the material and making it compatible with the instrument."
- Extraction is the lab's premium service with the longest wait. Extraction "could be its own core facility," and the lab used to recommend outside vendors who do not like its sample types and try to fit it into a box. The expert sees a huge unmet need.
