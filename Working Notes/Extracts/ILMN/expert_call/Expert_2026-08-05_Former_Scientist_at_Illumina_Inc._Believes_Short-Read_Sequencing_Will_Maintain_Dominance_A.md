# Expert call – Former Illumina Medical & Scientific Affairs scientist (now Roche) on short-read dominance, competition, and clinical sequencing

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-08-05_Former_Scientist_at_Illumina_Inc._Believes_Short-Read_Sequencing_Will_Maintain_Dominance_A.md` · Date: 2026-08-05 · AlphaSense expert interview (published 2026-08-10); expert is a former Scientist, Medical and Scientific Affairs at Illumina, now Scientific Affairs Manager for sequencing at Roche Diagnostics (US); interviewer is a buy-side client (unnamed) · expert call*

**Summary.** A clinical-sequencing specialist with eight years in the field argues that Illumina's short-read platforms are "tried and true" and very hard to displace in clinical labs, even as newer rivals (Element/AXELIOS, Ultima, Thermo Fisher) compete on price and workflow simplicity. The expert sees long-read sequencing as a niche complement rather than a major threat. They see Illumina's main exposures as the NovaSeq 6000 phase-out, the burden on clinical labs of assembling multi-vendor workflows (liquid handling, library quantification), and uneven results in acquisitions. The expert repeatedly states limits on their knowledge of long-read, high-throughput, proteomics and recent Illumina disclosures.

## 1. Expert background and how to weigh this call

**Who the expert is.** The expert is a former Scientist in Medical and Scientific Affairs at Illumina Inc. [Expert: former Illumina scientist, 2026-08-05]. Medical and scientific affairs is the technical, science-facing function that supports customers and clinicians rather than selling. The interview was held on 5 August 2026 and published 10 August 2026. AlphaSense tags the expert perspective as "Former" and the analyst perspective as "Investor-Led (Buy-Side)".

**Career, as described by the expert:**
- Eight years in sequencing, "mostly in the clinical arm of it."
- A start-up spun out of ARUP (a clinical lab based in Salt Lake City), which Illumina acquired in 2020. The expert's Illumina role came through this acquisition, though the expert does not say so explicitly.
- A few years in the clinical sequencing division at Thermo Fisher.
- Currently Scientific Affairs Manager for the sequencing portfolio in the U.S. at Roche Diagnostics. Roche is an Illumina competitor, so the expert currently works for a rival.
- Earlier, a postdoc at Mount Sinai in New York City, where they started in sequencing doing transcriptional analysis (measuring which genes are switched on).
- The expert also says they "did help build a clinical lab."

**How recent and how reliable.** The Illumina experience dates to roughly 2020 onward and the expert has since moved through Thermo Fisher and Roche, so first-hand Illumina knowledge is not fully current. The expert says several times that they are not the right person for certain topics:
- Long-read sequencing: "I'm going to just be straightforward and honest here, I don't know a ton of it." They have "personally haven't really worked with long-read sequencing."
- High-throughput use: "I am not really a high-throughput user, being in the clinical space."
- Core labs (shared research sequencing facilities): "that's not my customer segment."
- Proteomics and SomaLogic: "I feel like I cannot really comment on it."
- Illumina's recent announcements: "I don't really remember what some of the bigger companies in sequencing, like Illumina, have put out there recently."
- Near the end: "I feel like I'm not helpful right now."

The strongest, most direct-experience content concerns clinical lab decision-making, workflow assembly, and bioinformatics needs. The weakest content is on market sizing, long-read, pricing mechanics, and Illumina's strategy, which are largely recollection, hearsay, or speculation.

## 2. Market size and growth

- **Market size (rough, from memory).** The U.S. is the largest sequencing market at "around probably $4 billion overall." Europe is "probably half that," and other regions are smaller. The expert gives no year and no source, so treat these as loose estimates [Expert, 2026-08-05].
- **Clinical is gaining share.** Clinical sequencing (testing used for patient care, as opposed to research) "has been getting more and more market share over the years." The expert says market growth is "driven mainly by these clinical applications."
- **Oncology is the largest driver.** Roughly 50% of clinical growth/revenue is oncology (cancer), per the expert's estimate. Reasons given:
  - Strong research and clinical evidence.
  - New pharma drug indications tied to specific biomarkers. The number of biomarkers and linked drugs has been rising over time.
- **MRD (molecular residual disease)** is called a "buzzword right now." MRD testing looks for tiny amounts of leftover cancer after treatment. It began in blood cancers ("heme") and is moving into solid tumors, with many clinical studies. The expert says "there is still a lot to be unpacked for different disease areas, but it is starting to pick up," and that MRD is a competitive space.

## 3. Long-read versus short-read sequencing

**Definitions.** Short-read sequencing (Illumina's approach) reads small DNA fragments and computationally stitches them together. Long-read sequencing (for example Oxford Nanopore) reads much longer stretches.

**Where the expert has seen long-read being explored** (self-described as limited knowledge, mostly investigational rather than routine):
- Gene fusions (where two genes join abnormally in cancer).
- Infectious disease. Long reads avoid having to line many short reads against reference sequences, which is "a little bit easier sometimes, especially for shotgun sequencing" (sequencing everything in a sample).
- Large structural rearrangements in the genome, where longer reads give better resolution.
- Pediatric cancers. Some academic medical centers are looking at it and would like to put it into production labs, but the expert is "not sure if it really has been yet."
- Simultaneous whole genome sequencing and methylation classification (a chemical-marking pattern used to classify tumors) in cancers including leukemia. The expert believes a clinical researcher at Harvard is working on this. It could help resolve regions that are hard for short reads.
- The expert doubts it is "necessarily being implemented in clinical labs yet for patient testing."

**What has limited long-read (hearsay, not direct experience):**
- Quality, though top quality is not needed for everything. For infectious disease, a faster answer (for example from nanopore sequencing) may matter more.
- Cost.
- In some cases, turnaround time, depending on the platform.

**Threat assessment.** The expert says "I don't know that it's going to be a major threat." Short-read is "tried and true," and "you can get a lot of information out of short read." Long-read comes in for questions short reads cannot resolve. On whether long-read takes a big share: "I don't know." The AlphaSense summary frames long-read as not a major current threat to short-read platforms.

## 4. Illumina's market position and products

**General position [Expert opinion].**
- Illumina is "tried and true," "so widespread everywhere in the world, and it's pretty much what labs know." Displacing it is "really difficult, especially in the clinical sequencing world."
- Quote: "They're not going to go away anytime soon."
- Illumina is trying to keep up with new instruments from other makers, including faster and longer reads and a move into multi-omics and spatial (mapping molecules within tissue). The expert expects it to "continue to evolve and to remain a major player" because "they're going to have to."
- The expert calls Illumina "the leader in the space" with "great science going on."

**High-throughput segment.**
- The NovaSeq X is expected to be "everywhere." "Everyone is going to have a NovaSeq X." Customers may also "play around with an Ultima system."
- NovaSeq X's value is likely price: output per price per gigabase ("really cheap"), plus different flow cells (the consumable slides where sequencing occurs) and applications. This appeals to core labs and commercial sequencing providers. The expert says this is outside their own usage, and that clinical may move toward high throughput with whole genome sequencing.
- The NovaSeq 6000 (the older high-throughput model) is "supposed to become obsolete," and that is where "some of the other players could come in and start competing a little bit more with Illumina."

**Lower-throughput segment.**
- NextSeq: "a workhorse in a lot of clinical labs where maybe they don't have the batching needs for a NovaSeq X" (that is, they don't have enough samples to fill big runs).
- MiSeq i100 (described as the latest): "really turnkey in some ways" because of on-instrument analysis. It has oncology and infectious disease workflows.
- Overall: Illumina is "trying to play to everyone's needs with the breadth of their portfolio," and "that's the great thing about Illumina. They can compete in both spaces."

**AGBT message (recalled).** At the AGBT conference (a major genomics meeting), Illumina reportedly said it would keep improving current products, especially NovaSeq X, rather than launch something new. The expert says large core and reference labs want this: they do not want to change instruments or learn a completely different workflow and technology, and upgrading reagents is preferable to upgrading instruments.

**Illumina's responses to competition (partly vague recollection).**
- Developing other workflows and working on speed. The expert half-remembers an AGBT or other mention of whole genome sequencing and doing spatial on Illumina instruments ("I cannot remember the name of it").
- Diversifying applications and turnaround time.
- Acquiring companies, including for proteomics.
- Mixed acquisition record: Illumina is "pretty good at acquiring good technology," but not everywhere. The expert cites 10x Genomics as a company built off the Illumina platform that also works with other sequencer makers. The expert's understanding (uncertain whether acquired or developed in-house) is that Illumina's single-cell sequencing application effort "is just not going to compete with 10x."
- Speculation: because Illumina is everywhere, it may assume "everyone is going to be building off of us anyway," with some third parties acting as "verticals of Illumina." The expert admits "I'm not sure."

**Clinical product.** Illumina has an IVD (in vitro diagnostic, a regulated test kit) for TSO 500 (TruSight Oncology 500, a comprehensive tumor-profiling panel). The expert notes it is "limited and it's a manual workflow," but "at least they have an IVD CGP [comprehensive genomic profiling] application."

**Proteomics / SomaLogic.** The expert declined to size it. Optically it "could be a great opportunity" to move into spatial and proteomics. They are unsure how well proteomics has gone even for Oxford Nanopore, and note other firms convert proteins into DNA-like surrogate molecules to read on a sequencer. "I really just don't know much about that space."

## 5. Pricing and the economics of switching

**Pricing environment [Expert, partly speculative].**
- "It seems like everyone is at around the same price point at this point." Prices are driven largely by sequencing cost per gigabase, but there is "always some marketing thing involved," and public prices are "not always all-inclusive."
- "Everyone is coming down to the same price point because they have to." Newer players may need to be even more attractive just to get a lab to bring on a non-Illumina instrument.

**Capital cost.** The NovaSeq X lists at "just above $1 million, like $1.2 or something" (instrument list price, per the expert's recollection). The expert doubts anyone pays list because of account-by-account deals. Newer entrants "definitely have to have some sort of capital cost incentive that is lower," plus another incentive. The expert gave no specific discount percentage needed to trigger a switch.

**Consumables and footprint as rival selling points (the expert says they are coming from Element's AXELIOS, which has launched).**
- Fewer consumables: AXELIOS has one sensor module rather than four flow cells.
- Flexible run time: customers choose a run of 30 minutes or four hours to maximize data.
- Smaller reagent footprint: no need for "four freezers for the reagent cartridges that have to be frozen," and less storage space. The expert is unsure whether Illumina flow cells still need 4°C storage or just no longer need 4°C shipping.
- Rivals must think about "all the little things" to be attractive.

**Why labs rarely switch (direct experience from building a clinical lab):**
- Risk questions the expert would ask: Will we know how to work with every component of the wet lab workflow? Do we have the expertise to handle differences in bioinformatic files and data from the sequencer relative to Illumina? Is that a risk? "Is it really going to be worth it?" Turnaround time and price also matter.
- The expert does not think rivals are really "displacing": "It is just opening, having some sort of differentiation to even consider bringing in this other instrument."
- Expected pattern: a core lab would add one Element, Ultima or AXELIOS instrument alongside, say, six NovaSeq Xs, not replace NovaSeq X.

**Multi-platform labs.**
- Expert is not sure it is a challenge; it may just be diversity. They have not spoken to many core labs and say sales specialists would know better.
- Some translational labs like "different tools in the toolbox" for throughput, turnaround and read length.
- Core labs want flexibility for the researchers they serve. A downside: with a new instrument, some researchers will not use it, and evidence has to be built. "Some researchers that are used to Illumina, they're never going to convert to a different instrument."

**Who might switch.**
- In clinical, "I don't think anyone is really going to move away from Illumina," since much information is built on the Illumina platform.
- Turnkey, end-to-end workflows appeal to some clinical customers, such as Thermo Fisher's GeneStudio or Genexus. But Thermo Fisher cannot do whole genome sequencing, or at best shallow whole genome sequencing (about 1X–5X coverage, one sample at a time). It has a place in clinical oncology. The expert has seen "maybe smaller labs" that only have Thermo Fisher, but "it is few and far between."

## 6. Informatics and data analysis

The expert says importance of informatics depends on customer type and even on who is asked within a segment:
- **Core labs and research:** often have their own bioinformatics teams, so built-in software is less needed.
- **Academic medical centers with clinical development labs:** varied. Citing MSK talks, teams have "limited bandwidth" and don't want to build pipelines for every clinical application. They like a ready end-to-end workflow that is open, "not a black box": they want to see alignment and its constraints, variant calling, and tools such as IGV (a viewer) to inspect read pile-ups.
- **Labs with fewer bioinformatics resources:** want primary and secondary analysis (basic processing and variant identification) at minimum, often plus a third party for tertiary analysis (interpretation by disease area). Even large labs with bioinformatics teams usually do not want to start from scratch.
- **Large reference labs (Labcorp, Quest, perhaps ARUP):** see the instrument as a tool, want only the data, and build their own pipelines.

## 7. Future challenges and opportunities for Illumina

**Share trajectory (clinical focus).** Next-generation sequencing is "high complexity" for clinical labs: it requires funding, wet-lab staff, and bioinformatics expertise. Even with an end-to-end workflow, some bioinformatic expertise is still needed. Illumina "is going to have to find a way to deliver on all aspects of the workflow, at least clinically." Competitors could "chip away" at share through offerings beyond sequencing, such as other instruments in the workflow, for example liquid handling (automated pipetting). Illumina has third-party partners, but "they are really going to have to get a little bit more creative."

**Go-to-market suggestion (direct experience from two employers with full workflows).** The expert says it is "a pain" for clinical labs to cobble together an Illumina workflow: a third-party liquid handler with separate maintenance packages, perhaps a PCR instrument, a Qubit (device measuring library quantity) before sequencing, and more downstream. They suggest Illumina be more creative with third-party vendors, for example "better support plans." They add "perhaps they are already doing it."

**Biggest vulnerabilities.**
- The NovaSeq 6000 situation: "I don't know what's going on with their NovaSeq 6000, but it does seem like that is going to create an opportunity for a lot of other companies."
- Competitors learning what customers dislike about Illumina and building differentiated products to fill gaps.
- Counterweight: "a lot of people just know Illumina and they really are comfortable with it."

## 8. Summary of the expert's overall stance

- Short-read stays dominant and trusted in clinical labs in the near term; Illumina remains a major player but must keep evolving.
- Some share loss is possible from rising competition, but entrenchment and portfolio breadth make full displacement difficult.
- Competition centers on price, bundled/turnkey workflows, and pain points such as consumables, footprint and multi-vendor workflow assembly.
- Long-read is a complement with promising niche uses, limited by quality, cost and clinical adoption; not seen as a major threat.
- The U.S. is the largest market, with oncology about half of clinical growth, and MRD is an emerging, competitive area.
