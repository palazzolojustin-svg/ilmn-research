# Expert call with UT Austin core-facility director Sara Goodwin on 4Q26 academic research, core-lab demand, sequencing, single-cell, spatial and competitors

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-20_KOL_ILMN_TMO_TXG_A_4Q26_Academic_Research_and_Core-Lab_Demand_With_UT_Austin_CBRS_Director_Sar.md` · Date: 2026-10-20 · Guidepoint expert call; expert Sara Goodwin (Director, Center of Biomedical Research Support, UT Austin; formerly Director of the Next Generation Sequencing Analysis Core Facility at Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory); moderator Andrew Carmen · expert call*

**Summary.** A university core-facility director says academic demand for Illumina sequencing, 10x single-cell and 10x spatial work is broadly flat going into 2027, with flow cytometry the one clear growth area. Rising reagent, labor and compute costs and tight grants are pushing institutions toward centralized sequencing hubs and outsourcing to commercial providers rather than buying their own NovaSeq instruments. She expects Roche's new sequencer to adopt slowly and does not see Element's Aviti as a threat to Illumina's market share.

# Expert call notes: UT Austin core facilities, academic and core-lab demand, 4Q26

## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

- The expert is Sara Goodwin, Director of the Center of Biomedical Research Support (CBRS) at the University of Texas at Austin. The call was hosted by Guidepoint, with Andrew Carmen as moderator, on Oct 20, 2026, and ran about 37 minutes.
- Her relationship is that of a **customer and user of Illumina and competitor platforms** (an academic core-facility operator), not a former Illumina employee. The transcript labels her viewpoint "Academic KOL - Physician Scientist" (KOL means key opinion leader).
- Until about the end of July 2026 she was Director of the Next Generation Sequencing Analysis Core Facility at Cold Spring Harbor Laboratory. She returned to UT Austin, where she earned her PhD, for her current job. Her sequencing experience is therefore very recent, but she has been in the UT Austin role only about three months. She says she is "new" to it, and some answers draw on her Cold Spring Harbor and wider network experience.
- In her current role she oversees roughly 10 to 11 different core facilities (shared labs that sell instrument access and services to researchers). They cover genomics, cryo-electron microscopy (cryo-EM, imaging of frozen molecules), microscopy, flow cytometry (counting and profiling cells), mass spectrometry, a shared instrumentation facility (general biomedical equipment support), a biomedical imaging center, and a scientific computing facility with compute infrastructure and bioinformatics support. She also oversees service centers such as stock rooms and shops that maintain the equipment.
- These are only the biomedical research cores. UT Austin has many other cores in other departments.
- She notes a surprising gap: UT Austin has no histology core (tissue slide preparation), which she says the university is looking into.
- Primary companies discussed: ILMN, TMO, TXG, A. Associated: DHR, BIO, WAT, BRKR, RVTY, QGEN, ONT, PACB.

## Summary points highlighted by the document

- Demand is broadly stable, but varies by technology. Genomics, single-cell and spatial workflows are flat. Flow cytometry and advanced cell analysis are expanding.
- Rising equipment, reagent, labor and computing costs push institutions toward cautious purchasing, outsourcing and system-wide collaboration.
- She believes large-scale institutional collaboration is essential for long-term sustainability, and that core facilities are nearing a structural turning point because funding limits and operating costs undermine purely fee-for-service models.
- She thinks Roche sequencing may be adopted more slowly than expected because of its late market entry, library-preparation challenges and unclear differentiation.

## Sequencing: volumes, instruments and utilization

### Overall trend into 4Q26 [Expert: UT Austin core director, Oct 2026]
- **Stable.** Internal users within the UT system, particularly UT Austin, are using services at about the same level as last year (direct observation).
- Outsourcing to NovaSeq centers has increased somewhat but "hasn't heavily picked up yet." She is forecasting increased outsourcing demand into 2027.
- Spatial technology sequencing happens at UT Austin but has not materially raised volumes over last year. Single-cell work is likewise not driving volumes up.
- She said genomics is "probably the most challenged right now" of her areas because of how widely available high-throughput sequencing has become.

### Illumina's higher-throughput products and the academic capital problem
- She says Illumina has been promoting a 30B NovaSeq flow cell (a flow cell is the consumable slide on which sequencing occurs; "B" refers to billions of reads) on the existing NovaSeq X, with higher accuracy, new flow cell types and falling costs, which she calls "fantastic for the field."
- However, these gains are not easily reachable in academia because of very large capital investment. As capacity on one instrument grows, more cores, institutions and researchers can be multiplexed (pooled) onto it. Academia therefore struggles to judge the value of having a truly high-throughput instrument in-house versus within a system.
- UT Austin does not currently offer NovaSeq services. NovaSeq access is available within the broader UT system. UT Austin itself currently sends work to other Texas institutions because of existing relationships; she describes this as preference, not a system-platform choice.
- She sees similar structures elsewhere: system-wide placement of high-throughput instruments or increased outsourcing to commercial sequencing companies such as Novogene, Genewiz and other CROs (contract research organizations). Demand growth is in whole-genome Illumina sequencing, where costs are falling and data access is rising, but it is increasingly centralized at large institutions or CROs.
- She understands the push to centralize as aimed at achieving economies of scale, especially as providers move into the 35B flow cell and must fill it.

### Flow cell choice: 10B versus 35B (partly from her own outsourcing experience)
- Groups she works with to outsource her own NovaSeq work, which take diverse projects, like the **10B flow cell**. It has a relatively short runtime and a depth that works well for pooling different projects without waiting to gather many projects.
- Groups doing exclusively whole-genome or clinically oriented work, with the same types of samples every time, can better exploit the **35B flow cell**.
- The 10B flow cell also contributes to the centralization push.

### NovaSeq 6000, NextSeq 2000 and NovaSeq X
- She knows of no NovaSeq 6000 meaningfully in use at any of her connections. Some legacy projects still use it, but those remaining in the field are "wildly underutilized if being utilized at all."
- People use the **NextSeq 2000** for rapid turnaround, with one project back to back with another, at "unsurprisingly higher costs." Others move to NovaSeq X infrastructure because the 6000 has lost its competitive edge against the XLEAP flow cells on the NextSeq 2000 and "unquestionably" against the diverse flow cells now on the NovaSeq X.
- She says the NextSeq 2000 must be kept full and, even then, is becoming expensive to run.

### Buying a NovaSeq at UT Austin
- Her team is not trying to get into the 6000. Building a NovaSeq infrastructure means multi-million-dollar investments: the sequencer, staffing, and building a user base. It often does not make sense to place an instrument, certainly not through standard capital expenditure.
- UT Austin is still looking to acquire a NovaSeq X, but not through leasing or fundraising; it is trying to place it via grant mechanisms. She says the new funding cycle for instrumentation grants is coming up and the institution is debating whether to continue pursuing local placement, with valid arguments on both sides.
- In response to a later question, she said NovaSeq is something her core is **not investing in** despite believing it would be used, because of the high cost and the ability to outsource readily. By contrast, cell-based technologies cannot easily be sent elsewhere, so there is more forward planning on those.

## Single-cell and spatial

### Single-cell (10x Chromium)
- She does not think her facility does anything meaningful without 10x. Instrument-agnostic or "instrument-free" platforms have appeal but have not performed at the level of Chromium for identifying transcripts and counting cells, according to both internal experience and colleagues.
- Single-cell demand is **flat**: no decline, no growth. She does not see much cannibalization of single-cell workflows by spatial. Single cell is established, and people know what it can and cannot do.

### Spatial (10x Xenium)
- Their spatial platform is Xenium. Utilization has been largely flat. People are doing it and talking about it, but onboarding takes **at least 4 to 6 months from first contact to even preparing samples**. She expects growth to continue, but the trend "has not been terribly steep."
- Interest exceeds actual projects. Barriers she cites:
  - No histology center for upfront sample preparation.
  - Cost of even pilot experiments, which she says the last two years of grant challenges have made a hurdle.
  - Technical expertise: preparing samples for spatial is challenging and not easy across all tissue types. Troubleshooting why a sample failed requires expertise, which is scarce. Existing histologists and pathologists must learn new skills, and the industry is "fighting with each other" over a limited pool of people who know what they are doing.
- Users want spatial particularly for multi-omic applications.
- She is not looking at 10x's higher-throughput spatial instrument (she recalled the name as Altera after being corrected). It does not align with her demand, and they bought Xenium not long ago, so appetite to expand instruments is limited. The moderator noted it looks impressive but implies heavier Xenium use.

## Core-facility economics and funding

- Margins in genomics, including spatial and single-cell, are getting "narrower and narrower" for cores. Reagent, labor and operating costs keep rising while grant funding has not risen at the same rate.
- Current state is "sustainable now": UT Austin recoups the costs of its single-cell infrastructure, but she says they are "holding our breath" that early positive funding signs continue. This is mostly NIH-driven. If the facility did more plant or NSF (National Science Foundation) work she would be much more concerned, given those grants' income lines.
- Grant picture is uneven. There are more big grants awarded, and positive growth particularly in translational and disease-specific research, which is driving UT Austin's growth, but not across all facilities or technologies. She cautions that Texas has somewhat different funding because of robust state support for science in a couple of biomedical fields, giving some insulation from federal grant challenges.
- Other funding sources she cites: CPRIT and a dementia-focused equivalent (she said "GPRI"), described as cancer- and dementia-specific grants, which support forward planning in flow cytometry.
- Behavior change over the past 3 to 6 months: core facilities face increasing pressure to focus on the bottom line, with added pressure on indirect costs, increased oversight and rising cost of living. She says cores are at a "major shift point" and it is "kind of reaching a boiling point" over what a modern academic core looks like and what its long-term sustainable model is. She has seen more discussion of this in six months than in a long time.
- "Simply running cores as a business isn't the solution. If that was the case, everyone would be going to CROs, and there'd be no reason to have a core facility."
- Research is not getting bigger: the same researchers are submitting projects, and projects are becoming **more specialized, not larger**. Examples: more metabolomics demand in mass spec (a big training focus), and more interest in methylation sequencing alongside whole-genome data.

## Purchasing outlook and capital spending

- UT Austin recently completed a good expansion of some core facilities through a **recapitalization initiative** (an internal equipment-renewal funding program). Interest and growth are partly replacement but "not exclusively," especially in flow cytometry and cell analysis.
- Approach: caution. Replacements and improvements are planned meticulously, viewing core infrastructure as a whole ecosystem.
- Instrument purchasing likely in 2027 (multiple initiatives, none definite):
  - **BD FACSDiscover S8** (a spectral cell analyzer/sorter that profiles cells with many simultaneous markers; she recalled "up to 40" simultaneous measurements). Interest is rising as UT Austin adds medical expertise, and there are potential funding paths to place one.
  - A **nanoscale flow cytometer** for sorting small particles; a grant has been submitted. She later said the candidate is from **NanoFCM**.
  - Possible **Astral-class mass spectrometer** (a high-end proteomics instrument), but this would be driven by an individual researcher's needs and is less robust.
  - **Cryo-EM** growth is multi-year and project-driven, for example an incoming principal investigator wanting specific technologies, not broad core improvement.
- She abandoned plans to expand into **CyTOF** (mass cytometry) because of reagent costs, challenging metal-conjugated antibodies and high human expenditures.
- Sony flow equipment was mentioned by the moderator as appearing on the UT website; she said they are looking at the BD instrument and others.

## Beyond genomics: utilization trends by area

| Area | Trend per expert | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Flow cytometry | Growth phase | More projects, costs to some extent decreasing; recapitalization; new capabilities planned for 2027 |
| Cryo-EM | Stable ("with a big star") | Buzz around AI-powered downstream analysis; growth in Texas Advanced Computing Center (TACC) usage supporting AI, cryo, and some genomics and multi-omics |
| Mass spec | Selective interest | Metabolomics demand rising; Astral considered; helium shortage a problem |
| Genomics | Flat to most challenged | Wide availability of high-throughput sequencing |
| Single-cell / spatial | Flat | See above |
| Compute | Cost constrained | See below |

- She also said cell-based imaging is growing and cryo-EM faces similar CRO capabilities to genomics.
- She says the AI analysis topic is "completely beyond my wheelhouse."

## Consumables and cost pressures
- **Consumables are inching up, particularly in genomics.** She attributes this to the mid-throughput platforms they run; the cost savings are coming on higher-throughput platforms.
- **Helium** has been in shortage for some time, mainly hurting mass spectrometry.
- **Compute:** expanding entry-level GPU capability is difficult because of GPU and graphics-card cost increases driven by the "AI bubble." Costs for graduate-student-level nodes have "gone through the roof." Her compute has two tiers: TACC for big, AI-driven data, and an internal system for GPU-accelerated image analysis (for example MRI and PET image calling from the imaging facility), which needs GPUs but not data-center-class ones. Internal expansion is the challenged area.

## Long-read sequencing (PacBio, Oxford Nanopore)
- Long-read has come up but instruments are expensive. A **PacBio Revio is about a $1 million investment** with unclear internal demand. It is a prioritization question: UT Austin can push for a Revio or PromethION, or for a NovaSeq, but "we can't do both."
- Within UT Austin users there is more demand for high-throughput traditional short-read sequencing than for long-read, so they are "choosing to pursue short-read sequencing right now."
- Long-read access is spreading: Texas A&M now has one, Stowers and Washington University are adding multiple Revios or PromethIONs, and Plasmidsaurus now offers long-read service. It is "no longer as specialty as it used to be," and not everyone needs one in-house. UT Austin collaborates with other Texas institutions for long-read; she believes MD Anderson has a Revio but is not certain.
- **PacBio Vega** (a lower-priced, lower-throughput long-read instrument): she calls it "weirdly positioned." It is a more accessible price but still substantial capital, with lower throughput. A Revio fills easily with even modest demand because so much coverage is needed (e.g., plant work). Vega suits "I only need long-read sequencing occasionally," and running things occasionally is expensive.

## Epigenetics, 5-base and multi-omics
- There is growing interest in methylation (a chemical modification of DNA that affects gene activity) alongside whole-genome data. 5-base work (Illumina's method reading methylation together with the genome) is done with Illumina. She describes it as the same platform and instruments, but more specialized technology.
- Her view on whole genomes: researchers see limits to what is discoverable from whole **human** genome sequencing, and capacity would outstrip demand "if everyone who wanted a NovaSeq had one." Example: autism, where many whole genomes exist publicly, but gains in identifying genomic drivers "have been incremental at best." There are calls for bigger whole-genome cohorts, but also calls to explore alternatives such as 5-base and multi-omics (linking variants to modifications, transcription and proteins).
- She does **not** claim to know this is true: "I don't think that we know that for sure." Large-scale epigenomic characterization is new (5-base from Illumina, TAPS, non-chemical approaches, phased methylation, RNA modifications), not feasible 10 years ago at this scale. Adding AI makes it "more exciting, but also challenging."

## Centralization and the future of core facilities

- Genomics has "a real possibility of increased system-wide consolidation." Platforms are so large that universal NovaSeq placement would exceed demand.
- Her prediction: a combination of centralization and commercial providers, depending on technology. Some work must stay local: clinically adjacent projects with human participants, or cells collected locally and analyzed locally.
- "Non-complex, bread and butter, everyday, nothing special projects need to be handled in bulk." She foresees system-wide hubs (UT System sequencing hub, University of California hub), possibly specializing (one for methylation, one for long reads, one for RNA).
- Competing on value: cores must focus on specialized services, things they do particularly well, faster turnaround, subsidized costs. Institutions not buying a NovaSeq should organize access to one elsewhere. This adds administrative burden and some growth in core administration to facilitate inter-system collaboration.
- Vendor implications: purchasing "has to become more efficient." Systems should avoid duplicated resources; 10 NovaSeqs within the UT system "doesn't make sense," yet buying 5 or 10 brings meaningful cost savings (Broad Institute and University of Washington cited). She wants systems to present "a united front" to companies for large placements supporting a whole system. This is her opinion about the direction, not a reported change.
- Pace: Broad-scale centralization "is not happening tomorrow"; it takes significant institutional collaboration and memoranda of understanding. First step is within the UT system (which includes MD Anderson and all UT campuses). She hopes to increase links with Texas State, Texas A&M and Rice, but is "nowhere near that."

## Unmet needs
- The biggest unmet area is a concept rather than a technology: **clinical and clinical-adjacent research**. There is increased oversight of human protected data (imaging, genomics, organoids). The UT system is targeting growth in cancer, dementia, neuroscience and translational work.
- Gaps: no histology lab, and systems to handle and transport protected human data across buildings and compute centers in a compliant way. That infrastructure is being built.

## Competitors and 2027 outlook

### Roche sequencing
- Her "surprise" for 2027 is the inverse of a common view: "I don't think Roche is going to take off the way that some people think they are." She said there is "so much buzz" (she named it the "LXEOS," a transcription of Roche's platform name) but is "not convinced."
- Reasons: steep adoption trajectory, library-preparation challenges "far larger than they anticipate," late market entry, and she is "still not convinced that their technology is measurably better than what already is out there." They have interesting applications and may carve out a toehold; she thinks it is possible but "very, very hard." This is opinion without direct use.

### Element Aviti
- UT Austin does not have an Element. She reports a lot of satisfaction with the Element mid-throughput sequencer; it is positioned well versus NextSeq 2000 and has potential cost benefits for mid-sized institutions unlikely to get a NovaSeq. Responses on its cell-profiling capabilities are mixed (secondhand, no personal experience). Aviti may carve a small niche in "mid, high-mid needs."
- "I don't see Element substantially cutting into Illumina's market share though... I don't think they're a challenge to Illumina."

## Key quantitative reference points
- Core portfolio: about 10 to 11 facilities.
- Spatial onboarding: often 4 to 6 months from first contact.
- NovaSeq flow cells discussed: 10B, 30B, 35B.
- Revio investment: about $1 million.
- FACSDiscover: roughly 40 simultaneous measurements (expert's recollection).
- NovaSeq X placement: multi-million-dollar build-out including staffing.

## Concluding statement [Expert, Oct 2026]
Core facilities, especially in genomics, are at an interesting point; cryo-EM faces similar CRO capabilities. Because of changes in the granting landscape, cost of "simply existing" and oversight from granting agencies, institutions cannot think in isolation. "Large-scale collaboration is the only way to go forward at this point."

## Caveats
- Single-source opinion from a newly appointed director; much of the UT Austin detail is firsthand, while views on Roche, Element cell profiling, MD Anderson's Revio, and future centralization are secondhand or speculative.
- Product names were transcribed imperfectly (e.g., "LXEOS" for Roche, "Altera," "CBERS"/"Sarah" in the title text); GPRI as a dementia grant program is unverified.
- No estimates, ratings, price targets or company guidance appear in this document.
