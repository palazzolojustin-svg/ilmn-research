# Expert call (Tegus) with a Senior Director of Strategic Product Management at Oxford Nanopore (ex-Illumina, Adaptive, 10x): sequencing market drivers, long vs short reads, and Roche AXELIOS as a threat to Illumina

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_Tegus_Senior_Director_of_Strategic_Product_Management_at_Oxford_Nanopore_Technologies_Illumina_I.md` · Date: 2026-10-01 · Tegus expert interview; expert is a Senior Director of Strategic Product Management at Oxford Nanopore Technologies, a former Illumina employee (2008-2014); target company Roche; interviewer is an anonymous Tegus client. The call date is not stated and is estimated from references to a recent quarter and Nanopore's half-year report. · expert call*

**Summary.** A veteran sequencing-industry insider (Illumina, Adaptive, 10x, Oxford Nanopore) says sequencing growth now comes from clinical diagnostics, especially cancer testing at about 10-20 large labs, while academic research demand has collapsed with US funding cuts. They view Illumina as the entrenched "workhorse" that is very hard to displace, and see Roche's AXELIOS as innovative but held back by complex chemistry, two-box workflow, labor cost, regulatory validation and a slow, multi-year switching cycle. They expect Roche to take only about 10-20% from Illumina in three years, with a possible $1-2 billion Roche sequencing business in ten years. They are openly skeptical of Roche's commercial execution and of PacBio, Ultima and Element.

## Expert background and how to read this call

**Who the expert is.** The speaker is a Senior Director of Strategic Product Management at Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT, a long-read sequencing company). The interview is labeled "Target: Roche," so the questions focus on Roche's new sequencer. The expert has an unusually broad history in sequencing [Expert: Senior Director, Strategic Product Management, Oxford Nanopore, ~Oct 2026]:

- Began as a user, pouring slab electrophoresis gels (the old DNA-reading method) as a graduate student, then moved to next-generation sequencing around 2006 with 454 Life Sciences and then Illumina. They attended an early Cold Spring Harbor sequencing course in 2006 or 2007 (its second year).
- Joined Illumina at the beginning of 2008 and stayed about six years. They were recruited because they had already used the technology successfully many times. They say they were the first known person to publish on cloning human microRNAs from blood plasma as a circulating cancer biomarker.
- Spent about three years at Adaptive Biotechnologies, an Illumina customer that runs a clinically approved test for minimal residual disease (MRD, detecting tiny amounts of leftover cancer after treatment) on Illumina machines.
- Spent about four years at 10x Genomics working on single-cell analysis ("still wedded to the Illumina technology"), about one year in spatial genomics (a separate business just outside sequencing), and about two and a half years at Oxford Nanopore.
- They say most of their career, until about a year and a half ago, was in sequencing.

**How to weigh it.** Their Illumina experience is old (2008-2014). Their view of Illumina's current business comes from public information, not inside knowledge. They repeatedly say they are not privy to Illumina's numbers, have not asked anyone for them, and do not know what Roche's or any customer's internal numbers are. They are also employed by a competitor to both Illumina and Roche, which may color their views. Direct experience is flagged below where it exists. Everything about Roche's future, pricing, and customers' economics is the expert's own inference ("when you just do the math"), not observed fact.

## Market growth and what is driving it

**Diagnostics up, research down.** Based on the latest round of quarterly earnings calls, the expert says "everybody's done reasonably well" last quarter. 10x Genomics is "one of the more glaring exceptions" with flat revenue growth, and "Illumina had a great quarter, all things considered." The expert says growth is concentrated in diagnostics. The research-use-only (RUO) market "has fallen off a cliff in the Trump administration's second turnaround" and "probably comes back in the future." The expert expects some return of research funding "in the next couple cycles." They note that much of therapeutics innovation comes out of academic labs. [Expert, ~Oct 2026]

**Size of clinical volume (speculative, dated).** When the expert last checked, "admittedly a couple months ago," they estimated total clinical diagnostic sequencing at somewhere in the low millions of samples, possibly up to double-digit millions. They see "an enormous amount of growth potential" across the whole human population. If the price per sample falls far enough, which the industry has not quite reached but "will probably get there pretty quickly," sequencing could expand into consumer applications and near-daily use cases.

**Core labs under stress (direct experience of funding, general view of current state).** Core labs are shared-resource facilities inside universities and institutes. They typically own at least one Illumina instrument, take samples from across the institution, and often subsidize running costs per institution. They are funded largely by NIH, NSF and other US government grants, and the expert says the US core lab market has "really been struggling for the last couple of years."

- The expert says funding was already tough when they were a postdoc about 20 years ago. The "pay line" (the percentile of grant applications that get funded) for an R01 (the main investigator grant, typically about $500,000 to maybe $1 million a year) was around 12.5% then. They say it is now below 10%, though they have not checked the latest figure.
- The result is that scarce funding is spread across a wider pool of researchers. Money still flows into sequencing, but Illumina's own statements point to diagnostics, "clearly not in research-use cases," as where growth is.

**Who drives Illumina's growth (expert's expectation, not inside knowledge).** The expert expects Illumina's growth to be "dominated by roughly 10 or so large sequencing houses that are growing quite rapidly," named as Natera, Guardant, Tempus and Caris. These labs have operated for about 10 years and have driven clinical sequencing, particularly in oncology, now creeping into other indications. Later they say it may be "maybe 20" institutions driving the bulk of Illumina's sample volume and revenue.

## Long reads vs short reads

**Why the distinction matters less.** The expert says they "care less about the distinction" over time. Roche AXELIOS sits in "a nice middle ground": with enough reads per sample, read length matters less. They also say read length "matters less and less" for standard diagnostic applications.

**Where long reads still win.**
- Finishing a complete human genome: "Oxford Nanopore is currently the only thing you'd ever think about using seriously." The expert calls this an "incredibly research-use purpose at the moment," with possibly underexplored future clinical uses.
- Native detection of epigenetic markers (chemical tags on DNA that modify gene behavior). Nanopore reads these directly, whereas other platforms need a "chemical trick." The expert says PacBio is similar but not quite as good, and that Roche "will really struggle." Roche claims a roadmap for it, but because of how its technology works, adding epigenetics would be "a really significant R&D effort," while for Nanopore it is "just another turn on the training of a machine learning model."
- Haplotype and phasing information, meaning working out which of the two copies of a gene is responsible for disease. This is harder with short reads.

**Illumina's response and the rare-disease angle.** The expert says Illumina launched, at the beginning of this year, a product they believe is "still called Constellation" (the expert is unsure of the name). It is "a bit of a trick," not a perfect long read, but gets closer. In their view it is probably still worse than Nanopore. The use case is patients with rare diseases, a large population "largely ignored by clinical diagnostics firms until Nanopore came along." If Illumina improves here, it "probably threatens Oxford's long-read clinical sequencing position a bit." The expert still thinks Nanopore is better but more expensive, making reimbursement by payers the question.

**Price comparison.** Illumina is now at about $150 per genome, and Nanopore at "at least double that probably on average," so not "breaking the bank" on a roughly $1,000-per-sample basis, but more expensive. [Expert, ~Oct 2026]

**Illumina's entrenchment and the failures of challengers.** "Illumina still is the workhorse of the industry, remains that way probably into the foreseeable future." Overcoming it takes "a lot of activation energy." Short-read challengers Ultima and Element have not succeeded at scale. Element "still somehow draws in fundraising" and is doing okay but probably not well enough to succeed without a change in capacity and cost. Once a customer is fully entrenched, "everything is baked in" and the barrier is significant. The expert says Oxford Nanopore struggled with the same barrier while they were there.

**Where Roche could be disruptive.** For applications that count short fragments, such as RNA sequencing and liquid biopsy (testing circulating tumor or cell-free DNA in blood), read length does not matter; read density does. The expert says that is where a Roche system "has quite a disruptive potential," especially if Roche holds to its stated goal of $150 per human genome. They add that Roche "are reading the tea leaves" on the needed price point and "not dummies" after long experience in diagnostics.

## Roche AXELIOS: technology

- **Chemistry (opinion and public knowledge).** The expert calls it "quite funky," originating in Seattle and bought through an acquisition years ago. It converts every nucleotide of the template into a copy called an Xpandomer, relatively non-biological, "almost a Frankenstein monster of an oligonucleotide copy." After roughly a decade of development it "seems to be quite effective," but it is "by far the weirdest of the technologies out there."
- **Nanopore base.** It is based on a nanopore (a tiny hole through which molecules pass and are read electrically). The expert says it is closer to a genuine solid-state system than Oxford's, but "not perfectly so," requiring assembly of component parts.
- **Evidence of performance.** Roche has generated "a healthy amount of data," including with early-access customers for what sounds like one to two years. The expert began seeing presentations at sequencing conferences late last year (unsure of exact dates).
- **Read length and scale.** It seems poised to read up to about 1,000 base pairs per strand, at relatively high scale.
- **Long-term speed potential.** Nanopore systems in general, including Oxford's and Roche's, read nucleotides extremely fast. In principle, especially on solid-state nanopores, whole human genomes could be sequenced in minutes rather than hours, but "nobody has achieved" that yet. The expert says Oxford "slurped up all of the IP in this space that they could."
- **Weak link.** "I honestly think their weakest link is that chemistry. It's just cumbersome and probably more expensive, definitely more expensive than you would want it to be." Oxford, by contrast, loads native strands straight into the machine.
- **Two boxes.** Roche has one instrument for sample prep and one for sequencing, which raises capital cost more than the expert would choose as a product manager. They add that Oxford "has their own problems, frankly."
- **Not for long reads or epigenetics.** The expert sees Roche's current chemistry as unable to do long reads or epigenetics.

## Target applications and customers for AXELIOS

**The expert's pitch (direct experience).** The expert pitched Roche at AMP (the Association for Molecular Pathology meeting) last November: replicate on AXELIOS what Adaptive did on Illumina. "Nobody ever called me back," which they consider a shame because Roche "could be quite threatened."

**MRD logic.** MRD in T- and B-cell cancers needs only about 1,000 to a couple thousand base pairs of sequence at one rearranged gene that marks each patient's disease. The challenge is depth: detecting one positive cell in one million, which requires sampling around five million cells' worth of DNA times copy number. The expert calls it "a slam dunk kind of application" for liquid biopsy on AXELIOS, and liquid biopsy is "obviously growing the most in the entire industry." It has been hard because of read depth and price per sample. If Roche automates sample prep, which the expert has "no real doubt" it can do at scale, and holds a price point they think it is "already" at (where reimbursement has been forthcoming), entry into liquid biopsy is "obvious." The remaining task is displacing the incumbent.

**Why incumbents will not rip and replace (expert's own calculation, posted on LinkedIn last November).** Labs such as Natera, Caris and Guardant have "a factory floor full of Illumina systems." The expert guesses dozens or even hundreds of instruments per institution, though says they do not know actual numbers. Replacing them would be an enormous capital expense with no dramatic difference in pricing and sample volume. Operating costs may even rise: running more samples faster needs more operator time unless automation improves, and the expert found it "hard to imagine" that happening quickly. Sequencing costs might be about equal while overall operational costs could be higher on Roche, "slowing them down."

**Where adoption could start.** Each large lab has a tech-development team with "one of everything." They may find a use for AXELIOS, probably liquid biopsy, but scaling it into a fleet doing millions of patient samples could take "some time."

**Overall view on Roche's commercial execution (opinion).** "Long term, optimistic for the industry... Short term, I think for Roche particularly, they have their work cut out for them." They say they are not seeing the "commercial power" they would want, would "be asking some hard questions" if in Roche's leadership, and that Roche has been "talking a lot and not saying much" for about two years and needs to "put up or shut up." They acknowledge they cannot see inside Roche and it may be going slowly on purpose.

## Roche pricing strategy

- **Vague so far.** Since Roche first spoke publicly at AGBT (a genomics conference in Florida) in January 2025, it has only said it will be "competitive with market pricing," without specifics.
- **Capital cost (expert's impression).** The instrument is fairly in line with what people pay for larger-throughput platforms, and the two boxes together cost less than a NovaSeq X (Illumina's flagship high-throughput sequencer).
- **Per-sample cost.** Roche has said $150 per human genome, which the expert uses as a rough proxy for other applications such as RNA-seq and liquid biopsy. On reagents alone, they think Roche is "already at a competitive footprint with Illumina and some of the others."
- **No race to the bottom.** "Nobody's trying to chase Ultima to the bottom of the barrel on pricing." The expert thinks "Ultima's doomed in the long run anyway" and expects players mostly to maintain margins for now.
- **Limits.** The expert would be "shocked" if Roche could cut instrument prices by an order of magnitude; shrinking big, bulky machines to what Illumina did with the MiSeq or NextSeq "seems hard."
- **Illumina's mix (expert estimate from public information, not inside knowledge).** Mid-throughput NextSeq-type platforms are probably about $500 million to $1 billion of revenue, an eighth to a quarter or less of Illumina's total, and "clearly not the driver of Illumina's fortunes." The NovaSeq X is the driver, at "on average $1 million or more per instrument at pull-through" (the expert's wording; the period and the exact measure are not specified).
- **Roche's challenge.** To match that, Roche would likely need more competitive per-sample pricing because samples per system are fewer, and faster throughput requires more operator time and labor cost, which the expert thinks could offset any price advantage "for the foreseeable future." The expert has not asked operators how they budget for this.
- **How aggressive Roche must be.** It would need to be "quite aggressive" on all-in price (everything from receiving a sample to returning a result) to threaten Illumina over a 10-year timeframe. The expert is "skeptical" but notes Roche "can do volume that nobody else in the industry can meet," so it depends on how much Roche wants to fund this.
- **Roche's diagnostic precedent.** Roche Diagnostics has done "extraordinarily well on extremely cheap diagnostics" with enormous volume ($1 to $10 price points). In sequencing, through Foundation Medicine (bought "10 years ago or something"), it "did okay" but never became as dominant as newer entrants, in a more expensive test category (thousands of dollars, now maybe hundreds).
- **Could sequencing reach single-digit prices?** Clinicians, regulators and government payers would "love" that, but the industry cannot do that without cratering its own revenue. "I don't see it happening in the next 10 years," though a product development "shoe" may yet drop.

## Barriers to Roche's adoption

**Switching friction.** "Activation energy" is the primary barrier. Roche can be opportunistic: find sites where an Illumina service contract is about to lapse, offer a good price, and accept margin loss to get in. The expert calls this "slow but sure" and says Roche has "the deepest pockets in the industry imaginable."

**New entities.** Each new diagnostic lab takes about six to 10 years (six minimum) from early development to a CLIA- and CAP-approved platform (US lab quality accreditations) running as an LDT (lab-developed test) and eligible to seek reimbursement. The expert's pitch to Roche was to target those new entities with no Illumina base.

**Rapid replacement is implausible.** Replacing Illumina's installed base in three to four years would require a "ridiculously cheap" system. Capital cost is only part of it, and if per-sample operational costs are roughly equal or slightly higher, margins on reimbursable tests do not improve. Only players with spare cash to make a capital move could buy Roche systems at scale, and the expert does not know who would.

**Reagent rental idea.** Roche could place instruments under a reagent rental model (instrument cost near zero, with a slightly higher reagent price). The expert doubts customers would place hundreds of instruments that way because Roche would carry heavy upfront capital, recouped over three to five years. Because diagnostic labs are bound by fixed, government-set reimbursement, they cannot pass higher prices on, and reimbursement will not rise soon.

**Macro and geography.**
- The expert notes Oxford Nanopore posted "a pretty decent half-year report, honestly, 10% or 12%, I think it was, growth overall." Most sequencing companies are growing without much help from China or other geopolitical threats.
- The largest, most centralized clinical diagnostics entities are in the US. There are European houses and plenty of work across Asia, but Europe alone cannot drive growth. Losing China and trying to replace capital equipment in a European market "generally less enthusiastic about that CapEx spend" means a slow path.
- The expert thinks Roche faces "a 10-year program," which Roche can afford ("institutional pockets and patience").

## Does Roche have to take share from Illumina?

**Market-size argument.** Intuitively, no: current institutions "are clearly underserving the patient population at large in sheer volume globally." But global patients mostly lack access or the ability to pay, "at least not yet."

**The Adaptive example (partly direct experience).**
- The expert says Adaptive seems to be "turning a corner." If it carves out and divests its immune medicines/RUO business (which it has discussed "for some time") and focuses on clonoSEQ, "they're printing cash." That shows there is market growth available to a Roche-backed entity or to Roche persuading Adaptive to switch its LDT to a Roche platform, which "would take years."
- Switching from NovaSeq 6000 to NovaSeq X, the same basic chemistry at higher throughput, "probably took Adaptive years." The expert recalls Adaptive saying on earnings calls that the difference between being unprofitable and profitable with clonoSEQ was turning on the NovaSeq X, and that came a year and a half to two years after the first instruments shipped. Changing platforms alters throughput, library prep and automation strategy, "retooling your entire factory floor."
- Switching an existing entity to a different provider entirely is "a four- or five-year project."

**Two paths for Roche (both slow).**
1. Stand up a new entity, independent of Illumina, which still takes about six years to regulatory acceptance.
2. Convert an existing LDT institution from Illumina to Roche, which takes a similar time because of the "extraordinarily high" validation burden.

**Selling and geography.** New entrants must build relationships with providers and payers, win approvals, and persuade clinical centers they are better, faster or cheaper. In Europe, much clinical diagnostic work is institutional or regionally consolidated in public entities and less often outsourced to private labs than in the US, making market entry harder there.

## Competitive threat from Roche to Illumina

**Expert's headline estimate.** Roche "could probably pick away 10%-20% relatively easily" (over about the next three years), depending on how close Illumina's concentration is to an 80/20 split with its diagnostic mega-providers. The expert says in the next three years they do not see Roche "displacing more than that 10% or 20%," but over 10 years or longer "the potential is there for them to be quite threatening."

**Historical customer concentration.** When the expert was at Illumina, the 80/20 rule was "inverted": giant genome centers (Broad Institute, Sanger, WashU) drove sequencing. The expert built the fourth-largest genome center at the University of Washington in Seattle, and BGI then became the largest "by an order of magnitude almost." The industry "democratized out" and is now consolidating again around roughly 10 to 20 clinical diagnostic institutions that drive most of Illumina's sample volume and revenue, despite a large global installed base.

**Where Roche could gain.** Instruments into tech-development labs everywhere, core facilities at platform-renewal points, and newer entities, including CROs (contract research organizations) wanting contract sequencing with no entrenched Illumina position.

**Plasmidsaurus example.** Plasmidsaurus, a large service provider, was a major Oxford Nanopore customer, started with plasmid sequencing (popular with pharma and drug developers), spread across Western nations, is moving into Asia Pacific, and began offering RNA-seq last fall. The expert infers that RNA-seq was run on an Illumina system, which the company only recently bought. The expert calls it the best counterexample of a business built on a non-Illumina technology that scaled fast (even though it bought an Illumina system for part of the work). The expert would be surprised if it were not evaluating Roche, or even already had an AXELIOS, but says they have not asked and do not know.

## Industry size and valuation context

These figures are the expert's rough, partly speculative numbers.

| Item | Expert's figure | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Illumina annual revenue | "$4+ billion" | Used as the industry yardstick |
| Oxford Nanopore | Under $500 million for a full year | Inferred from its half-year results |
| PacBio | About $150 million | Expert says "I don't think they're long for this world"; "who needs them?... probably nobody"; could be folded into Nanopore |
| Whole industry in 10 years | Around $5 billion | Expert's guess |
| Roche sequencing in 10 years | $1 billion to $2 billion | "A complete dart at the wall kind of number"; $1 billion described as about 25% of the current market; "isn't crazy" |

The expert thinks the industry doubling its current value over 10 years "might be considered a win."

**Why growth is capped.** Market growth is bound by fixed reimbursement per sample, which must pay for collection, sample handling, logistics, sequencing, analysis and reporting. If it does not change, and the expert does not expect that, growth slows to incremental progress as more patient populations are reached. The industry is 20 years old, so it is "hard to see a dramatic increase in the numbers of applications" not already found.

**Potential upside: epigenetics at population scale.** The next big growth case, in the expert's view, would be large-scale epigenetic sequencing of huge patient populations, linking epigenetics to health, disease and longevity. This could be "an insane growth driver" but needs cross-government funding, "another renaissance in scientific funding," which the expert does not see coming in the US in the next several years. They point to 2008 and the early Obama administration, when a wave of sequencing money produced much of today's industry. The dataset could be "several billions of dollars" of investment, and the expert doubts purely private funding would do it, though pharma could "unite" and fund it.

## Risks and caveats the expert raised

- Roche's chemistry complexity and cost, and the two-instrument workflow.
- Labor cost offsetting any price advantage.
- Fixed reimbursement limiting margin benefits from switching.
- Regulatory validation (LDT, CLIA/CAP) taking six or more years for new entrants and years for switchers.
- Weak research funding in the US and slowing in China and Europe.
- Roche's slow commercial progress and vague pricing since January 2025.
- Possible unknown "shoes left to drop" in product development from any player.
- The expert acknowledges lacking visibility into Roche, Illumina and customers' internal numbers.
