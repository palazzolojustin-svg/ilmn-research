# Channel check with a research-core specialist (hospital-based cellular profiling core): biopharma-driven sequencing growth, weak NIH funding, 100% Illumina NovaSeq X Plus

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-14_Specialist_at_Academic_Research_Core_Facility_Optimistic_About_Sequencing_Growth_Amid_Biop.md` · Date: 2026-10-14 · AlphaSense Channel Check (AI Interviewer) with an anonymous customer-side expert; interview 14 Oct 2026, published 16 Oct 2026 · expert call*

**Summary.** A single-cell genomics specialist at a research-only core facility in an academic hospital says the core's sequencing output rose about 20-30% year over year in Q3 2026 and about 20% for full-year 2026, driven by a few very large biopharma-funded projects, even as NIH grant funding and academic sample counts fell. The core runs 100% of its sequencing on Illumina NovaSeq X Plus, sees no long-read demand, and has not adopted TruPath or Roche AXELIOS, but hopes new competitors push prices down. The expert expects flat volume in 1H 2027 and worries about a decline in 2H 2027 if biopharma projects do not repeat.

## What drives sequencing demand at the core

- The expert named two main drivers: the total cost per usable unit of data, and the application or project the researcher wants to run [Expert, Oct 2026].
- Single-cell sequencing and spatial genomics have recently started to influence demand. The expert says these need specialized library preparation (turning samples into a form the sequencer can read), platform-specific workflows and heavy downstream data analysis. The expert files this under "application."

## Funding mix and funding trends

### Who pays

The expert said 90-100% of projects are funded by NIH (the US federal research funder), biotech/pharma, or philanthropic sources. Asked for a split by money, the expert gave this breakdown, with caveats noted below.

| Funding source | Share of funding (by dollars) | Expert's note |
|---|---|---|
| Biopharma | about 60% | Fewer projects, but much larger in value |
| Federal grants (NIH) | about 30% | The largest number of grants; every principal investigator (PI, the lab head) has at least one, but each is far smaller in value than biopharma projects |
| Institutional or philanthropic | about 10% | Remainder |

The expert framed the percentages as illustrative ("let's say"), based on the money given, not on sequencing volume.

### Biopharma was stronger than expected in Q3 2026

- Biopharma-funded sequencing in Q3 2026 was "stronger than expected" because the core won "a number of very large projects" from local biopharma companies [Expert, Oct 2026].
- The expert's reasoning is that biopharma is now more aware that large amounts of data can be generated from hospital-accessible samples, and that this data is "lucrative." To get access to samples at scale, they must partner with hospitals.
- The expert named three project types, in order of importance:
  1. Data monetization: generating large volumes of sequencing data that the biopharma intends to sell to other companies (the data can also serve their own research).
  2. Disease atlases: large comparative atlases across diseases, for example several autoimmune diseases such as lupus and rheumatoid arthritis, skin diseases, and multiple tissues across a large patient cohort.
  3. Drug testing: biopharma testing its own drugs or treatments on a large number of samples it supplied. The expert said this "came third."
- The Q3 projects were largely single-cell sequencing (reading individual cells) on a large number of samples, with projects involving "100 or 200 samples."

### Grant funding decreased in Q3 2026

- Grant funding available to the researchers the core serves decreased versus the prior quarter. Users told the core that their funding was either not renewed or reduced [Expert, Oct 2026].
- The decline was not field-specific. The core serves immunology, allergy, cancer, animal research and neurology, and the complaints spanned all of them.
- The expert said NIH funding is "harder and harder to get, harder and harder to renew," and that some people had grants "unexpectedly terminated." In the expert's understanding, this stems from the government cutting research funds ("It is out of our control").
- A second squeeze is indirect costs (the share of each grant that goes to the institution's overhead). With inflation and rising costs, the hospital is taking a higher percentage of each grant. This applies to PIs and to cores like the expert's.
- The AI summary labels this a net headwind: reduced federal grant funding plus rising institutional indirect costs are "negatively impacting academic research output."

## Sequencing volumes: results and outlook

### Results

| Period | Direction | Size | Driver |
|---|---|---|---|
| Q3 2026 vs. expectations | Exceeded | n/a | Large biopharma single-cell project |
| Q3 2026 vs. Q3 2025 | Increased | "maybe by 20%," then "20%, 30%" ("a significant increase") | Large projects of 100-200 samples |
| Full-year 2026 vs. 2025 | Increased | "about 20%" overall | Large biopharma-funded projects |

- The expert stressed that the growth came from biopharma and not academia: the number of samples from academic labs "actually decreased," while samples from biopharma-funded studies "showed a major increase" [Expert, Oct 2026].
- On the 2026 forecast, the expert is "generally more optimistic." Volume and sample count are enough to keep the core running ("We should be fine").

### Outlook for 2027 (expert expectation, not fact)

- **1H 2027:** sequencing expected to "remain the same," because the large projects continue and finish "at least through the first half of 2027."
- **2H 2027:** the expert is "more worried" that there will be fewer samples and lower output. They are unsure the core will receive the same biopharma volume as in 2026. If it relies only on academic labs, "then our trends might decrease."
- The expert agreed that a 2H 2027 decline would be a deceleration from the roughly 20% growth of 2026.
- **What would prevent it:** attracting more customers. The expert has no high hopes for more large academic projects given the current academic environment, so growth would have to come from biopharma. Biopharma pays extra indirect costs to the hospital because it is considered an external customer; the expert said these need to be reduced so the core's services are more attractive to biopharma.
- **Spatial transcriptomics** (mapping gene activity within tissue) demand is also increasing at the core, but the expert said the technologies they use "do not require sequencing," so this does not add to sequencing output.

## Instruments and platform choice

- **Fleet.** Short-read sequencing (reading short DNA fragments, a few hundred letters at a time) is 100% Illumina, on the NovaSeq X Plus. The core uses the largest flow cell, 25 billion reads, which gives "the best pricing." Failing that, it uses the 10 billion-read flow cell. A flow cell is the consumable slide on which a run is performed.
- **History.** The core adopted NovaSeq X first, then moved to NovaSeq X Plus "about three years ago." The expert calls it one of the newest models they have.
- **Short vs. long read.** 100% of the core's requirements are short-read. The core has never had an inquiry about long-read sequencing (reading much longer DNA stretches in one piece) in the expert's tenure.
- **Long-read outlook.** The expert does not see demand growing. Long-read advantages they cited were structural variants, complex genomes, microbial sequencing, plasmids, full-length transcripts and haplotypes, but they described it as lab-specific. They said certain clinical biopharma applications could be interested. Short-read is preferred because it is versatile, compatible with existing workflows, high throughput and increasingly cost-effective. "I do not see the trend changing towards more long-read sequencing."
- **High versus low throughput.** The core always seeks the highest throughput because it lowers cost. Even with little data, it batches samples until it can fill a large flow cell. "The larger the flow cell is, with the highest throughput, the lower the cost is."

## Why Illumina is the 100% choice

The expert gave these reasons for running only Illumina [Expert, Oct 2026]:

- A strong, established position, used by the core for five to 10 years and by other local researchers.
- Reliable throughput and mature, well-tested workflows.
- Compatibility with widely used assays: 10x Genomics single-cell (including Flex, a fixed-cell assay), the Visium HD spatial assay, and CRISPR screening.
- Machines available at multiple locations in the area; quick data turnaround.
- A large body of public data sequenced on Illumina, which makes it easier to compare a lab's data with published data.

## Competition

### Vendor share

- No major change in vendor share through 2026 so far, at Illumina or others [Expert, Oct 2026].
- The expert expects no share loss at their own core since they use only Illumina. They are "hoping" new competitors drive sequencing costs down, given the growing cost of large sequencing experiments.
- The expert has anticipated Illumina dropping prices "a little bit" but has not seen it yet ("We are hoping for it").

### Roche AXELIOS (new sequencer; the expert refers to "AXELIOS one")

- Not adopted; the expert is aware of it. They call it "an important new entry to watch out for," chiefly because it brings another sequencing chemistry and gives labs more choices.
- What the expert wants to see before adopting: real-world data on usable output, accuracy, consistency across applications, library-prep compatibility with other platforms including 10x Genomics, reproducibility, and total cost per gigabase of data.
- Open questions the expert raised: whether customers will use it for large workloads or only "small pilots, early access studies," and whether it gets well-tested, established workflows. They noted the initial targets are research institutions, and that clinical applications might gain confidence later. Possible differentiators: speed and (hopefully) reduced cost. They said the market is becoming more application-specific and AXELIOS "could find a new place" in it.
- The interviewer's question referred to a reusable sensor model, instrument price and cost per run; the expert did not give specifics on these.
- **Threat to Illumina:** The expert called it "a good competitor with short-read sequencing" and a "potentially strong competitor for Illumina once it becomes more and more adopted in academic settings," because academic labs can afford to test new chemistry. They hope it eventually drives Illumina's prices down.

### Other vendors on the expert's radar (expert's awareness, not use)

- **Element Biosciences:** named as a new entrant.
- **Ultima Genomics:** "becoming more known in the field of research"; targets very high throughput and low-cost sequencing versus Illumina; compatible with large whole genome studies and single-cell applications.
- **MGI DNBSEQ:** uses DNA nanoball technology; "could potentially be a competitor to Illumina."
- **Thermo Fisher Ion Torrent:** becoming more available in some clinical or small-volume labs, but not in high-throughput labs.
- **Singular Genomics:** "could also have appeared in the market."
- **Oxford Nanopore and PacBio:** the expert has experience with both (long-read vendors) but does not use them at present.

### Clinical versus academic buying criteria

- Clinical labs prioritize validated workflows, fast and reproducible turnaround, quality checks (QC) and meeting regulatory requirements.
- Academic facilities, because of limited funds, prioritize cost, utilization, application flexibility (usable by multiple PIs), fit with grant-funded demand, and versatility across research groups.

## Illumina product roadmap and corporate items

- **NovaSeq X roadmap.** The interviewer stated that Illumina has committed to raising NovaSeq X flow cell output by up to 40% and speeding whole genome sequencing by about 30% (interviewer's statement, not verified by the expert). The expert said this "definitely helps." Instrument speed is "not a major factor" for academic labs because experiments are planned around run time. More data per flow cell is "definitely an important point."
- **TruPath Genome.** The interviewer said Illumina launched it in February 2026, that it removes library prep from the whole genome workflow, and lists at about $395 per sample. The core has not adopted it and "has not had interest in that yet." The expert noted it adds long-range genomic information to the NovaSeq X workflow. They think it could help retain, or move, customers interested in long-range information onto Illumina, since they can stay on the same platform and instrument already in-house, "rather than switching to, let us say, PacBio." This is opinion, not observed behavior.
- **SomaLogic.** The interviewer said Illumina closed the acquisition in January 2026. The expert said it has not changed how much work the core sends to Illumina ("This has been consistent"). They observed that it broadens Illumina's positioning into proteomics and integrated multi-omics, so it "is no longer competing only for sequencing projects," but it has not affected the core.

## Capital spending and instrument purchases

- No plans to replace or upgrade the NovaSeq X Plus in the next 12 months; given limited funding, "we are not trying to invest more money in the instruments."
- No instrument placement plans were cancelled or delayed in Q3 2026.
- The core did expand spatial transcriptomics, buying two 10x Genomics instruments this year: a new Xenium and a new "Atera," which the expert described as a whole-transcriptome, single-cell-resolution spatial instrument. The expert said these do not require sequencing.
- Capital spending on sequencing instruments for 2026 is expected to be flat versus 2025 ("a stable stage where we just do not want to invest more").

## Pricing, discounts and consumables

- **Discounts.** The core saw small discounts only on the 25 billion-read flow cell, about 10-15%, which the expert guessed is an incentive for academic cores to keep using larger flow cells. Otherwise pricing was consistent through the year. Incentive and commitment terms in Q3 were unchanged versus the prior quarter ("We did not see more incentives").
- **Consumables spending (Q3 2026 vs. Q3 2025).** Price overall stayed consistent. Reagent purchases rose with sequencing load, about 20-30% versus 2025, so the increase was volume-driven, not price-driven. Comparable-consumable prices in 2026 were "approximately the same" as 2025, apart from the 25-billion flow cell discount.
- **Stocking.** The core built stock ahead of known large projects, enough reagents and flow cells for "the next, let us say, two months." Current inventory is "just sufficient"; the core avoids stocking for experiments it does not need.
- **Lead times.** Unchanged in Q3; no issues with reagent availability or delivery.

## Vendor support

- Overall satisfied: support is good, response is fast and vendors visit the site frequently. Support levels were unchanged in Q3 versus the prior quarter. The expert did not name any positive or negative outliers.

## Closing summary from the expert

The expert's final points were [Expert, Oct 2026]:

- Short-read sequencing remains deeply embedded in research and clinical labs, and Illumina benefits from mature workflows and a large install base (the number of instruments in customers' hands); Roche and others are potential competitive alternatives.
- Long-read is increasingly application-driven and depends on the individual lab.
- Cost is a major limiting factor on experiment scale, especially for academic labs.
- Single-cell and spatial assays are growing rapidly and shaping sequencing demand, though some spatial platforms (those from 10x Genomics) bypass sequencing.
- For new entrants, "the key question" is commercial adoption: reproducible performance, cost, service and how sustainable the sequencing volume is.
