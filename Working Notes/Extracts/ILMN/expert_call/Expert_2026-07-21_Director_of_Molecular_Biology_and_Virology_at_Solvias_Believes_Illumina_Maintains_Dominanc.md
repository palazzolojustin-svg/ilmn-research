# Expert call with a Solvias/Divergene molecular biology director (ex-Roche sequencing market development) on Illumina's clinical NGS dominance, Roche SBX, Element/Ultima, SomaLogic, Fluent and MRD

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-21_Director_of_Molecular_Biology_and_Virology_at_Solvias_Believes_Illumina_Maintains_Dominanc.md` · Date: 2026-07-21 · Expert network transcript (published 23 Jul 2026; interview 21 Jul 2026), customer-perspective expert, buy-side (investor-led) client; names not given · expert call*

**Summary.** A long-time NGS customer (clinical diagnostics and CRO labs, former Roche global sequencing market development manager) says Illumina's base is still solid and that competitors mostly sell research-use-only (RUO) instruments, following the same playbook Illumina once used against Thermo Fisher. He calls Roche's SBX "another choice," not a revolution, and is unsure Illumina's diversification (SomaLogic, Fluent) is smart, citing workflow and cost hurdles. He advises Illumina to focus on a few areas and sees point-of-care sequencing as the next big thing.

# Expert call notes: Illumina's position in clinical NGS, competition and diversification

## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

- The expert is a **customer** (perspective label: "Customer"; client is an investor-led, buy-side analyst). They are not an Illumina employee [Expert: NGS customer, Jul 2026].
- Roles listed in the bio: founder and career strategist for PhDs at The Pivot Lab; General Manager at Divergene, responsible for scientific vision and for ensuring analytical methods are coordinated across oncology and cell therapy programs. They mentor a scientific team refining high-precision assays such as CRISPR and NGS (next-generation sequencing, the high-volume DNA/RNA reading technology). They also choose lab technologies that raise throughput and shorten time to clinical milestones. The article headline places them as Director of Molecular Biology and Virology at Solvias.
- Former Global Sequencing Market Development Manager at Roche: gathered customer feedback, did competitive analysis, fed customer input to R&D for product changes and worked with sales on early-access customers. The bio does not give dates for this role.
- The expert says they have worked in clinical diagnostics and CRO (contract research organization, an outsourced lab-services firm) for about 15–16 years, mainly in NGS. They have been a customer of Illumina, PacBio and Oxford Nanopore, overseeing labs that used each vendor's reagents (the chemical kits) and sequencers for many years. Their current company applies NGS to cell and gene therapy.
- Most of what follows is direct experience, conference conversations or peer hearsay; the expert flags where they are unsure.

## Illumina's current position

- The expert says Illumina is "still pretty solid in terms of the base" [Expert, Jul 2026].
- Low throughput: Illumina recently launched new chemistries and platforms, including the MiSeq i100. The expert has seen many customers converting from the older MiSeq (which Illumina "sunsetted," meaning discontinued, a few years ago) to the MiSeq i100.
- High throughput: Illumina is "pretty well covered" with the NovaSeq X and the older NovaSeq 6000 generation.
- Molecular diagnostics: Illumina "still has a very deep reach and very broad adoption" in NGS. The expert says that today 80–90% of the clinical diagnostic NGS space is dominated by Illumina.
- Expert's view: Illumina is an ecosystem rather than just an instrument seller. They sell instruments, reagents, bioinformatics (including DRAGEN, Illumina's data-analysis software) and cloud solutions, so runs can be largely automated through to a report.

## Competition: Element, Singular, Ultima, MGI and others

- Segments matter: the expert asked whether "market" meant molecular diagnostics, research use only (RUO, instruments not cleared for patient testing) R&D, or multi-omics (measuring several molecular layers like DNA, RNA, protein).
- Element, Singular and others focus on specific R&D applications (for example spatial and single-cell, which look at molecules within or across individual cells and tissue positions) rather than competing head-on with Illumina in diagnostics. The expert gives two reasons: their instruments are not designed for diagnostic throughput, and they chose other applications at launch. Roche's new sequencer is the exception that may enter diagnostics.
- The client noted that Illumina's biggest market is diagnostics consumables more than capital sales; the expert agreed that Element is "very opportunistic" in diagnostics: it would not turn away a diagnostic company wanting to adopt it, but the strategy is RUO first.
- The playbook (expert's description, from what he has seen with most instrument makers): build credibility in R&D with white papers and key opinion leaders (KOLs, respected academic users), then expand slowly into clinical use. Clinical diagnostics has only a few applications, narrowly focused, because everything must be validated on human samples that lead to a diagnostic report.
- Historical precedent: Illumina did exactly this. Earlier, Thermo Fisher dominated clinical diagnostics with the S5 and PGM sequencers; within the last 10 years the share shifted to Illumina.
- Instruments launch as RUO and then get a locked-down "Dx" version: same chemistry, but the software and some elements are locked and traceable for diagnostic use only.
- Pricing pressure question: asked whether price-sensitive labs (start-ups, academic labs) buy cheaper options like Element, the expert answered mainly about Illumina: academic core labs (shared genomic centers serving many labs in one institution) are buying Illumina machines, driven by single-cell and other omics applications. Single-cell and spatial are not clinical yet because "there's not an action that the physician can take"; he foresees possibly five to 10 years before a few applications become actionable. He gave no data on Element share or pricing.

## Roche SBX (sequencing by expansion)

- Expert opinion (from colleagues, conferences, leaders): SBX is "not revolutionary," it is "another choice," and people don't have to adopt it. SBX, NovaSeq and Ultima are "pretty much on the same class of instrument": high throughput, fast whole-genome turnaround.
- Roche's strategy, as the expert reads it, targets specific applications: MRD (minimal residual disease, detecting leftover cancer after treatment) and neonatal whole-genome sequencing, where fast turnaround is critical because "you cannot wait a few days or a week."
- Economics: SBX needs high volume to be cheap. With few samples it is "pretty costly"; a batch of a few dozen up to about 48 or close to 96 samples per run can lower cost "drastically." It therefore does not make sense for applications that do not need fast turnaround.
- Reusable flow cell (the consumable chip where sequencing happens): the client said others describe reusing it about 20 times to save cost, raising contamination risk. The expert explained that the cell is a nanopore design and washable, like Oxford Nanopore's, so it is "not revolutionary." Washing extends turnaround, because pore throughput declines each wash and more time is needed for the same depth; there is a limit on washes. It does not necessarily cause errors, but the quoted seven-to-eight-hour turnaround would not be possible after many washes, which matters for NICU (neonatal intensive care) use. There may also be limits on how many samples can be batched on a washed flow cell, which can be rate-limiting.
- Size: reportedly big, "pretty comparable with the NovaSeq X," not benchtop, so buyers will compare it with Illumina's X (heard at conferences, not first-hand).
- Bioinformatics: the expert has heard of limitations and says he is still watching how sequencing-bioinformatics integration works out. He noted Roche's original 454 sequencer (the first commercial NGS machine, before Illumina) "fell" on integration and the large dataset, not on chemistry. Buyers need an instrument integrated into clinical workflow; raw data "is only going to be ATGC" until pipelines and a person do primary, secondary and tertiary analysis to produce a report. He sees little marketing from Roche on pipelines, "unlike Illumina" with DRAGEN and its wider ecosystem.

### Roche and Foundation Medicine

- The client asked whether Roche could switch Foundation Medicine (which Roche owns, and which probably uses Illumina sequencers) to its own sequencer or bundle products.
- The expert: there is collaboration to run some assays on SBX, but they need intensive validation (for example MRD). Foundation is a service company; Roche is an instrument OEM (original equipment manufacturer) selling directly to hospitals, reference labs and pathology labs.
- He sees Roche as one company covering the entire buyer's journey: hospitals lacking in-house volume send samples to Foundation; high-volume hospitals bring a Roche sequencer and kit (chemistry and panel) in-house. The same kit would be run internally at Foundation and sold to hospitals.

## Diversification: SomaLogic (proteomics)

- The expert agreed Illumina is expanding but said, "I don't know if it's a smart move or not," because good technology can fail to fit workflows.
- Per a conversation with Illumina, SomaLogic library prep requires buying (or rent-to-buy) a Tecan liquid handler (robot for pipetting), priced in the "mid six-figures." It is configured only for this library prep and cannot be used for other applications.
- The product manager said this purchase is "one of the biggest hurdles" to adoption. Illumina is targeting service providers with large volume, such as GENEWIZ (now part of Azenta Life Sciences) and Novogene (which the expert believes is Chinese), plus a few smaller ones, and perhaps some core academic labs. Labs running a few samples per month would not benefit.
- Clarified: the best business case is a CRO/service provider with enough volume from many academic labs nationwide. The expert does not think Illumina wants to be that service provider: he has not seen Illumina or any OEM succeed that way because "it's a completely different business model." They are "still a tool" seller.

## Diversification: Fluent (single-cell) and 10x Genomics

- Expert's reading of the Fluent acquisition: Illumina is covering the upstream single-cell workflow, which was dominated by 10x (10x's instrument partitions cells; sequencing then runs on Illumina). This would end dependence on 10x's machine. Depending on another instrument can cost some quality and flexibility, and Illumina could design workflow upstream for single-cell and spatial to control quality and turnaround.
- Multi-vendor workflows lead to vendors blaming each other when quality problems arise; customers just want good data fast. Better coverage of the workflow is good if affordable and fits current practice.
- Monopoly question (the client referenced the forced divestiture of GRAIL): the expert said it is not monopolistic today because 10x still dominates single-cell. Fluent was "very small" when acquired, and 10x usually targets higher-throughput customers while Fluent is on the smaller side, with different chemistry utility.
- 10x has had "a few layoffs" in recent years, and the expert thinks they see headwind because they are no longer the only company. The single-cell market is "pretty competitive."
- Throughput: most single-cell customers are lower throughput (discovery work). Core labs, service providers and CROs accumulate higher volume, but single-cell and spatial remain relatively low throughput until they become clinical diagnostics.

## MRD, CGP and data models

- Illumina's roadmap (expert's view): "they definitely want to be the winner" in MRD and CGP (comprehensive genomic profiling, broad tumor-gene testing). Many companies already run CGP-related assays on Illumina. MRD is the "second wave" after CGP 5–10 years ago and "has a lot of potential growth," but Illumina is no longer the only instrument company able to do MRD, so it is competitive.
- Winner criteria: not just the best instrument but smooth, transparent end-to-end workflow including bioinformatics, reporting and automation, since the output requires interpretation.
- Data-selling question: the client noted companies in the area say profitability is hard because of poor reimbursement, and that Tempus AI and Caris test broadly and sell data. The expert agreed for CGP, which uses whole genome or exome and generates a lot of data, but said it is less applicable to MRD.
- MRD explanation: it tracks a specific mutation after therapy, usually single or few genes, with very deep coverage (whole-genome sequencing would dilute reads). It can detect one mutated cell in one million, hence "minimal" or "measurable" residual disease.
- He does not see Illumina becoming a data seller. Illumina's customers (Tempus or another clinical lab) will, using Illumina or other machines, including for clinical trials and pharma biomarker discovery. CareDx and others also use this model.

## Expert advice to Illumina

- Rather than "going into every direction" (single cell, spatial, all multi-omics), pick and choose a few and do them well ("they do whatever they want").
- Improve the turnaround time of the chemistry and make the instrument "super small." His "magic wand" idea: a PCR-like machine in every physician office delivering answers in about 30 minutes, with cartridge-in, button-push operation, built-in interpretation and no bioinformatician, so physicians need not send samples to Foundation or service providers and wait one to two weeks. He thinks that "market will be huge."
- Current reality: NGS is still complicated, with library prep needing weeks of training, so it is "just impossible" in a physician's office now. Oxford Nanopore's MinION is iPhone-sized and used for environmental testing or virus testing (for example in South Africa) but still needs bioinformatics knowledge. His conclusion: "Point of care, I think that's the next thing."
