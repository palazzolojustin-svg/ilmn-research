# Expert call with Oxford Nanopore Sales Director (EMEA Central): long-read momentum in Europe and Illumina's vulnerable share

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-23_Sales_Director_EMEA_Central_at_Oxford_Nanopore_Technologies_Believes_Long-Read_Sequencing.md` · Date: 2026-10-23 · Expert-network channel check (published 27 Oct 2026; interview 23 Oct 2026); expert is a Sales Director EMEA Central at Oxford Nanopore Technologies (competitor); questions from a buy-side investor-led interviewer · expert call*

**Summary.** A competitor-side expert (Oxford Nanopore) argues that long-read sequencing is gaining momentum in Europe as past bottlenecks (accuracy, end-to-end workflows) are solved, and that Illumina's European share will fall from about 75-85% to 55-60% over three to five years even as the market grows. The expert says Illumina keeps strengths in clinical entrenchment, workflows, accreditation, solid-tumour work and price-sensitive commercial labs, but is hurt by inflexibility, the MiSeq discontinuation and strong rivals (PacBio, ONT, Roche AXELIOS, Element, Ultima). The expert openly says they may be biased and declined to comment on several topics.

# Expert call: Long-read momentum in Europe and Illumina's market position

## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

- **Relationship:** The expert is a **competitor** (Sales Director EMEA Central at Oxford Nanopore Technologies, "ONT"). The call was classed as a competitor perspective with a buy-side (investor-led) interviewer. Interview date was 23 Oct 2026; published 27 Oct 2026. Experience is current.
- **Career:** Molecular biologist by training, with a PhD and postdoc at the German Cancer Research Center. They then moved into industry to focus on next-generation sequencing (NGS, high-volume DNA reading).
  - About six years at **GenDX** (Netherlands), an NGS-based transplantation company. They supported customers across Europe and later globally on HLA (immune-matching gene) sequencing, supporting **Illumina, PacBio and ONT** applications.
  - Then **Bionano Genomics** (San Diego), responsible for part of Germany.
  - Three years ago they joined Oxford Nanopore, covering **27 Central European countries**: the DACH region (Germany, Austria, Switzerland) directly, and **24 Eastern European countries** through channel partners (distributors).
- **Bias disclosure:** Speaking about labs adding long reads on Illumina, the expert said, "I might be a bit biased here." They are a long-read vendor, so their views on Illumina's weaknesses should be read in that light.
- **Direct experience vs speculation:** Statements about European lab behaviour, workflows, German reimbursement and customer sentiment come from direct sales experience. Percentages (10-20%, 80%, 50%, 15-20%, 10%, market-share forecasts) are the expert's estimates or guesses, not measured data. The expert said they could not comment on SomaLogic economics or the US-versus-Europe growth gap.

## Adoption of long-read sequencing (long reads read much longer DNA stretches than short reads)

- **Share of new projects:** The expert estimates roughly **10-20% of new applications in Europe** are based on long-read. The market is still "very entrenched" with Illumina applications built over the last decade. [Expert: ONT sales director, Oct 2026]
- **Growth engine:** A large growth driver for long-read companies is also existing applications currently covered by Illumina, not just new ones.
- **Pace:** Adoption has "definitely accelerated significantly" versus two years ago, because many long-read bottlenecks were addressed in the last two to three years. Long-read is moving from narrow dedicated uses to applications "100% occupied" by short-read. The main earlier obstacles were **accuracy** and the **availability of end-to-end workflows**.
- **Evidence cited:** The expert says financial data from long-read companies shows particularly high growth in clinical, which "automatically means that market share is moving from short-read into long-read."
- **5% of the genome claim:** Asked about Illumina's statement that long-read is mainly needed for about 5% of the genome (regions hard to map with short reads), the expert said the statement "is not wrong," but long-read can serve the same purposes as short-read with better data. Once implemented, it can replace the remaining short-read work too, giving the lab a consolidated approach.
- **Switching a lab's whole-genome work:** Takes **three to 12 months**, depending on resources, existing infrastructure, and ability to implement new bioinformatics and lab information systems. Labs can run both side by side first, starting with selected applications and expanding.

## Clinical applications

- **Pharmacogenomics (PGX, how genes affect drug response):** Long-read is a clear advantage; it answers questions short-read "simply couldn't" because of the length of some genes analysed. The expert called PGX one of the most important spaces for personalized medicine.
- **Rare disease:** Long reads suit repeat expansion disorders (very long, complex repeated DNA), and can also give modification information (chemical marks on DNA). PacBio and ONT provide this; Illumina "at the current stage cannot provide as simple as the other providers."
- **Oncology:** Must separate hematologic malignancies from solid tumours. For solid tumours there are still significant long-read bottlenecks that only short-read addresses. The expert said this is an area where "Illumina will stay strong also in the next couple of years."
- **Pathology:** Short-read is the method of choice and likely stays so for several years. Not because short-read is better, but because sample storage (paraffin-embedded, chemically treated tissue) damages DNA so reads cannot be long. A switch would require moving to fresh-frozen storage, which "takes decades in the labs."
- **Going back to short-read:** The expert said it is too early for a final answer (selected applications are transitioning to long-read now), but given long-read's value it is "really unlikely" labs return to short-read.
- **Complementary use:** For dedicated applications, labs may always want a long-read instrument beside a short-read one (such as NovaSeq X), which opens the door for long-read to replace short-read step by step.

## How locked in is the clinical base?

- The interviewer cited Illumina's statements that clinical is **65% of consumable revenue (reagents needed for each run)** and growing mid-teens.
- Expert: clinical is "relatively locked in" because processes, bioinformatics and accreditation are in place and switching is hard, but it is a step-by-step process.
- Untapped growth exists where short-read plays no role, such as **biopharma applications** still done with legacy methods; long-read can address these, giving growth in areas where Illumina is not active.

## Barriers to replacing an Illumina sequencer in European clinical labs

1. **End-to-end workflows:** Labs need a clear process to implement an application, get results and interpret them. Illumina built this over about 10 years and it is a main reason for its success.
2. **Bioinformatics:** Historically built on Illumina data.
3. **Pricing:** Long-read has been more expensive, which matters for reimbursement. While pricier, it is harder to enter markets against cheaper short-read.

### IVDR (EU in-vitro diagnostic regulation)

- The expert called the topic "really hard and really controversial" and said IVDR is "cooked hotter than it really is," because the market is still in transition and labs mainly need an **accredited** solution rather than an IVDR one. Accreditation has been built over years for Illumina, which is its main advantage.
- Illumina has an advantage in IVDR-compliant workflows, but it does not go beyond its existing dominant share. For the future, the expert sees an "equal chance for all companies" to meet IVDR requirements.

### Dragen (Illumina's bioinformatics software)

- Not a barrier. "Dragen is nice, but it's not a necessity"; many software tools work across providers. Legacy solutions are still mostly Illumina-based, but they are expanding and slowly adding long reads, which will open doors for long-read. "No, Dragen is not a reason why to stay with Illumina."

## Hospital/public vs commercial labs; Germany

- Behaviour depends on the country. In **Germany**, government-funded initiatives let **public labs only** use any sequencing solution they want, with high reimbursement of about **EUR 3,500-4,500 per whole-genome run**, enough to cover short- or long-read costs. The programme runs three to five years (expert: "I believe"). The interviewer identified it as the **genomDE Initiative**; the expert confirmed.
- genomDE is **platform agnostic**, but rollout was slow, so investments were made three to five years ago when Illumina was more dominant; many labs bought Illumina equipment to participate. Because of the high reimbursement, most sites are still open to trying other methods, mainly long-read.
- **Commercial/private labs** are far more cost-sensitive ("green numbers in the books"). Better price comes with better utilization, so Illumina remains very strong there, owing to cheaper short-read and implemented workflows. Commercial labs with large R&D infrastructure may differ, but the expert sees very few willing to invest heavily in long-read R&D.

## NovaSeq X and instrument placements

- **Replacement mix:** The interviewer noted about 100 NovaSeq X placements in Q2 (interviewer's figure). The expert estimates **about 80% in Europe are former NovaSeq (6000) customers**, since outside the UK (a centralized market) the European market is decentralized and most labs lack capacity for big NovaSeq X machines.
- **Volume effect of lower cost per genome:** The expert guesses **no more than 50%** of accounts truly increase utilization with the new instrument; most European labs already ran at full capacity, and the other half is unlikely to see significant growth.
- **Supply contracts:** Consumable supply contracts are usually **one to three years**.

## Illumina's long-read offering and reputation

- Illumina's complete long-read prep kit (runs on existing instruments) is "perceived in a positive way," but lacks valuable unique selling points versus other long-read suppliers, so value is "probably not what Illumina wants it to be."
- Expert: "no more than 10%" of Illumina labs wanting to add long reads would consider Illumina's solution, instead going to PacBio or similar, depending on budget.
- **Reputation:** Perception of Illumina "has suffered in the last years" because it uses its market position. Specific complaints (expert's view, mostly Europe):
  - Equipment was never discounted; Illumina "always got what they wanted" on price, with no special offers and inflexibility on end-user pricing.
  - Extreme inflexibility on support, delivery prolongation and the overall customer journey.
  - Discontinuing the **MiSeq** (small benchtop sequencer) is "a huge disaster for many clinical labs," with no discounted replacement devices or flexibility on outstanding deliveries.
- Customers are "really seeking to replace Illumina" since good alternatives exist, and "people are happy to switch to the competition if they can."

## Switching intent vs actual switching

- Fewer than **50%** of Illumina customers would say they are happy with Illumina as a company (expert's estimate).
- Actual switching today is **no more than about 15-20%**, but increasing, depending on application and urgency.
- Roche **AXELIOS 1** is "severe competition against NovaSeq X." In single-cell, Roche has a "very unique value proposition."
- A pathology lab running specific gene panels could easily switch to **Element** for the same result at lower price.

## Competitive vulnerability

The expert said Illumina "will lose market share. It's just a matter of how quick." Reasoning: Illumina succeeded as a monopolist; in the last two to three years serious competitors have emerged. Per application:

| Area | Expert's view of Illumina's position |
|---|---|
| Long-read | Not competitive against ONT or PacBio |
| Single-cell | Not competitive against Roche or AXELIOS |
| Panels (price per sample) | Not competitive against Element |
| Huge population-scale projects | Ultima has a "fantastic value proposition"; Illumina can't compete |

"They still own the market because of legacy, but they will not keep the market as it is."

## Market share forecast

- Illumina's European share is currently about **80% (range 75-85%)** and the expert expects a fall to **55-60% over three to five years**. The overall sequencing market keeps growing significantly, but "Illumina will lose its monopoly."
- Asked whether Illumina still grows, the expert said it will "remain where they are right now": maybe light growth, probably a slight decrease, "overall stable."

## Strengths, challenges and other topics

- **Challenges for Illumina:** the competitive landscape and the maturation of bioinformatics for alternative suppliers (the main bottleneck for rivals).
- **Where Illumina stays strong:** the cancer market, commercial service providers who need the lowest price per sample on established systems, and labs that can build on Illumina's infrastructure; elsewhere it "will for sure lose."
- **SomaLogic acquisition (proteomics, protein measurement):** The expert called proteomics "super interesting" but said it will not give an immediate return on investment; alternatives like Olink exist. They declined to judge market size, growth or price.
- **Europe vs US clinical consumable growth:** The interviewer said the US grew above 20% while Europe was slower. The expert could not comment much, saying only it "will probably go back again."
