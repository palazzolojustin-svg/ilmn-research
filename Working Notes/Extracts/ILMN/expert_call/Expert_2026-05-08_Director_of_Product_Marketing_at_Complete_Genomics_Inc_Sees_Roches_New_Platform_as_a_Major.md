# Complete Genomics product marketing director sees Roche's new nanopore-based short-read sequencer as the biggest threat to Illumina

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-05-08_Director_of_Product_Marketing_at_Complete_Genomics_Inc_Sees_Roches_New_Platform_as_a_Major.md` · Date: 2026-05-08 · Expert network call (transcript published 12 May 2026; interview 8 May 2026). Expert: Director of Product Marketing at Complete Genomics Inc (competitor perspective, ex-Illumina product manager). Questions from a business-led client analyst. · expert call*

**Summary.** A current Complete Genomics executive, who previously managed Illumina's diagnostic sequencers (MiSeqDx, NextSeqDx), argues Illumina remains the leader through innovation, cost structure, reputation and a clinical ecosystem that is hard to switch away from. He calls Roche's new platform (nanopore-based, using Xpandomer, short-read output, $150 genome) the number-one threat, estimating a possible 20-30% short-read share in a couple of years, though Roche's pricing disappointed those who feared a price war. He also describes the loss of China as Illumina's first "materialized" threat and Illumina's likely responses (assays, instrument discounting, end-to-end solutions).

# Expert call notes: Complete Genomics executive on Illumina and the Roche threat

## Expert background and perspective

The expert is a Director of Product Marketing at Complete Genomics Inc (CG). The call is tagged as a "Competitor" expert perspective with a "Business-Led" analyst perspective. Interview date was 8 May 2026 and it was published 12 May 2026. [Expert: Complete Genomics product marketing director, May 2026]

**Career history (direct experience):**
- Educated in clinical diagnostics in China, then earned a master's degree in molecular biology.
- Worked five years on the Human Genome Project using Sanger sequencing (the older, slower method of reading DNA), almost 30 years ago. They say they finished about 1% of the first human genome sequence. They have worked with DNA since about 1998.
- Earned an MBA and moved from the lab to business as a product manager at MJ Research and Bio-Rad Laboratories (PCR, qPCR consumables and liquid-handling devices; PCR is a method that copies DNA, qPCR measures it as it copies). Later worked at molecular diagnostics companies including Beckman Coulter and Asura Molecular (spelling as transcribed), using qPCR to detect pathogens.
- Joined Illumina in 2019 and spent four years managing its diagnostic sequencing platforms, MiSeqDx and NextSeqDx.
- Joined Complete Genomics about three years before the call (around 2023). They manage the product team covering sequencers, automation and spatial products (tools that map gene activity within tissue).

**Relationship to Illumina:** Former employee (left roughly 2023) and now a direct competitor. They say they still talk with former Illumina friends and a former boss, and many former colleagues now work at Roche. Their Illumina knowledge of recent strategy is partly second-hand through those contacts.

**Complete Genomics context (expert's account):**
- CG's parent is MGI, a Chinese company that acquired CG in 2013. The expert says MGI/CG are "considered as the biggest competitor to Illumina on the short-read sequencing" worldwide. (Short-read sequencing reads DNA in small fragments; long-read reads much longer stretches.)
- Illumina kept CG out of the U.S. for years through IP litigation. The expert says Illumina "actually lost the lawsuit," and before the loss the two settled and cross-licensed everything. Illumina paid over $300 million to CG as part of the settlement.
- CG re-entered the U.S. in 2023, which is why it recruited the expert.
- The last three years have been difficult in the U.S. because of geopolitical tension and legislation, pending in Congress and at state level, that seeks to ban MGI or Chinese companies more broadly from selling sequencers in the U.S. Even so, CG achieved year-over-year revenue growth.
- An announcement earlier in 2026 said MGI will divest CG to a Swiss company. The expert hopes the ownership change means CG will no longer be seen as a national security threat.

## Market growth and applications

- New technologies start in research labs and then move into clinical diagnostics (testing used for patient care), which is now the main driver of sequencing demand. [Expert, direct observation plus recollection]
- When the expert left Illumina, Illumina said about half its revenue came from clinical diagnostics. The expert thinks the latest earnings put it at around 60% (approximate recollection, not a verified figure).
- Pharma uses sequencing more often in drug development, which is another growth driver.
- In research, spatial and single-cell applications (analysing individual cells or gene activity in tissue) are the major driver and need a lot of sequencing. The expert says this is a major attraction drawing customers to CG's platform, since CG has a spatial business. These applications have not yet entered diagnostics.
- In diagnostics, oncology (cancer) is now the biggest driver. Prenatal screening was previously the largest clinical application. Germline genetic disease and rare disease testing, especially newborn testing, are also growing.

## Long-read versus short-read

- The expert says long-read is "fundamentally" better in principle, drawing on Human Genome Project experience with hard-to-assemble repetitive regions. Short reads nevertheless dominate the market because they have higher accuracy and higher throughput. Long-read has remained a supplementary technology. The expert gives no figure for long-read's long-term market share.
- Bioinformatics helps stitch short reads into longer sequences. Both Illumina and CG are developing "synthetic long-read" technology, which assembles short reads into long ones and could blunt long-read's advantage.
- **Where long-read has the advantage:** de novo sequencing (building a genome with no reference genome to match against; with a reference, short reads can simply be aligned, reducing long-read's value); highly repetitive disease-relevant regions such as SMA (spinal muscular atrophy); antibody-encoding DNA, which is highly variable; and 16S bacterial sequencing (about 1,500 bases, longer than short reads). Long-read is popular in microbial work where accuracy matters less.
- **Why long-read is unsuitable for cancer:** variants may be present in only 1% or 0.1% of cells, so accuracy is critical, and long-read errors could be mistaken for real mutations.
- For infectious disease identification (e.g., flu vs COVID), the signal is a patch of sequence, so higher error rates are tolerable. The expert says bioMérieux and other microbiology labs tend to use long-read sequencers that are smaller, faster and cheaper.

## Illumina's competitive position and moats

The expert says Illumina is still the market leader and that many competitors have come after it without taking most of the share. Reasons given:

1. **Innovation.** Illumina still leads. It consolidated bridge amplification (a step that copies DNA clusters on the chip) onto the sequencer, and the new "TruPath" method can integrate library prep (preparing DNA for sequencing) onto the sequencing chip, streamlining the workflow. The MiSeq i100, launched a couple of years ago, offers room-temperature shipping and storage, which customers welcomed; the expert says rivals such as MGI and CG watch Illumina's features and copy them. Illumina keeps investing heavily in R&D.
2. **Reputation and vendor viability.** Smaller competitors, such as Element and Ultima even without political pressure, raise doubts about whether they will survive. Customers use sequencers for critical tasks and fear a vendor going bankrupt and leaving instruments unsupported. Illumina does not have that problem, and it exploits this doubt in selling.
3. **Pricing flexibility.** Competitors target Illumina's high prices. CG and others price at only 20%-30% of Illumina's price. The expert says Illumina has lately become much more willing to discount, especially for large customers, and that its prices are high by choice, not because its costs are high. With its volume its cost is "pretty low," though not as low as MGI's China-made products. If it sacrificed margin, it could match competitors.
   - The expert says sequencing volume has risen significantly over several years while Illumina revenue stayed flat, and down in some years, because of discounting to retain volume. This hurts margin but is also a weapon: "If they really want to wipe out competition, they have the financial strength and the cost structure to do that."
4. **Ecosystem.** Over 20 years Illumina has built an ecosystem of applications (oncology tests, prenatal screening) on its platform, including a number of FDA-approved assays. Once an assay is FDA-approved and clinically validated, customers do not want to change it even for better technology. The expert calls this "a huge advantage."

## Roche as the number-one threat

**Expert's view of why Roche matters:**
- Roche is not going away and has strong reputation and financial strength. The expert says Illumina people used to call Roche "the tomb of the sequencing" because Roche launched and abandoned several sequencing initiatives, but "this time, it looks different."
- All three product managers for Roche's new sequencers came from Illumina or were the expert's former Illumina colleagues, which makes it look credible. Many Illumina staff, including decades-long VPs and directors, now work at Roche; the expert says most people at Roche's conference booth are ex-Illumina.
- The expert says the technology is "a lot more promising than previous attempts by Roche." Roche also benefited from talent leaving Illumina after its downsizing following the GRAIL acquisition.
- Roche previously tried to enter the market several times and was defeated by Illumina, and even attempted a hostile takeover of Illumina that failed (expert's account).
- Expert's conclusion: "the Roche is a formidable threat, or number one threat to Illumina." Technology is the main reason, more than Roche's size.
- Illumina's internal attitude, per the expert's conversations with former colleagues: "let's wait and see," because many "new technologies" have failed before.

**How the Roche technology works (expert's explanation):**
- Illumina's technology is sequencing by synthesis (SBS), which extends DNA one nucleotide at a time. MGI/CG, Element and Ultima use SBS variants too, though CG's library prep differs and avoids bridge amplification.
- Roche uses nanopore technology (threading DNA through a tiny pore and reading it electrically), which is what Oxford Nanopore uses for long reads. Nanopore's usual problem is that DNA passes too quickly, causing higher error rates.
- Roche uses a "Xpandomer" (transcribed once as "expander") to lengthen the DNA molecule so it passes more slowly and each base is recognized more accurately. Accuracy is still not high enough alone, so Roche performs "duplex reads," reading each strand twice, back and forth, to reach accuracy comparable to short-read sequencing.
- Because of this, the Roche system cannot do long-read; it uses long-read technology to deliver a short-read result.
- Advantages: lower cost (no enzymes for synthesis) and much faster speed (no many cycles of synthesis).
- Whether the technology is proven "remains to be seen," but early-access customers are very happy (expert's statement).

**What would prove the platform (expert's view):** Customers want consistent, good-quality data across many applications; Roche partnered with 10x Genomics as an example. Growing adoption and customer satisfaction would show viability. The expert notes past technologies that looked good conceptually but failed in practice. It is unknown whether Roche's platform will be universal or specialized for specific diagnostic assays; because it sits in Roche's clinical diagnostics group, the expert expects a clinical focus, although early access work with 10x is research-based.

## Roche adoption, pricing and share

- **Target customers:** The Roche instrument is expensive and equivalent to Illumina's largest production-scale instrument. The expert expects initial customers to be large industrial customers or heavily funded genome academic labs with the funding and sample volume. Smaller labs and hospital labs are probably not priority targets. Some CG customers are considering the Roche platform.
- **Pricing:** Roche's reagents (consumables, the kits needed for each run) are comparable to CG's pricing, per the expert. Roche's cost is probably much lower, but Roche needs very high margin to fund R&D and corporate overhead; the expert would not be surprised by a reagent margin of 90% or above (speculation).
- Roche announced a "$150 genome." The expert says everyone had "sighs of relief" and that Illumina's stock rose during Roche's presentation. A sub-$100 price would have been more aggressive, and "$50 genome, that will be devastating to everyone." The expert reads $150 as Roche not wanting a price war; Roche will compete on differentiation, technology, service and reputation. Roche is less aggressive on price than MGI or CG; the expert calls pricing MGI's "weapon of choice."
- **Pace of adoption:** "I don't think it's like tomorrow, everybody will switch to them... it will take some time."
- **Share forecast (expert's estimate, partly citing field bloggers):** Roche may take 20%-30% of short-read sequencing in a couple of years. The first year probably minimal (maybe 5%-10%), then 30%-40% by years two and three. If very successful, Roche could reach 40%-50% and overtake Illumina. The expert sees 20%-30% as the critical mass: if Roche caps there, its technology is only good for a segment and Illumina still dominates with its loyal base; if it exceeds that, it could "take the market by storm."
- A 20%-30% share would make Roche the number two player, "the biggest threat" in the expert's words.

### Challenges for Roche

- **Switching costs:** Customers like Foundation Medicine, with FDA-approved pipelines on Illumina sequencers running many patient samples, are unlikely to switch to an unproven platform. They might only pilot it, which could take a few years.
- **Early adopters** are likely leading academic labs that like new technology and want to publish. Such use is not production-scale, so reagent volume would be low. The expert notes most revenue for a sequencing company comes from reagents, so low pull-through means slow revenue growth, which the expert sees as Roche's major hurdle.
- Roche's history of failed sequencing ventures also weighs on it, and Illumina will cast doubt on an unproven technology.
- **Q1 2026 NovaSeq X Plus sales:** The expert says Illumina sold the NovaSeq X Plus (its high-throughput instrument) "extremely well" in the first quarter, "80 or 60" units (uncertain recollection). They suggest some customers may have been waiting for Roche, but after the announcement may decide to stay with Illumina as the safer option.

## Illumina's two threats: China and Roche

- **China (already materialized, per the expert):** Growing China was one of five pillars of Illumina's strategy and the only country named. For the expert's product line, annual sales once launched in China were double the rest of the world combined. Because of geopolitical issues and MGI and other Chinese companies now producing quality products, "Illumina's China market is decimated," and Illumina was banned in China, collapsing a revenue growth engine. Illumina pivoted to "more sequencing value," and growth in other markets mostly compensated.
- **Roche:** The expert says it could be as big a threat as China. Illumina once viewed MGI as the biggest competitor, hence its litigation and political lobbying to keep MGI out of the U.S. Now "Roche is the biggest threat," and if Roche wins significant share, "which is highly likely," Illumina "will lose quite a bit."
- **Mitigant:** Market expansion, especially in clinical diagnostics. Downstream customers such as GeneDx, Natera, Guardant Health and GRAIL (which Illumina divested) have grown testing volumes at double-digit rates, indicating a fast-growing market that could offset share loss.

## Expected Illumina responses (expert's opinion)

1. **Build the ecosystem and assays.** Illumina mostly works with customers who develop assays on its platform but has some internal assays. Sequencing cost is falling steeply; the expert cites a customer application where sequencing costs about $0.20 per sample, versus about $1 per sample for plasticware like tubes and tips, so value is shifting toward library prep, analysis and other workflow components. Illumina is pushing end-to-end solutions, which the expert says was part of the drive to acquire GRAIL. The expert thinks that direction was sound (testing brings more revenue than instruments and reagents) but it "failed because of some antitrust and misstep," and execution was problematic.
2. **Push TSO500** (Illumina's oncology assay, TruSight Oncology 500). The expert says it is "not the best assay on the planet, especially from workflow perspective," but Illumina is promoting it aggressively to lock in customers, particularly hospitals that rarely change validated workflows (validated for 10-20 years).
3. **Sacrifice instrument margin** to place instruments and lock in customers, since reagents and consumables are the main revenue driver. The expert says Illumina "should be willing" to do this.
4. Expand into the testing market, such as TSO500 and prenatal testing, which is a much larger market than selling sequencers and reagents.
