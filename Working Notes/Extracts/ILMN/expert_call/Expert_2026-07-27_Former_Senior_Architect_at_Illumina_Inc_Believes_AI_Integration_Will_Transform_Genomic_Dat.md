# Former Illumina Senior Architect on AI/LLM Natural-Language Data Querying and the Shift of Genomics Value from Sequencers to Analytics

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-27_Former_Senior_Architect_at_Illumina_Inc_Believes_AI_Integration_Will_Transform_Genomic_Dat.md` · Date: 2026-07-27 · AlphaSense expert call (interview 27 Jul 2026, published 29 Jul 2026); expert is a former Illumina senior/chief software architect (unnamed); interviewer from a buy-side (investor-led) perspective · expert call*

**Summary.** A former Illumina software architect (12 years at Illumina, 3 years at NextBio before that, left August 2025) describes a proof-of-concept AI agent he built that lets customers ask plain-English questions of their genomic data, converts them to SQL, and returns answers in 5-20 seconds across about six trillion data points. Management embraced it and it was pushed to pre-alpha production before he left. He stresses that validation is essential because LLMs are probabilistic (in his demo, 5 of 10 answers were wrong), and he argues that over 5-10 years sequencing will commoditize and Illumina's value should shift to analytics and an "Amazon for DNA" platform. Much of this is his own opinion or speculation, not company guidance.

## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

The expert is a **former employee** of Illumina (ILMN), and the interview was conducted from an investor-led (buy-side) perspective [Expert: former Illumina senior architect, Jul 2026]. Key facts about their experience:

- They worked at Illumina for **12 years** and describe themselves as a chief software architect for the data analytics that handle data generated by Illumina's sequencers (the instruments that read DNA). The AlphaSense bio lists the title as "Senior Architect," reporting to a Director. They left in **August 2025**, so their experience is roughly 11 months old at the time of the call.
- They were part of the **connected data analytics team**.
- Before Illumina they spent **three years at NextBio**, a company later acquired by Illumina.
- Illumina career had two phases: about **three to four years managing a team of five** who developed DNA algorithms for customers, and **eight to nine years as an individual contributor** in a large team, acting as software architect for how software and data are managed and how to build scalable algorithms that run on any number of samples.
- Per the bio, they designed, implemented and deployed scalable genomics algorithms and production pipelines for DNA and gene-relationship analysis, including validation and verification. They are now adjunct faculty teaching big data modeling and analytics (distributed computing, MapReduce, Spark/PySpark, Amazon Athena, Google BigQuery) at a business school, and can speak to Spark, Snowflake, Athena and large-scale genomic pipelines.
- They state they hold a **PhD in computer science** and have worked on AI/LLM for about **four years**. They say they know DNA/RNA data well but that domain knowledge sits with researchers and scientists, not computer scientists.

Most of what follows about the proof of concept is **direct experience**. Views on pricing, competition, the long-term strategy and "Amazon for DNA" are the expert's **opinion/speculation**, and they themselves call the long-term revenue shift "premature maybe."

## What the expert liked and found hard about the job

- They enjoyed that customers gave feedback at every stage. Algorithms were built from requirements supplied by many clients who would deploy them in production, not because an engineer liked a given algorithm.
- A recurring challenge was that **clients sometimes did not know what they were looking for**. Example: customers wanted to find rare variants (uncommon DNA differences) and patterns, but had no clear idea how they would use or deploy the results. For well-known algorithms customers knew what they wanted; for uncertain needs they did not.

## The gap the expert saw: fixed algorithms versus open data exploration

- The expert says Illumina historically developed and deployed **fixed sets of algorithms** that customers click and run. What was missing, and what AI/LLM makes possible, is opening a platform (API) so customers can explore their own data and ask their own questions.
- Opinion: "I think the era of developing the fixed set of algorithms is going to go away." Well-known algorithms will remain, but customers "should have access to their own data by using AI LLM," which also requires validation and standardization.

## The proof of concept (POC) and its status

- About one year before leaving (so roughly mid-2024 to August 2025; the expert says "just one year before I left"), they built a POC of AI/LLM integration on a set of data. It was **not built for a particular customer**; they demoed it to managers and senior managers.
- How it works: a user asks a question in **plain English**, an AI agent translates it to **SQL** (the standard database query language), the SQL is executed and validated, and the user sees the result.
- Scale and speed: the data involved is about **six trillion data points**, and queries return in **5 to 20 seconds**. The expert notes this is not real-time (not one or two seconds).
- Management reaction: the expert says they did not expect management to embrace the idea, but management did, and **before they left they pushed the component to pre-alpha in production**. The interviewer's recap (that management embraced it and pushed it to pre-alpha production) was confirmed as "very correct."
- Technology: the LLM used was **Snowflake's built-in AI/LLM** (Snowflake being the cloud data platform Illumina's data sat in). They did **not** use OpenAI or Anthropic; the expert says those were "not even available for us to integrate" at the time.
- The expert expects a learning period: customers will need time to master it and there will be errors at first, but eventually the platform will be more useful to clients and "will benefit Illumina." No revenue, pricing or launch-date information was given.

## Value proposition for customers

- Customers are given **metadata** (the table names and column descriptions, not the raw data volume) so they can see how their data is organized and ask informed questions.
- Example: a researcher looking for a rare variant on chromosome 17 with a specific allele (a version of a gene/DNA letter) could ask directly, without clicking an algorithm the expert wrote years earlier.
- Expected benefits: more valuable new research and new product creation; customers feel empowered; they learn more about their own data. The expert says Illumina's clients are very intelligent and ask the right questions, but algorithm development takes time and customers lacked access to their own infrastructure.
- Cycle time: scientists would no longer need to spend **seven to eight weeks** telling Illumina what they want; they could test new ideas on their own data and get SQL generated and run for them. The expert calls this "amazing turnaround time." The interviewer noted validation might add some time back; the expert agreed validation is still required.
- Trust: the expert says it would build trust between Illumina and customers, who would have full access to ask any question of their data. Quote: "If I was the customer of Illumina, I will demand this feature."
- Examples of simple questions customers could ask: "How many of my patients are between age 10 and 20, and they are Hispanic?" and which patients have a particular allele on chromosome one. Customers could even use speech recognition rather than typing, and would not need to go to Illumina or any other company to develop algorithms.

## Data security and architecture

- The interviewer suggested that rather than exposing sensitive data to an outside LLM, using Snowflake's data lake with its embedded LLM is more appropriate; the expert agreed ("You're right").
- Each customer can access **only their own data** unless data is shared. The expert says security was very important and they implemented features so customers cannot see each other's data. Some **public data** was accessible to all customers.

## Challenges: non-determinism, hallucination and validation

- The expert says LLMs are **probabilistic, not 100% deterministic**, so validation is crucial. They repeatedly stress that "validation is the key."
- Example of failure: a request for information about patients can produce very different results depending on whether the SQL uses a **left join versus an inner join** (ways of combining tables on common IDs; the expert likens tables to spreadsheets). A wrong join or wrong ID gives a result that looks valid but is not what the user intended.
- Causes of wrong answers: the asker may not pose a proper question or give the LLM enough context; with minimal information the LLM will hallucinate (invent a plausible but wrong answer). With a proper prompt and proper metadata, the expert says the chance of a correct result is "over 90%," but "that 10% is a problem." Quote: "There are no AI LLM models which can give you 100% correct answers. None." They say they could give "hundreds of examples" of wrong output.
- In the demo they showed **five questions answered correctly and five answered wrongly** (10 total). A user who does not know SQL might think all 10 results were fine.
- Implication: customer **education** is key. The more users know SQL and their metadata, the better they catch errors. Illumina and its customers need a platform for validation and authentication of results to confirm the output matches intent.
- Closing message: validation of AI-generated code or SQL is key to using AI/LLM anywhere; without it hallucinations produce wrong answers.

## Limits of AI agents: well-defined algorithms still needed

- Some tasks, such as finding relationships (mother, father, child, parent-sibling) among **hundreds of thousands of DNA samples**, are well-defined algorithms that "cannot be done by just an AI LLM agent in a very simple manner." They must be developed and deployed for many clients, with long development and testing cycles.
- Ad hoc, simple exploratory questions are where AI agents can add value, alongside the standard algorithms.

## Product roadmap idea: generate analysis-ready data at the sequencer

- Problem the expert saw at Illumina: a DNA sample from a sequencer yields about **5 million rows** of data. The analytics team then runs ingestion algorithms that expand each row to roughly five or six rows, producing about **30 million records** suitable for querying.
- Each ingestion can take **15 to 20 minutes** in a cluster environment.
- Recommendation: Illumina should add modules to the sequencer so it generates the 30 million query-ready records directly, bypassing ingestion. The expert calls this "a big optimization" and a major problem Illumina needs to fix. This is the expert's proposal, not a stated company plan.

## Example customer dataset

- The expert recalled a set of **5,000 autism samples from Stanford University**. They describe this as small data: 5,000 samples x 30 million records is about **15 billion data points**. Giving researchers AI/LLM access to such data would let them "do wonders" and ask many questions, which researchers would prefer over a fixed set of algorithms.
- In response to the interviewer's personal comments about autism, the expert said opening such data to researchers could open "a new avenue" toward cures, medicines or other discoveries not possible with fixed algorithms. Speculative opinion.

## Pricing, competition and the long-term shift in value

- Pricing/costs: the expert thinks **sequencers and data generation are becoming cheaper**. No specific prices were given.
- Competition: they note competition "from different companies such as Roche or other companies" generating short reads and long reads (types of sequencing output). No further detail on Roche or other competitors was given.
- Opinion: in the long run the **value of DNA data will be in analytics, not generation**. "In long run... the sequencers and data generation will be commodities. Anybody can generate that." Over **five to 10 years**, Illumina should think about generating revenue from data analytics components rather than the sequencers. The expert admits this is "premature maybe."
- Value examples they cite: analyzing a pregnant mother's data to learn about the baby so problems can be fixed "at the root," and querying whether a DNA sample has autism-related genes. A platform with LLM and AI for data queries would be more valuable than raw sequencing.

## Competitive strategy: "the Amazon for DNA"

- Opinion: software, integration and ease of use are "the foundations." Data without an environment to analyze and view it is useless.
- The expert says "Illumina has to be the Amazon for the DNA data": customers come, pick and select services for their data, and **pay based on usage**. Illumina would need a pricing formula, for example by megabyte or terabyte of data.
- What it would take: putting data into the repository should be as easy and painless as Amazon services; storing, viewing and analyzing should be transparent; a reliably integrated environment customers trust; ability for customers to bring in **other data Illumina does not have**; and AI/LLM enabled so customers can examine their own data.
- Retention argument: if Illumina only offers applications and fixed algorithms, customers will eventually want to do things on their own rather than relying on Illumina. "Giving the full access to their own data is the first fundamental rule in keeping the customers."
