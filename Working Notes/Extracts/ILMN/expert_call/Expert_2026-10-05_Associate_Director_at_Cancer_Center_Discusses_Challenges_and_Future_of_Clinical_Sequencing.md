# Cancer center molecular diagnostics lab director on clinical sequencing volumes, a possible NovaSeq X Plus trade-in, Caris competition and Roche Axelios readiness (channel check, Oct 2026)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-05_Associate_Director_at_Cancer_Center_Discusses_Challenges_and_Future_of_Clinical_Sequencing.md` · Date: 2026-10-05 · Channel check expert call (AI interviewer), published 2026-10-09; expert is a customer: Associate Director of the Molecular Diagnostics Lab at a Cancer Center in Pennsylvania · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer who runs a CLIA-certified cancer center lab (about 50 samples a week, 100% Illumina on NovaSeq X Plus and NextSeq 2000) says Q3 2026 volume was below expectations but up 10-20% year over year, and expects about 10% growth in 2026. Leadership change has stalled two newly validated high-volume assays (a 1,500-gene panel and a ctDNA MRD assay) and may lead the lab to abandon them, so the expert expects volume to fall about 10% in 2027 and the lab is discussing trading the NovaSeq X Plus for two NextSeq 2000s. The expert sees Illumina as dominant in clinical sequencing, Roche Axelios 1 and Oxford Nanopore as not yet clinically viable, and reports no change in Illumina pricing, discounts, lead times or support.

## Expert background and how to weigh this call

- **Who:** Associate Director of the Molecular Diagnostics Lab at a cancer center in Pennsylvania. The channel-check blurb says they have visibility into operations and purchasing for sequencing platforms including Illumina and Oxford Nanopore. The expert later refers to "Temple Health" acquiring community hospitals, which suggests the health system is Temple Health (inferred from that remark, not stated outright).
- **Relationship to Illumina:** Current customer. The interview is a channel check (a structured survey of a customer about their usage, buying and views of vendors), conducted by an AI interviewer on 2026-10-05 and published 2026-10-09 [Expert: cancer center molecular diagnostics associate director, Oct 2026].
- **Recency:** Fully current. The expert talks about Q3 2026 and plans for the next 12 months.
- **Direct experience vs. speculation:** Lab volumes, instruments, pricing, discounts, lead times, support and the internal decision process are first-hand. Views on Roche's Axelios 1 are secondhand ("I heard they launched... this year") and speculative. The expert did not know the lab's annual output in gigabases (the unit of sequencing data) and answered in samples instead.
- **Small sample caveat:** This is one mid-sized clinical lab (about 50 samples per week) whose 2027 outlook is driven by internal leadership and budget decisions, not by market trends.

## Lab profile

- **Volume:** Roughly three batches a week of about 15 samples each, so about 50 samples per week. The expert did not know the gigabase figure.
- **Tests run:** A comprehensive cancer panel of about 300 full genes covering solid tumors and heme malignancies (blood cancers), plus a fusion panel (detecting genes fused together by rearrangements) of about 1,000 genes, also run on the sequencer. The lab also uses a small solid-tumor panel from Archer.
- **Customers:** Patients of the cancer center and affiliated hospitals in the health system. About 80% of patients are the cancer center's own.
- **Regulatory status:** CLIA-certified (the US federal standard for clinical labs) and CAP-certified (College of American Pathologists accreditation). It is a clinical lab, not research-use-only.
- **Application mix:** About 90% of samples are oncology tumor profiling on the comprehensive panel, with RNA fusion testing run on the same samples. There is no standalone inherited-cancer assay, but the lab asks oncologists to send tumor and normal tissue so inherited-cancer results can be reported from the same run. The remaining roughly 10% is pharmacogenetics (how genes affect drug response) such as DPYD germline testing for cancer patients, plus NIPT (noninvasive prenatal testing) done in collaboration with the OB/GYN department.

## Headwinds and tailwinds to volume

**Tailwinds (expert's account of own lab):**
- About six months ago the health system adopted a policy that physicians ordering NGS (next-generation sequencing) tests should go to the in-house clinical lab first and send out only if the lab lacks the panel or gene. Cancers such as breast and colon were previously sent out and can now come in-house.
- The lab validated a new 1,500-gene panel, which the expert likens to, but not quite, a whole exome (all protein-coding genes).
- The lab also validated a ctDNA (circulating tumor DNA from blood) assay to monitor MRD (minimal residual disease, cancer remaining after treatment), which requires very deep sequencing.

**Headwinds:**
- Physician inertia: once physicians are familiar with another company's assay, they do not want to switch. The lab is still working to win samples back.
- Leadership change in the department means both new assays, though validated and with reports written, have not launched.

## Application growth outlook (next 12 months)

- **Fastest:** DPYD pharmacogenetic testing, which the expert expects to grow very fast because the FDA approved a requirement (the interviewer described it as a black box labeling requirement) linked to the chemotherapy 5-FU, so every 5-FU patient needs DPYD testing. Volume has already increased a lot.
- **Slowest:** The comprehensive cancer panel, described as only slight growth.
- **Others:** Inherited cancer should stay as before, since it rides on the cancer panel. NIPT validation has not started but is planned.

## Instruments and short- vs. long-read mix

- **Fleet:** One Illumina NovaSeq X Plus and one NextSeq 2000 in the clinical lab. Oxford Nanopore (long-read) access comes only through the institution's research core facility, used for pilot studies on special tests Illumina cannot handle.
- **Mix:** 100% of clinical output runs on Illumina short-read. Nanopore is research only.
- **Long-read outlook:** No plan to move clinical tests to long-read. The pilot found markers that short-read could also detect, and the barriers are cost and the lack of any clinical validation work.
- **Why 100% Illumina:** The expert says Illumina is "very dominant in clinical sequence market," at least in the US, and a cancer center will not touch a sequencer "which have not been used in the clinical sequence for many years." The lab is "very conservative." No Roche, Element, Ultima or other machines.
- **No share change:** Vendor share did not change in Q3 2026, and the expert expects Illumina to keep 100% over the next 12 months.

## NovaSeq X Plus economics and a possible trade-in

- **Division of work:** The NovaSeq X Plus has very high capacity and suits whole-genome or large panels. The NextSeq 2000 has lower capacity but faster turnaround. The plan was to move most cases of the new 1,500-gene panel to the NovaSeq X Plus, while small panels (fusion, Archer) stay on the NextSeq 2000 to keep turnaround short.
- **The problem:** The lab still uses the 1.5 billion-read flow cell (the consumable that holds the sequencing run) on the NovaSeq X Plus and does not have enough volume to fill the 25B flow cell. Therefore the NovaSeq X Plus does not save the lab money.
- **What would fix it:** Running the 1,500-gene panel, whole exome, whole genome or the ctDNA assay would allow bigger flow cells and lower cost per sample. The expert says that was why the lab bought the NovaSeq X Plus.
- **Current discussion:** With both assays effectively abandoned, the lab is discussing trading in the NovaSeq X Plus for two new NextSeq 2000s. No decision has been made. If both assays are eventually run, the lab may keep the NovaSeq X Plus.
- **Performance view:** The expert is happy with the NovaSeq X Plus. A bridging study against the older NextSeq 500/550-class machines showed higher accuracy, attributed to XLEAP-SBS chemistry (Illumina's newer sequencing chemistry) and the new flow cell design.
- **Roadmap commitments don't help:** Told that Illumina has committed to raising NovaSeq X flow cell output by up to 40% and whole-genome speed by about 30%, the expert said no, it would not increase use, because volume, not flow cell capacity, is the constraint.

## Q3 2026 and forward volume

The expert gave these figures for the lab's own sequencing output.

| Period | Expert's view | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Q3 2026 vs. expectations | Below expectations | Some physicians still send samples to Caris; own 1,500-gene panel not launched |
| Q3 2026 vs. Q3 2025 | Up about 10-20% | In-house policy; Temple Health's acquired community hospitals now send samples |
| Full-year 2026 vs. 2025 | Up about 10% | Originally expected about 20%, which depended on the new panel launch |
| 2027 vs. 2026 | Down about 10% ("significantly decrease") | Both new assays likely abandoned; replacement validation takes time |

- **Caris:** The expert named Caris (a commercial tumor-profiling company) as a competitor for samples because it offers whole exome and whole transcriptome on the same sample, which some physicians like. The lab's 1,500-gene panel would be the in-house answer, but it is not launched.
- **Sentiment shift:** Q3 made the expert more pessimistic about 2026. Per the expert's understanding of discussions with department leadership and the lab director, the lab may drop the validated panel and validate a new comprehensive cancer panel from another company, which takes about half a year. The expert expects volume "not decreased, but cannot grow" over the next six months.
- **2027 logic:** The ctDNA assay, on which the lab spent a lot of money and finished validation, is also likely to be abandoned, and the expert is unsure whether a new ctDNA assay will be validated. Even so, the expert said "the whole sequence cost will be decreased a lot" next year. The interviewer summarized, and the expert confirmed, that the swing is from 10% growth in 2026 to a 10% decline in 2027.

## Roche Axelios 1

- **Adoption:** Not adopted. The expert says Roche launched it this year and sees it as aimed at academic or research labs, since its reagents and software have not been used in or approved for clinical testing (secondhand knowledge).
- **Path to clinical use:** Roche would need a partner clinical lab to validate the instrument, reagents and data-analysis software, with secondary and tertiary analysis compatible with software clinical labs already use. The expert does not expect their own lab to be able to use it even next year.
- **Illumina's moat:** Illumina has been in clinical diagnostics for many years, and its DRAGEN ecosystem (Illumina's data-analysis software) supplies secondary and tertiary analysis tools that are easily adopted. The expert says this makes Illumina "very strong in this field."
- **Time and cost of adopting a new platform (from own experience with the NovaSeq X Plus):** Installation took about one to two months because the building is old and required IT and cross-department coordination. Including state registration, about half a year until the machine was ready for testing. After that "everything goes very smoothly." Earlier in the call the expert said a new assay validation takes about half a year. The interviewer's summary, which the expert confirmed, was close to a year or more before Axelios could be used clinically.

## Illumina products and pricing mentioned

- **TruPath Genome:** The interviewer said it launched in February 2026, removes library preparation from the whole-genome workflow and lists at about $395 per sample. The expert reacted to a figure of "$300" per sample as "very attractive" and noted that the lab's cost for its small 300-gene panel is similar. The lab has not adopted it or discussed it; this was a personal observation.
- **Effect on long-read demand:** TruPath does not change the long-read picture. The expert remains interested in long-read, for example Nanopore, for markers hard to catch with short-read: FLT3-ITD (a blood cancer mutation), IGH translocations and DNA methylation. If third-generation sequencers cut cost per sample, they would be "very attractive." The expert's ideal is one test, one run and one report covering single nucleotide variants, copy number, structural variants, translocations and methylation, because physicians dislike receiving results in separate reports a week apart.
- **SomaLogic (Illumina's proteomics acquisition, closed January 2026 per the interviewer):** Probably no effect on the lab, whose work is traditional markers (single nucleotide variants, copy number, fusions) that guide treatment.

## Purchasing, spending and pricing

- **Instruments:** No plan to buy any new sequencer in the next 12 months, because of budget. No orders for reagents or machines were cancelled in Q3 2026, but no purchase is planned next year. Instrument discounts and incentives were unchanged versus the prior quarter.
- **Capital spending 2026 vs. 2025:** Expected to stay about the same, given no new instruments. The expert also said overall sequencing cost will increase in 2026 versus 2025 because volume is up.
- **Consumables spending (reagent kits bought for each run):** Up about 10-20% in Q3 2026 versus Q3 2025, tracking volume. The expert said it was "pretty much driven by the volume, because we didn't get any further discount from Illumina." For full-year 2026 versus 2025, spending was said to be up about 10%, again from volume, with Illumina's price "not big difference compared with 2025."
- **Internal inconsistency to note:** At one point the expert said reagent cost would stay the same in 2026, and later gave a roughly 10% increase attributed to volume.
- **Discounts and terms:** Reagent discounts and commitment terms from Illumina were unchanged in Q3 2026 versus the prior quarter.
- **Inventory:** The expert first said "insufficient," then corrected to say there is enough reagent on the shelf, so inventory is sufficient and not a supply issue.
- **Lead times:** Unchanged in Q3 2026 versus the prior quarter.

## Vendor support

The expert is "pretty satisfied" with Illumina customer service, on both sales and technical sides ("they are good"), and support levels were unchanged versus the prior quarter. No outliers were named.

## Emerging technologies the expert is watching (next 12 months)

- **Roche:** The expert hopes Roche does more clinical validation and wins adoption at a big commercial or academic clinical lab. The expert's understanding is the machine is good and the total price is low.
- **Nanopore:** The expert hopes its cost falls and accuracy improves.
- **Aim:** The expert hopes both will eventually compete with Illumina so more clinical labs adopt them. This is a hope, not a plan.

## Risks and signals for Illumina from this call

- **Negative (one account):** A possible downgrade of the lab's footprint, from a NovaSeq X Plus (high-throughput) to two NextSeq 2000s (mid-throughput), which would still keep the lab entirely on Illumina but lower consumable use per instrument. Consumable demand at this lab is expected to dip about 10% in 2027 if the assays are abandoned.
- **Neutral to positive:** 100% Illumina share, no switching intent, stable pricing, discounts, lead times and support, and satisfaction with the NovaSeq X Plus's accuracy.
- **Competitive:** Axelios 1 and Nanopore are not seen as clinical options for at least a year; the nearer-term competitor for this lab's samples is Caris, a testing service, not another instrument vendor.
