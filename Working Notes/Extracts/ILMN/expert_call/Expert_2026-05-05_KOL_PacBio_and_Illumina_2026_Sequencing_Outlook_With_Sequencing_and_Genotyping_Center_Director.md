# Academic sequencing core director on PacBio and Illumina: 2026 outlook, NovaSeq X skip, Revio reusable SMRT Cells, AVITI competition, TruPath skepticism

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-05-05_KOL_PacBio_and_Illumina_2026_Sequencing_Outlook_With_Sequencing_and_Genotyping_Center_Director.md` · Date: 2026-05-05 · Guidepoint expert call; expert Brewster Kingham (Director, Sequencing & Genotyping Center, University of Delaware); moderator Andrew Carmen · expert call*

**Summary.** A university core-lab director (customer of Illumina, PacBio, Element and Oxford Nanopore) says 2025 was his busiest revenue year, partly because grant holders pulled sequencing spend forward amid federal funding uncertainty, and 2026 is running similar. He chose not to upgrade to the NovaSeq X because of its cost of ownership and fill-rate challenges, sees Element's AVITI gaining share against Illumina's mid-throughput NextSeq 2000, and is highly skeptical of Illumina's TruPath as a rival to PacBio's Revio. He is positive on Illumina's 5-Base solution and single-cell products, and expects PacBio's reusable SMRT Cells to cut his spend initially but expand demand over time.

# Expert call notes: PacBio and Illumina 2026 sequencing outlook (Guidepoint, 2026-05-05)

## Expert background and basis of views

- The expert is Brewster Kingham, Director of the Sequencing & Genotyping Center and Associate Scientist at the Biotechnology Institute, University of Delaware. The viewpoint is labeled "Academic KOL - Physician Scientist" (KOL = key opinion leader, an outside specialist whose views are sought). The moderator was Andrew Carmen of Guidepoint, an expert-network firm. The call was about 26 minutes and was a repeat call ("once again", "great to have you back").
- Relationship to Illumina: he is a **customer and user**, not a former employee or competitor. He runs a core laboratory (a shared facility that sequences samples for many researchers, internal and external, for a fee). He reports no change in his role since the last call. He also manages the "Next Generation Testbed Laboratory" for a partner biopharma institute.
- Tenure: he says his career spans "almost 30 years", so he has seen long-run NIH funding cycles.
- Instruments he mentions: Illumina NovaSeq 6000 and NextSeq 2000; Element AVITI; PacBio Revio (bought before the prior call); Oxford Nanopore MinION and PromethION 2. He is in PacBio's CSP program (Certified Service Provider, where PacBio lists and refers customers to approved labs).
- Direct experience versus speculation: his lab's revenue, instrument usage, purchase decisions and project mix are first-hand. His forecasts of funding and spending are explicitly uncertain ("I really don't know"). Statements on Illumina's internal motives and the NovaSeq X economics at other providers are inference. He did not directly review NextSeq 2000 data.

## Demand, revenue and funding trends

**Short-read trends.** [Expert: Kingham, May 2026] He sees no significant change in short-read (sequencing of short DNA fragments, Illumina's core approach) demand versus last year. Interest and spend continue to grow in single-cell (profiling individual cells) and spatial (mapping gene activity within tissue) technologies. In his lab, every single-cell and spatial experiment "funnels right into primarily short-read sequencing". Interest is growing in using long-read sequencing for single-cell and spatial work.

**2025 was a record.** [Expert: Kingham] 2025 was the busiest year he has had for lab revenue, which he says is the opposite of what many would have expected. 2026 is running "similar to last year", with no dip so far.

**Why: spend pull-forward.** [Expert: Kingham, his interpretation] Because of federal-level uncertainty about priorities, researchers pushed spending forward, fearing the government might claw money back. Example: a multi-year award (2 to 3 years) might allocate $200,000 for sequencing, normally spent roughly equally each year. Instead, much was spent early. His lab, as a major resource for such money, saw a surge.

**Expected dip.** He expects a dip in the "near to mid-term future" because award funding is fixed; money spent early cannot be spent later. He has not yet seen one.

**Caveats on the funding outlook.**
- Over almost 30 years he has seen large year-to-year swings in NIH and other federal research spend, but "even if overall spend goes down, spend per year for genomics research always goes up." He lacks granular insight into whether genomics budgets are flat or falling.
- A year earlier he had expected funding to get tough; instead the lab was overloaded with work. This makes him "hesitant to try to predict" the near or mid-term.
- On NIH: despite the administration's "bluster" about proposed cuts, he thinks NIH's budget "went up about 1%" last year (his recollection). He says mid- and lower-level decision-makers are making strategic choices, and the share allocated to genomics seems to always rise. He attributes this partly to evidence that returns on genomics investment exceed other biomedical research.

**Capacity constraint.** Bandwidth, effort and turnaround time limit his lab. He believes he could take on more projects and earn more revenue if turnaround improved. Investigators prioritize cost and turnaround, and turnaround is currently what suffers. The bottleneck is staffing: "it is much easier for me to buy a $1 million machine than it is to hire a low-level lab technician" in academia.

**Customer mix.** About 65% of last year's lab revenue was external (non-University of Delaware), which he says matches his prediction. He calls this unusual versus most university cores because he actively recruits external customers and has international visibility. A lot of internal NIH-funded users are those rushing to spend.

## Instrument purchasing and the NovaSeq X decision

**Capital scrutiny.** [Expert: Kingham] Core labs face "much higher scrutiny and a much higher baseline for justifiable purchasing of capital instrumentation." He cycled through upgrades before 2025 and has no critical upgrade needs; if he did, it "would be a significant problem." He has bought no instruments since the last call.

**NovaSeq 6000.** He uses it very rarely. Only the highest-output S4 flow cells make financial sense; the three lower tiers cost more and give lower data quality than running on his NextSeq 2000 or Element AVITI. The 6000 is there because a partner biopharma institute bought it and covers the service contract, which is "the most challenging expense" of that system and would be of the NovaSeq X.

**Not upgrading to NovaSeq X.** He does not intend to upgrade. He says it is the first Illumina instrument for which upgrading did not make sense; historically the benefits of each newest, highest-output Illumina instrument outweighed the downsides. His reasons:
- Purchase cost and cost of ownership are "far too high."
- Illumina's cost-per-gigabase claims (cost per billion bases of data) reflect only the highest-output runs. If he ran only those, he would run the instrument about 8 to 12 times a year, hurting turnaround; running lower output forfeits the savings.
- He would struggle to fill the machine and would have higher expenses.
- Illumina does not go into this "granular detail," and "you could say that they could be more open about it."

**Customers going elsewhere.** Some University of Delaware investigators send high-throughput work to third-party sequencers, some he knows of and some he does not. He feels "a little bit of hurt feelings" but stands by his decision. He believes they would still outsource even if he owned a NovaSeq X, because those providers run almost exclusively top-output runs and have economies of scale.

**Possible exception.** The NovaSeq X could still come into his lab via the biopharma partner, who would cover the service contract.

## Short-read competition: Element AVITI versus Illumina

- [Expert: Kingham] He has had the AVITI a little over 2 years. By his recollection of usage graphs, AVITI usage shows "gradual but noticeable growth," while Illumina in mid-throughput (desktop NextSeq 2000) would be "either flat or significantly lower growth" than AVITI. He did not directly look at NextSeq 2000 data.
- AVITI24 (Element's upgrade): an on-instrument upgrade to his existing AVITI, not a new instrument. List price $150,000; he is "quite sure" he could negotiate to about $100,000. It is his number one wish-list item if he can justify the purchase.
- Pricing: mid-throughput short read is "the most competitive space" in genomics, with "crazy aggressive pricing." Both Illumina and Element are "very aggressive in trying to retain business," which benefits him as he plays them against each other.
- For small runs, he says the NextSeq 2000 or AVITI is cheaper with higher data quality than the NovaSeq 6000 low tiers.

## PacBio: Revio, CSP and reusable SMRT Cells

**CSP program.** He remains a CSP. It brings him customers, both via his listing on PacBio's website and through PacBio's internal process of connecting inquiring researchers with a CSP.

**Reusable SMRT Cell / new SMRTbell prep.** [Expert: Kingham] He is not part of the early-access program and is unhappy about it; he pushed hard, but it did not work out and he does not know why. He received the first pre-order email the day before the call and placed a "significant pre-order" within an hour.
- Savings: the SMRT Cell (PacBio's consumable sequencing chip) is "the most expensive component of a PacBio sequencing project," so reuse is "a huge advantage" and will technically benefit all applications. Most benefit: population genomics and projects needing multiple runs.
- Workflow: run one customer's sample, wash, run a second customer's sample on the same cell. Benefit only occurs when samples are queued; his lab usually has 20 to 30 PacBio projects in the active queue.
- Carryover: PacBio told him carryover between runs is "fractions of a percentage." He takes them at their word ("PacBio has always been honest about what we should expect from new technologies") and will know from the first run.
- Utilization and spend: it will not change Revio utilization. It will likely reduce his spend with PacBio for a period (fewer cells bought). He then expects investigators to spend more as savings filter through, citing a historical "build it and they will come" pattern in both long and short reads: lower cost per unit of data leads to more data generated and larger future grant requests.

**Applications moving to Revio.**
- Genome annotation (labeling genes in an assembled genome) has grown hugely in his lab over the past 2 years, probably faster in the past year. Iso-Seq (full-length RNA transcript sequencing) and full-length 16S sequencing (bacterial identification) also grew.
- MAS-Seq (a method concatenating many fragments into one long read) raises data output at the same price by "6 to 12x," driving big output increases for Iso-Seq and 16S. Reusable cells will boost this further.
- His lab's niche is genome assemblies. He calls PacBio HiFi data (highly accurate long reads) "an indisputable must-have" for assemblies, with Oxford Nanopore "a nice-to-have." Iso-Seq data then lifts a good assembly to a higher quality via annotation.
- Methylation: PacBio provides direct methylation data (chemical marks on DNA read without converting the DNA), which he regards as significantly higher value than indirect methylation data, for the same reason true long reads beat pseudo long reads.

## Illumina TruPath (synthetic long-read)

- [Expert: Kingham, opinion] He does not think TruPath will be real competition for PacBio or Revio, and says "I think is not going to work."
- Reasoning: Illumina has tried synthetic or pseudo long-read technologies repeatedly over about 15 years (Moleculo 12 to 15 years ago, another product launched 4 or 5 years ago, now TruPath). At launch, those supported only human genomic sequencing, and he understands TruPath is the same now. Long-read demand is "much more sought after for non-model organism sequencing." True long-read is "invaluable"; pseudo long reads are "a poor replacement."
- He expects some users but thinks Illumina acquires or builds these cheaply, so failure is not "a money pit" and there is no reason not to try. He notes Illumina "did try to buy PacBio" and understands the importance of long reads.
- Moderator's question framing: whether TruPath has any advantage beyond serving someone who already owns an Illumina sequencer; the expert saw none.

## Oxford Nanopore

- His lab has a MinION (small portable device) that "served its purpose" and a PromethION 2, acquired at very low cost in partnership with the biopharma institute. It is used little, mostly jointly with the institute; he would not have bought it for the academic core lab. Idle time is available for academic use but "it does not see much activity."

## Multiomics, single-cell and spatial

- [Expert: Kingham] Interest in multiomics (combining data types such as RNA, methylation, proteomics, chromatin) is significant; many of his recent projects incorporate it, with real advances over the past 1 to 2 years.
- Illumina's 5-Base solution (a method reading standard bases plus methylation in one run) is "really impressive" and, of Illumina's recent launches, the one he thinks "is going to deliver the most to them."
- Illumina's single-cell product: his lab has started using it, calling it "very high quality." He expects the same of Illumina's spatial product (not yet used). Of Illumina's new technologies over the past 2 years, "they've really done a great job," with TruPath the exception.
- Economics: single-cell and spatial projects have a high initial buy-in. In about 95% of his such projects the primary goal is RNA/transcriptomic data. Once that is funded, adding another omic such as methylation or proteomics adds little cost, which is pushing multiomics adoption.
- The moderator mentioned Illumina adding SomaLogic (proteomics) to 5-Base; the expert agreed Illumina is doing well relative to others, with no further SomaLogic detail.

## Closing forward-looking view

- [Expert: Kingham] Interest in single-cell (10 years in the making) and spatial (5 years) has "picked up faster than I even thought" over the past 1 to 2 years, with new announcements and capabilities ramping interest significantly. The moderator proposed discussing this on the next call.
