# Expert call – Director of an academic genomics core facility (South Carolina) on sequencing volumes, Element AVITI24 adoption, Illumina share, pricing and new products

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-30_Director_at_Genomics_Core_Facility_Optimistic_About_Sequencing_Growth_and_Technology_Advan.md` · Date: 2026-07-30 · AlphaSense channel check (AI interviewer; published 2026-08-03); anonymous expert, associate professor and genomics core director at a South Carolina university · expert call*

**Summary.** A core-facility director at a South Carolina university says their sequencing output is up roughly 40% year over year, driven by single-cell work and cheap outsourced NovaSeq X runs. They are buying an Element Biosciences AVITI24 and hope to move at least 80% of in-house sequencing from Illumina to it. They expect Illumina to lose a little share (to Element, MGI, possibly Ultima), see no Illumina price change in the last 90 days, are enthusiastic about Illumina's native synthetic long reads (TruPath Genome), and are skeptical of Roche's AXELIOS. The main risk they flag is a possible large NIH/NSF funding cut in 2027.

# Expert call notes: academic genomics core director, 30 July 2026

## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

- **Who they are.** The expert describes themself as an associate professor in South Carolina who is also the director of a university genomics core facility (a shared lab that runs sequencing for other researchers). They routinely perform and use next-generation sequencing (NGS, the modern high-speed DNA/RNA reading technology) for their own research and as a core duty. The bio notes they oversee core operations, including purchasing of equipment and reagents (the chemical kits used in experiments). [Expert: genomics core director, Jul 2026]
- **Relationship to Illumina.** The expert is a **customer** (a channel check, not a former employee or competitor). They buy a few Illumina kits directly, own two older Illumina instruments, and send most high-volume sequencing to outside providers that run Illumina's NovaSeq X. Seniority is that of a purchasing decision-maker for the core. Their experience is current: the interview was 30 July 2026 and they speak about Q2 2026 and the prior 90 days.
- **Format.** The interview was run by an AI interviewer and published by AlphaSense on 3 Aug 2026. The AlphaSense auto-summary says the expert is optimistic on 2026 volumes but cautious on 2027 federal funding. Most statements below are direct experience; the expert marks forecasts and views of competitors as hopes, estimates or predictions.

## The facility and its customers

- **Customers served.** The University of South Carolina (main campus in Columbia and regional campuses), other South Carolina institutions (Clemson University, Coastal Carolina, Medical University of South Carolina and small four-year colleges), out-of-state customers across the US (even the West Coast), local hospital systems such as Prisma Health, and commercial biotech companies in South Carolina and Georgia.
- **Instruments owned.** An Illumina NextSeq 500 and an Illumina MiSeq (both Illumina sequencers); an Oxford Nanopore PromethION and MinION (long-read sequencers); robotic equipment for library preparation (turning samples into sequencer-ready material); and a single-cell pipeline from cell dissociation through library preparation.
- **Incoming instrument.** The facility recently won NIH funding to buy an Element Biosciences AVITI24 and is in the process of purchasing it.
- **Outsourcing.** The facility uses outside providers extensively for NovaSeq X (Illumina's newest high-throughput sequencer). Providers named: MedGenome and "Somagen" (as transcribed). Asked about PacBio and Ultima outside providers, the expert only confirmed NovaSeq X use.
- **Applications, ranked by the expert.** Transcriptome (RNA) first; then whole genome sequencing, especially low-coverage; then epigenomics (currently DNA methylation, plus CUT&RUN and ATAC-seq, which map DNA-binding proteins and open chromatin); plus a variety of microbiome applications.

## Factors affecting sequencing volume (Q2 2026)

- **Federal funding is the dominant factor.** The expert cites NIH and general federal funding and "great uncertainty and turmoil" around it.
- **Funding uncertainty cuts both ways.** In the short term it may raise volume, because principal investigators (PIs, the lab leaders who hold grants) unsure about funding continuation or a no-cost extension rush to spend money on big projects. On the other hand, some groups did not get an extension or competitive renewal, so their projects are on hold, and overall funding levels are lower.
- **Clinical research sequencing** is an emerging area but "not a main or important contributing factor yet"; the facility hopes to get more funding from it.
- **Technology and demand trends.** Growing demand for complex epigenomics, single-cell and spatial (where in the tissue molecules sit) projects, which the expert says will raise NGS demand long term. A new generation of PIs is more familiar with, and more willing to propose, multi-omics projects combining transcriptomics, epigenomics, metabolomics and so on, not just RNA sequencing.

## Sequencing output and forecast

- **Q2 2026 vs Q2 2025.** Illumina-instrument output (measured in gigabases, billions of DNA letters) is increasing, mainly through outsourced NovaSeq X projects, "especially that many providers like MedGenome are now using 25 billion flow cells" (the 25B flow cell is a higher-capacity consumable). The expert's rough estimate is about **+40%** year over year, mainly from single-cell demand. Output is **exceeding** the expert's expectations.
- **Why single-cell is booming** (expert's reasons):
  1. Single-cell work has become important for securing federal funding and publishing in high-profile journals.
  2. The core invested heavily in cell fractionation service and protocols that help PIs prepare good single-cell suspensions.
  3. Several technologies commercialized to compete with 10x Genomics have cut the cost of single-cell sequencing significantly, in particular library preparation, which led to greater sequencing depth and higher output demand.
- **Full-year 2026 outlook for Illumina-specific output.** The expert expects to shift volume to the AVITI24 once it arrives. Commercial providers use a fixed output (150-pair, as transcribed); many applications don't need it. In-house, the expert expects to use lower output per sample (for example "over 60 P instead of over 140 P", as transcribed) and a higher output per flow cell, so cost per sample falls: "we will use less Illumina and more AVITI24."
- **Share of in-house sequencing moved.** "I hope that at least 80% of the sequencing we will do in-house with AVITI24." The expert stressed this is a hope, not certainty, and that most applications would switch from Illumina to Element.
- **Change vs 90 days earlier.** More optimistic, because several approaches have been funded: "it's going to be a better year than we thought early in the spring." NIH and NSF appear to be working at least through the current budget year.
- **2027 risk.** If a "huge reduction of NSF and NIH" is proposed in 2027, "definitely we will have less customers and significantly less demand for the sequencing."

## Consumables and reagents

- **Direct Illumina kit use is small.** The facility uses Nextera kits (Illumina library prep) only for small Arabidopsis (a model plant) genomes, and Illumina methylation kits. The "workhorse" kits are from NEB ("Biolab" as transcribed) for most work and Zymo for ATAC-seq and microbiome. Illumina sequencing is mostly outsourced to Somagen and MedGenome. If the core switches to Element, "we will use more definitely Element Biosciences products."
- **Spending growth.** Outside providers charge per lane (one flow cell lane), which includes their consumables. Spending rose proportionally with sequencing volume: orders rose about **40% last year**, and this year spending is "at least 40%" higher. For comparison, 2024 to 2025 was about **15%** growth. (The expert's wording is ambiguous on whether the 40% refers to 2025 or 2026; they say both "last year" and "this year, at least 40%.")
- **Provider pricing is flat to slightly lower.** Prices are "very competitive" and didn't change; moving between providers even lowered cost. Quoted costs: Somagen "under $2,0000 per lane" (as transcribed, likely under $2,000); MedGenome under $1,800 per lane; and under $4,000 per lane for a 10B or 25B flow cell lane (as transcribed).
- **Illumina's own kit pricing, last 90 days.** No difference noticed. The expert doesn't know why. They say it is "complicated to work with Illumina": list prices differ from quotes, discounts come through quotes, and Illumina reportedly offers "huge discounts" on quotes for NovaSeq X (not tested by the expert; speculation that large volume discounts go to commercial companies). Overall impression: "Illumina prefers to work with customers and to provide individualized quotes rather than show unified pricing policies."
- **Inventory.** The core carries no consumables inventory; kits are bought and used on the project.

## Equipment pricing and competitive landscape

- **Instrument pricing.** The Element system cost less than the facility proposed in its NIH application a year ago. Illumina sales reps offered some discounts on NextSeq 2000 systems when the core discussed them.
- **Oxford Nanopore.** The expert says Nanopore announced it is discontinuing the PromethION 2 and raising prices on PromethION Solo, and also discontinued Flongle (a small low-cost flow cell), which "significantly affected logistics and flexibility." PacBio: expert is "not sure."
- **Element Biosciences.** Many cores are interested in or buying Element and its share is growing, but not enough to take significant share from Illumina.
- **MGI/BGI.** The expert thinks more important than Element is the "logistic demerge" of MGI from BGI. They say MGI equipment and kits now belong to a Swiss company (name not recalled) that can be considered not Chinese, and that it is "aggressively offering their instruments and services." (Expert's recollection, not verified.)
- **Why Illumina dominated and what is changing** (expert's view):
  - Illumina long dominated through "aggressive patent enforcement," but has lost several lawsuits in the last five years and some patents have expired.
  - Illumina has become "much more aggressive" commercially and now offers new kits and technologies, notably native synthetic long reads on NovaSeq X.
  - Illumina offers an integrated storage and analysis pipeline, including HIPAA-compliant (US health-privacy) storage and analysis, useful for clinical projects.
  - "I see that Illumina competitiveness and willingness to work with customers greatly improved in the last two, three years."
- **12-month share outlook.** "Illumina will lose some share but it won't be a significant loss." Likely gainers: "Element. Maybe Ultima and most probably MGI."

## Purchasing plans

- The core plans to upgrade its Nanopore system because Nanopore discontinued the P2 system, which will be supported for another three years. The expert wants a PromethION P2 Solo. The AVITI24 purchase is already underway.

## Illumina products and innovations

- **Native synthetic long reads on NovaSeq X** (Illumina's TruPath Genome, a method that gets long-range information from short reads): the most exciting item. The core has a human tumor genomics group; it helps with human pipelines, structural variation (large DNA rearrangements) analysis. "This technology is coming up, we are eager to use it."
- **Other positives:** the Illumina methylation kit; Illumina's purchase of a single-cell company (several years ago, as the expert describes it) and its move into single-cell.
- **TruPath adoption.** "Very likely" to use. By end of 2026 it could be roughly **5%-6%** of the lab's volume, from two or three cancer biology labs. It is not a replacement for long-read work but a premium option for groups at the research-clinical interface, particularly with limited material and a need for accelerated analysis.
- **SomaLogic acquisition (proteomics, the measurement of proteins).** Potentially an improvement and a good move into proteomics and integrated multi-omics. Currently not feasible for small cores because price per sample is high. It does not make the expert use more Illumina overall ("it's a different product"), but they hope SomaLogic will integrate with single-cell technology.

## Competitor products

- **Ultima:** offering a significant increase in volume and lower sequencing cost, "although the read quality is still questionable."
- **Element in-cell sequencing for spatial omics** and its G4 system are being watched.
- **Roche AXELIOS (new sequencing platform).** Lots of buzz, but the expert predicts it will "just simply die out" for lack of competitive advantage. Reasoning: its main advantage is longer reads (about 1,000 bases); cost and real-life output are unknown; the library-prep-plus-sequencing system is too much of a "model system"; Illumina and Element now offer 600 base-pair reads; Illumina's TruPath covers synthetic long reads; and Nanopore and PacBio are improving, especially Nanopore on quality. "I don't see a space for the technology." This is a prediction, not direct experience.

## Customer support

- Mostly satisfied with Illumina ("okay"), but Element Biosciences provides better support. No significant changes in Illumina technical support recently.

## Future of genomic sequencing

- The expert expects in-cell sequencing to be the most interesting trend and Nanopore/single-molecule technology to become more robust and take a larger share.
- Biggest impact: clinical applications, including regulatory-space progress, operational or fast-output sequencing, and rising demand for high-throughput, comprehensive clinical genomics in provider facilities.
