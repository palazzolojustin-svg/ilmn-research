# Expert call – Roche Precision Medicine & Genomics Lead (UK) on competitive erosion of Illumina's NGS dominance, decentralization of NHS testing, long-read, and genomic data

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-29_Precision_Medicine_and_Genomics_Lead_at_Roche_Holding_Ltd_Believes_Emerging_Competitors_Ar.md` · Date: 2026-07-29 · AlphaSense expert call (published 31 Jul 2026); interview with the Precision Medicine and Genomics Lead at Roche Holding Ltd (UK; former Thermo Fisher NGS application specialist); client was an investor-led (buy-side) interviewer who is a physician · expert call*

**Summary.** A Roche precision-medicine lead who spent about 15 years at Thermo Fisher (an Illumina competitor) and now advises NHS England argues that Illumina's dominance is being eroded by Ultima, Element, MGI and Oxford Nanopore, each attacking a different segment. The main mechanism is the shift of UK/European testing from centralized "send-out" labs (Foundation Medicine, Guardant, Natera) to NHS labs running transferred tests, with validation on non-Illumina sequencers under way. The expert is positive on clinical sequencing growth (liquid biopsy, MRD), sees long-read as niche, and says Illumina must innovate rather than launch "me too" products. The call also covers NHS genomic data commercialization and ethics, and drug pricing in the UK/US.

# Expert call notes: Roche precision medicine and genomics lead on Illumina's competitive position

## 1. Expert background and relationship to Illumina

**Who the expert is.** The expert is the Precision Medicine and Genomics Lead at Roche Holding Ltd, based in the UK. Their job is to work out how Roche best uses genomics to match patients to drugs, to oversee genomic testing through the right testing methods, to embed precision programs for genomics, and to ensure access to drugs and treatment [Expert bio, AlphaSense].

**Relationship to Illumina: competitor, not a former Illumina employee.** The expert is classified as a "Competitor" perspective. Their career, in their own account [Expert: Roche precision medicine lead, 29 Jul 2026]:
- They finished a PhD in 2004 and then worked for a forensics company that used next-generation sequencing (NGS, the high-speed DNA reading technology) in forensic applications.
- They then moved to a company "now called Thermo Fisher", a competitor to Illumina, where they were an application specialist. They set up many different NGS applications (non-cancer, cancer, even plant biology) and increasingly built clinical solutions, covering instruments, software and reagents (the chemical kits used in each run).
- They stayed in that role for about 15 years, then moved to Roche. The AlphaSense summary describes "over 15 years" of NGS experience.
- At Roche they apply that experience to personalized medicine: finding the right patients for clinical trials and for licensed medicines.
- They advise NHS England (the UK public health service) on which drugs are coming through and which technologies it needs to deploy so that tests are available before the medicines arrive.

**Recency and basis.** Their current role is recent and ongoing, and the NHS observations are direct and current. Their views on Illumina's competitors' accuracy are partly hearsay ("we have heard"), and their views on future technology are explicitly speculative. These distinctions are flagged below.

**Interview context.** The interviewer (the "client") is an investor-led, buy-side interviewer who says they have a background in medicine and biochemistry and have worked as an internal medicine doctor in hospitals. The client opened by saying Illumina is "generally considered top of the hill" but that, from other experts, its market share is dropping as Element, Ultima, MGI or BGI, and potentially Roche, come in. The call was a repeat conversation ("Thanks for joining me again"). Interview date was 29 Jul 2026; published 31 Jul 2026.

## 2. Overall view: Illumina's dominance is eroding and the market is splitting into segments

**Core thesis [Expert, Jul 2026].** Illumina has been and still is "the gold standard" with dominant market share, but that share "definitely is being eroded". Illumina started in research and moved into clinical, and the expert believes clinical is where the majority of its growth now is. Its technologies span high to lower throughput (throughput means how much DNA can be read per run).

**Competitors are attacking discrete segments of Illumina's business** [Expert, Jul 2026]:
- **Ultima Genomics** targets massive population-scale genomics and is driving down cost at the high-volume end. The expert says this will "really impact Illumina's growth in that space", and it targets clinical labs doing high-throughput work.
- **Element Biosciences** targets mid-throughput labs, competing on precision rather than volume. The expert says Element will start tapping into Illumina's space as well.
- **MGI** is named as a competitor offering solutions in discrete parts of Illumina's business (see accuracy comments in section 6).
- **Long-read players, Oxford Nanopore and PacBio**, play in complex disease research, epigenomics (chemical marks on DNA that affect gene activity) and point-of-care diagnostics, which the expert says is "quite a big area" in the UK, where people want diagnostics closer to the patient, at the bedside.

**Expert's conclusion.** Illumina's market will be divided into discrete segments, each with several competitors. The expert thinks Illumina will "struggle to retain the dominance that they have had over the past few years". Each rival "is bringing a unique feature", and the expert thinks it will be hard for Illumina to address all of them with its current technologies. They expect the trend to accelerate and say the effect will be seen "over the next five to 10 years".

## 3. Point-of-care and decentralized testing

**Oxford Nanopore and bedside testing [Expert, direct observation of UK trends].** Oxford Nanopore is UK-based and has taken its technology "out into the wild" for pathogen testing. The expert's COVID example: testing at the patient's bedside to identify the strain, treat appropriately, and move non-COVID patients out of a COVID ward. Another example is a patient presenting with diffuse cancer, who could get a quick answer and be routed to the right triage point rather than "bouncing around the system". There is a "huge governmental shift" in the UK to get diagnostics done faster, and testing in the hospital at the bedside is "quite a driver at the moment". The expert describes Oxford Nanopore devices as "like matchbox... super tiny, super easy to use".

**Reagent rental model.** The client relayed another person's description of Oxford Nanopore as handing out benchtop sequencers and making money on consumables. The expert replied: "To be fair, that's what Illumina used to do." Under "reagent rental", instruments are provided at a cheap price but the customer is tied to buying the company's reagents for a fixed term of two to three years. It produced repeated business through steady consumables flow. The expert says quite a few companies in the UK and globally have adopted this approach as a good way to ensure a stable business.

## 4. How centralized testing is becoming decentralized in the UK (direct experience)

**The cycle.** The expert describes a pattern: tests are centralized while expertise is scarce, then decentralize as they become commoditized (standard and widely available), "until the next test comes along that becomes centralized, and that cycle repeats itself."

The table below shows how many NHS labs offer each test type, per the expert, and where each is heading.

| Test | Past | Now | Direction |
|---|---|---|---|
| Comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP, a broad panel test of a tumor's many genes) | Deemed to need centralized expertise | Seven labs offer it as NHS standard of care | Still fairly centralized but much less than before |
| Liquid biopsy (cancer tests from a blood sample) | One lab for all NHS patients | Three labs | Expected to expand as it becomes a commodity |
| MRD (minimal residual disease, detecting tiny amounts of remaining cancer after treatment) | Not yet deployed widely | NHS is deciding which lab can deploy it | Expected to decentralize as it commoditizes |

**How the first wave worked (CGP).** Labs initially used Illumina instruments. Each lab designed its own tests to cover genes listed in the NHS National Test Directory; which tests to use was up to each lab. Some labs partnered with Thermo Fisher and some with Illumina. For example, Wales deployed Illumina technology, both instruments and reagents.

**How liquid biopsy and MRD work: technology transfer.** Because liquid biopsy has a complex reagent design, the NHS (and other international groups in Europe) partner with companies such as Foundation Medicine and Guardant. The company transfers its technology and trains the local lab to run the test at the same level as the company's own send-out service (where samples are shipped to the company's central lab). The UK has three labs, each running a different technology-transfer provider's test. For MRD, the NHS is now debating how to do technology transfer and which companies to pick. Natera "was one that was being discussed quite closely", and Guardant is another because it already has a lab in the UK that is part of an NHS lab.

**Examples of the model.**
- Manchester: an NHS lab partnered with Foundation Medicine and has an Illumina instrument "purely dedicated" to liquid biopsy using Foundation Medicine reagents. It is validated to run the test at the same level as Foundation Medicine's Penzberg laboratory (which offers it as a send-out). "Effectively, it's a Foundation Medicine lab."
- London: the same is true for Guardant.
- The same would be true for Natera: a lab operating "as a true Natera lab" within the NHS, with NHS staff running the reagents and instruments. Such labs typically have the vendor-recommended instrumentation plus a second Illumina sequencer for CGP and other non-MRD standard-of-care testing.

**Why this matters for Illumina.** Technology-transfer agreements mandate that the NHS lab buy the same sequencer, library preparation kits and ancillary machines the partner uses in its head-office labs. Natera, for instance, uses Illumina platforms today. The expert says "that's where potentially Illumina could have some erosion if these other companies, like Natera, start to validate their tests on other sequencing instruments." The NHS labs would then adopt whatever instruments and reagents the partner validated.

**Wider European shift.** The expert says there is "a big shift away across Europe from doing send-out services to bringing the technology in-house to retain expertise within the local laboratories", driving "a huge uptick of technology transfers".

**NHS lab structure and timing.** The expert cites seven labs in England, potentially four in Scotland and one in Wales (11 in total), which can operate independently, with technology transfers increasing. They say this "will end up definitely happening" and that Illumina, "if they don't keep up and they can't be competitive in that space, they will lose out", visible "over the next five to 10 years".

## 5. Are Foundation, Guardant, Natera moving away from Illumina?

**Expert's answer [Expert, Jul 2026]: yes, validation is ongoing, but today everything runs on Illumina.**
- Those companies are talking to Ultima and other firms with sequencers coming through, including (the expert says they cannot name it) "the Roche sequencer", because they can offer the technology at lower cost.
- Example: if running the Ultima sequencer lets Guardant lower its test cost, it gives Guardant "a huge competitive advantage".
- "There is validation ongoing with some of the other platforms. Whether they'll be successful or not is still up in the air."
- Once validated, labs that adopted the technology transfer would need to change their instruments from Illumina to the new platform. The expert notes this impact applies to Illumina only.
- Quality gate: labs "would not even validate something that did not meet the quality at least of what the Illumina technology offers", and they also check whether throughput is appropriate; if not, switching could cost them more.

**Illumina's defensive move suggested by the expert.** Illumina faces "a little bit of a threat if they don't start to partner with companies, such as Natera to make sure that their platforms are the ones that are being used within their send out service."

## 6. Competitor accuracy and quality (mostly hearsay)

All of the following is reported as "we have heard", not direct testing:
- **MGI:** "not quite as accurate as the Illumina system."
- **Element:** very positive reports on data quality and accuracy.
- **Ultima:** very positive reports; error rates are "super low", but it operates at a large volume throughput, so would be valuable mainly to a high-volume send-out operator like Foundation Medicine (FMI), which runs very large numbers of samples a day.
- **A new sequencer the expert calls "Xcelris" (later "XcelrisXcelus"; the transcript spelling is unclear, and from context this appears to be the Roche sequencer using sequencing by expansion):** positive reports on accuracy.

## 7. Oxford Nanopore, PacBio and the role of long-read

**NHS validation.** For applications such as MRD and epigenomics, there is "a lot of positive noise" around Oxford Nanopore, and the NHS is validating it for specific applications. The expert says they do not see so much of PacBio, citing "question marks on the reliability of PacBio". Oxford Nanopore benefits partly because it is locally developed.

**Where long-read fits.** The client noted their understanding that long-read is perhaps 10% of sequencing demand. The expert's view on its sweet spots:
- Pathogen detection, epigenomics and RNA modifications, big structural variations, and large whole-genome problems that are hard for short-read technology (where there are many errors).
- UK whole-genome work used to focus on cancer, but the NHS has "more commonly gone away from that" and concentrates cancer on short reads.
- Rare disease and other non-cancer areas are where whole-genome sequencing has become a real benefit; Oxford Nanopore "would have a real position in rare diseases".
- Initial NHS validation is for whole-genome sequencing, with epigenomics as the second phase.
- Cancer: some applications are possible (e.g. MRD-related modifications), but not the comprehensive-profiling space.

**Could nanopore replace sequencing by synthesis (SBS, Illumina's method of building a copy of DNA base by base)?** The expert said nanopore has fewer errors in principle because it pulls native DNA straight through and reads changes in electrical current, avoiding amplification and added bases. It can detect bases and structural/epigenetic changes in a single run. But "the long-read application is quite niche", and they "can't see everything moving to a Nanopore technology". They see potential in proteomics (the study of proteins), epigenetics, long-read and rapid point-of-care diagnostics, but say short-read has a lasting place in comprehensive profiling and biopsies.

## 8. Genomic data: value, NHS constraints and ethics

**Context.** The client noted that Tempus, Caris and others say the testing is not the profitable part; the data sold to developers and pharma is. The expert said the data question is an ongoing discussion with the NHS.

**NHS position [Expert, direct knowledge].**
- Data is the NHS's "unique selling point", but it does not sell it. It has a government mandate that it is patients' data and it will not profit from it.
- The NHS is developing the Unified Genomics Record (UGR). Labs feed data into it, which could build a full per-patient picture: diagnosis (e.g. lung cancer), treatments, a genomic profile at week three, alterations found, change of therapy, outcomes and adverse events. The expert knows the NHS collects this because they have seen it from NHS Digital, its data repository.
- The expert's proposal: de-identify the data and sell the product or pathway back to pharma or developers to improve sequencing technology or drug design. "That's definitely where the money is, by far and away", and it is the part "we don't capitalize on"; they think other countries do better.
- Pricing implication: outcome data would let the NHS negotiate. If a drug works well it might pay a bit more; if it underperforms its trial results, the NHS could argue to pay less.

**Ethics.** Per the expert, the debate is about selling patient data back to industry. The public sees "big bad pharma" and worries, for example, about private insurers learning of predispositions. The expert says guardrails would prevent this, but calls the public's stance understandable.

**Private firms.** Caris, Foundation Medicine and Guardant hold genomic databases and are weighing whether to sell the data. What they lack is which treatment the patient received and the outcome, so their data could be "even more valuable" but is hard to complete. Flatiron tried, but "started selling the data for a huge amount of money that people just couldn't afford to buy" and did not take off as it should have.

**Industry consortia and rare cancers.** Many industry consortia have approached the NHS. Pooling rare cancer data across borders (e.g. roughly 10 patients in a UK area, 10 in Europe, 5 to 10 in the US) would be "hugely valuable", and is a current gap.

**Patient compensation idea.** The client suggested paying patients (e.g. $0.50 per access) or asking cancer patients to donate their genome. The expert called it "a great idea" and suggested royalties could go to dependents or partners; most cancer patients "want to give something back". They said the NHS needs an opt-in/opt-out data-sharing approach and a repository that industry can pay to access.

**Our Future Health precedent.** This UK government-funded project recruits healthy volunteers, records basic measurements (age, height, blood pressure, weight), and later pulls GP records as conditions develop. Drug companies pay to access it; Roche does so and helps support the project. It is run as a trial, not cancer, and not genomic, but includes consent for genomics if a rare condition develops. The expert thinks this model should be brought into standard-of-care practice.

## 9. What Illumina should do (expert's opinions)

The client listed possible moves: diversification through single-cell (Fluent acquisition), proteomics (SomaLogic acquisition), spatial biology and multi-omics, versus focusing on a decentralizable benchtop sequencer. Some say Illumina is too late. The expert's recommendations were location-dependent and opinion-based:

1. **Point-of-care product.** In the UK, with the move to point of care, a product building on Illumina's "gold standard" approach would work, because "people know the name, people trust the name."
2. **Beyond genomics.** Look at transcriptomics (reading RNA) and broader omics: proteomics, RNA and DNA sequencing together. They said that if Illumina went into spatial transcriptomics, which they understand it is exploring, it would help keep its technology current, since customers will buy the Illumina instrument if the application is validated on it. Anything Illumina develops will be examined because of its reputation.
3. **Data, software and AI.** Sequencing is "becoming commoditized", so the challenge is shifting to "We can develop all this cool data. What the heck do we do with it?" Tools tailored to Illumina instruments could help retain customers. In NHS trusts, "the biggest challenge now is the data". A clinician given a whole-genome report will think "What the heck is this?", so data must be put into meaningful formats.

**Bioinformatics differentiation.** The client relayed an ex-Illumina expert's claim that nobody in the real world uses Illumina's bioinformatics platform with its instruments. The expert agreed in part: much software is open source and free, and many Illumina users build their own pipelines tied to their application, so an Illumina pipeline might go unused. Value could instead come from quickly pulling huge amounts of data into meaningful formats and building AI on top of it, especially for multi-omics, where "there's a huge gap". In the NHS the bottleneck is staffing and interpretation: "It's quite easy to run the machines, it's easy to run the pipelines, but actually validating and interpreting what comes out... that's where there's a bit of a bottleneck."

**Is Illumina late to multi-omics/single-cell?** The client noted claims that 10x Genomics is "king of the hill" in single-cell and is moving to validate on other sequencing platforms. The expert: "Absolutely. I think they are late to the game, definitely," but not necessarily doomed. There can be an advantage in not being first, by learning from 10x and others and going a step further. The expert warned: "If they just come out with a me too, I do think that's where they'll struggle." Illumina needs to repeat what it did at the beginning with NGS, something truly innovative. They said competitors are taking share by building on Illumina's successes and improving them, and that Illumina, having been top player so long, may need to bring in innovative people if it lacks them.

## 10. Market growth outlook (expert's view)

- **Clinical sequencing** is "where all the growth is at the moment"; the pie is getting bigger.
- **Liquid biopsy:** still a lot of growth and "a huge number of unmet need", even after a few years in the market.
- **CGP:** only small growth and "quite saturated" with many companies. Growth comes from markets that don't yet offer it; where it is deployed, increases are "very small... year-on-year". The liquid biopsy part of CGP would grow more.
- **MRD:** a definite growth area, with many applications being discussed.
- **Multi-omics:** great research but slow to validate and get into clinical use. The expert expects it to grow "quite rapidly" within five years, but later than liquid biopsy and MRD.
- Near-term emergent growth is therefore liquid biopsy and MRD.

## 11. Possible disruptive technology (speculation)

The expert pointed to the new Roche-linked sequencer (called "Xcelris/XcelrisXcelus" in the transcript) that uses sequencing by expansion rather than sequencing by synthesis, which brings high quality and speed. They said it "will change the landscape if it does deliver what it's supposed to deliver" and would accelerate shifts further. They added "I genuinely do not know" whether a whole genome in four hours is achievable. If something like Oxford Nanopore's approach were brought to a benchtop with such speed, genomic tests could be done at the bedside, with bioinformatic hubs crunching the data and sending reports to clinicians, which would be "a definite shift in the landscape". These are hypotheticals, not facts.

## 12. Side discussion: UK healthcare, NICE and drug pricing

This section is unrelated to Illumina directly.
- **NICE process (UK drug cost-effectiveness body).** Some consultants, especially those who ran the drug in trials, get frustrated when the NHS approval and pricing process blocks a drug, particularly when data are strong and no alternatives exist. They are less frustrated when other options exist. Patients mostly do not know about unfunded drugs unless a celebrity case reaches the news; the expert cited a prostate cancer drug not approved, publicized via cyclist Sir Chris Hoy, which led to patient-organization outrage.
- **US "fair market pricing"/most favored nation policy (referred to as Trump's policy).** In the past six months it has caused challenges outside the US for pricing in the UK and Europe. Some companies are delaying launches of medicines to see whether the administration remains in a few years. The client said the expected outcome was that companies would raise prices elsewhere rather than cut US prices, and that it "backfired". The expert agreed: "It's backfiring on the U.S., but it's backfiring on the other countries as well", because ex-US countries are not getting the launches. The expert does not know how it will resolve; there is much discussion about ex-US pricing, and the US is "the biggest growth market".
- **Advertising and global fairness.** UK drug advertising to the public is forbidden, unlike the US. The expert agrees the US pays more for medicines, and says a global decision is needed so no single country bankrolls others and "everybody needs to pay appropriately for innovation".
