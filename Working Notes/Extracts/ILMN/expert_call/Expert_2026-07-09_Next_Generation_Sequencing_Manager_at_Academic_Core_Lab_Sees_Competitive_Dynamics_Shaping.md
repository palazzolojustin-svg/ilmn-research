# Academic Core Lab NGS Manager (Vanderbilt) Channel Check on Illumina: Q2 2026 Volume Rebound, Tariff Pricing Fight, and Share Loss Risk to Ultima and Element

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-09_Next_Generation_Sequencing_Manager_at_Academic_Core_Lab_Sees_Competitive_Dynamics_Shaping.md` · Date: 2026-07-09 · Channel check expert call on an expert-network platform (AI Interviewer), published 2026-07-13; expert is a Next Generation Sequencing Manager at an academic core lab serving Vanderbilt University Medical Center (Tennessee) · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer-side expert at an academic sequencing core lab reports that their Illumina sequencing output rose about 18% year over year in Q2 2026 (a rebound from a soft Q2 2025, helped by fiscal-year-end spending), and expects 8%-10% growth for full-year 2026. Net reagent price rose only about 1% after the lab fought a surprise tariff charge, and the expert believes Illumina will lose share, mainly to Ultima and secondarily to Element, while Roche's Axelios is seen as a slower, mid-level threat. The lab is likely to buy an Ultima instrument before December 2026 and is newly enthusiastic about Illumina's TruPath Genome and Constellation methylation products.

## Expert background and how to weigh this call

**Who the expert is.** The expert is the next-generation sequencing (NGS) manager at an academic core lab, a shared service facility that sequences DNA for many research groups. They have held this job for eight years. Before that they were the NGS leader and director at HudsonAlpha Institute for Biotechnology for eight years, so they have about 16 years in the field [Expert: NGS manager at academic core lab, Jul 2026]. The platform's bio describes them as a manager at a medical center in Tennessee who works with Illumina and Oxford Nanopore platforms. The transcript indicates the lab is part of Vanderbilt University Medical Center.

**Relationship to Illumina.** The expert is a current customer, not a former employee. They are also a buyer actively evaluating competitors. Their experience is current: the interview took place 2026-07-09, just after the end of Q2 2026, and covers that quarter directly.

**Customers the lab serves.** The lab sequences for all of Vanderbilt University Medical Center and all of Vanderbilt, plus external nonprofit and for-profit institutions across the United States and some international ones [Expert, Jul 2026].

**Equipment installed.**
- Illumina: two NovaSeq 6000s (high-throughput sequencers), a NovaSeq XP (a NovaSeq workflow variant), a MiSeq i100 (a small benchtop sequencer) and a NextSeq 500. The lab says it only actively uses the NovaSeq XP and the MiSeq i100, and uses them for almost all projects.
- Oxford Nanopore: one instrument, used for long-read sequencing (reading much longer stretches of DNA at a time).
- Ultima Genomics: the lab does not own an instrument. It prepares sequencing libraries (the prepared DNA samples loaded onto a sequencer) and sends them out to be run on Ultima.
- The lab is deciding whether to buy a competing platform (see "Equipment purchase plans").

**Direct experience versus speculation.** Volume, spending, pricing, the tariff dispute, inventory, lead times and customer support are direct experience. Statements about other labs are second-hand ("every core lab that I have interaction with"). Share forecasts, Roche's timing, Ultima's prospects and the SomaLogic view are the expert's opinion. The expert says they have not yet tested TruPath.

## Q2 2026 volume and sequencing output

**Volume surge.** The expert said volume "greatly increased" in Q2 2026. Their fiscal year ended June 30, so the lab had "a huge increase in sequencing volume" and ran at maximum capacity. The expert believes researchers were spending funds before they expired at fiscal year-end [Expert, Jul 2026].

**Other drivers.** There is new interest in many technologies, including methylation (chemical tags on DNA that affect gene activity) and large projects using custom captures (targeted enrichment of selected DNA regions) and very cost-effective assays. The expert reported no negative factors affecting Q2 2026.

**Output change.** Total gigabases (a unit of sequencing data output) on the lab's Illumina instruments rose "close to 18%" in Q2 2026 versus Q2 2025.

**Why the comparison was easy.** In Q2 2025 there had been a change in government, which caused hesitancy about grant spending and about what would happen with indirect and direct cost allowances. Researchers were unsure how their year-to-year spending would go. The lab also had few new assays or new library-prep drivers then, so it was less busy.

**Versus expectations.** Output was "slightly exceeding" the expert's expectations. They expected a rebound "just not this much of a rebound."

**Reasons for the stronger rebound.**
- People are more comfortable with the government changes and less hesitant to spend.
- New assays are more cost-effective, and more funds have been released.
- The lab has less NIH funding but more pharmaceutical and for-profit clients, with fewer grant-funded projects.

## Full-year 2026 outlook

The expert expects 2026 output on Illumina instruments to "meet what we did last year with a slight increase" of 8%-10%. The drivers are more cost-effective, high-throughput assays, better ability to pool samples ("more bang for your buck"), and the shift from grant funding to pharmaceutical and for-profit spending. Their outlook is no more or less optimistic than 90 days earlier: they expected a slight increase then and still do.

## Consumables (reagents) spending and pricing

**Spending.** Reagent spending on Illumina in Q2 2026 rose year over year, in line with the roughly 18% volume increase. Consumables are the reagent kits needed for every sequencing run.

**Net price effect.** Over the past 90 days the expert's list price rose, tariffs were added, and then a slight discount was added. The net result was "about a 1% increase on reagent cost." The expert called this "fairly stayed the same" and "not a significant increase."

**The tariff dispute (direct experience).**
- The lab had to "fight for" the discount. Illumina did not tell them it was adding a tariff, and "the tariff was huge."
- Every February the lab already gets a reagent price increase, and it builds a 5%-8% annual reagent cost increase into its own service pricing because it is expected.
- The tariff charge was not in the contractual agreement. The lab told Illumina it would move to another vendor and would not honor the tariff price.
- The expert described "a big argument between our Illumina vendor and our contracts team." Illumina said it could not remove the tariff because its legal team mandated it, so it instead gave a bigger reagent discount. The expert also said Illumina "ended up removing those tariff costs," so the account mixes the two descriptions, but the net effect was an offsetting discount.

**Other labs (second-hand).** Experiences vary. Some labs paid the tariff without fuss. Some fought and got bigger discounts. Some refused to pay and had it removed. The interviewer characterized Illumina's approach as inconsistent across customers.

**Inventory and lead times.** The lab's reagent inventory is "sufficient," not excessive. Lead times on consumables over the past 90 days "stayed the same."

## Instrument pricing and competitive incentives

**List prices.** Illumina and all competing vendors have become very competitive on instrument pricing over the past 90 days, "not excessively so." Instrument prices are about the same, and none have been cut. Vendors have instead added incentives.

**Incentives named by the expert.**
- Reagent cost discounts and service contract discounts (service contracts are "very expensive").
- Long-term or interest-free leasing.
- Reagent-only placement: if the lab commits to reagent purchases, the vendor places the instrument at no instrument charge.
- Free reagents for R&D testing.
- On-site engineers with fast response times.

## Competitive landscape

**Who is gaining favor.** The expert said Illumina's competitors "all have equal footing and coming up."
- **Ultima:** The expert gets requests "every day" for Ultima library prep, which is very cost-effective. The lab prepares libraries and sends them out because it has no Ultima instrument.
- **Element Biosciences:** Customers ask when the lab will get an Element sequencer. Element has spatial (mapping gene activity within tissue) capabilities.
- **Roche:** Many people ask about Roche, but there is hesitancy because Roche is "a little slow" to release its instrument.

**Why customers switch.**
- Cost is the main driver: "It's all about cost. They're cheaper than Illumina."
- Quality: "The quality isn't quite as good on the Ultima, but it's good enough" for customers to switch.
- Turnaround time is the second factor. Ultima struggles here because it lacks enough instruments in the field, so data comes back slowly. The expert says this is "the only reason Ultima can't come up." People want data faster than Ultima providers can deliver, and with more instruments "they would provide Illumina with true competition."
- Element is a competitor but is still working on add-ons such as spatial components and the onboard "HIVE" (as transcribed).

**Share outlook for the next 12 months (opinion).**
- Element and Ultima will gain. Roche "has a chance to gain" if it gets its instrument into the field.
- Illumina "will lose... Illumina's going to go down as the other ones go up." More people will go to Ultima unless Illumina cuts prices. Even then, the expert says Illumina is "too spread out on customer service and instrument repairs, and their technology is released before it's ready." Illumina's new products are "still very, very early in the development."
- "It may inch down, but they'll lose to Ultima for sure, and probably to Element, but definitely to Ultima. Ultima just has to get more instruments in the field."

**Competitor product developments noted.**
- Ultima's chemistry and library prep are improving: less difficult, fewer failures.
- Element is giving more output.
- Ultima now has 10B flow cells, different read lengths that can be mixed and matched to better support 10x (single-cell) samples, and different pricing on some wafers (its sequencing consumable).
- No major chemistry or instrument changes comparable to Illumina's, but read-length and chemistry flexibility is moving toward matching Illumina's. Element's changes will better support spatial workflows.

**Roche Axelios.**
- It is not yet pressuring Illumina "because it hasn't been released yet."
- If launched, the expert sees it as a threat "somewhere in between" major and minor. It has potential, especially on the clinical side, and Roche's reputation will back it.
- It lacks flexibility and paired-end reads (reading both ends of a DNA fragment), so it will take time to become a true competitor. Before that, "Ultima... Illumina needs to be worried about."

## Equipment purchase plans

**Plan.** The expert plans to buy new sequencing equipment in the next 12 months, and plans have not changed in the past 90 days.

**Rationale.** In Q2 the lab ran at 100% capacity with a queue, which the expert dislikes because they want fast turnaround. They normally run at about 85% capacity. They will buy another instrument to offset the capacity.

**Options in order of likelihood.**
1. **Ultima (most likely).** Vanderbilt leadership is "really pushing" for it. Ultima is also pushing, and there is no Ultima instrument in the Southeast, so they want the lab as the regional service provider.
2. **Element.** More cost-effective: the instrument costs "only $350,000." It also has spatial and onboard hybridization components the lab could add to service lines.
3. **Roche.** Expensive, but the Roche name is strong. The expert's team does not think it will be released in time, since the lab needs an instrument before the end of December (2026).

## Illumina products and innovation

**Constellation methylation assays.** The expert is most excited about these because methylation is "a feature for genomics." They like the ease of workflow, the accuracy, and combining whole genome with methylation. Vanderbilt is most excited about Illumina's spatial assays.

**TruPath Genome.**
- A month earlier the expert would have said "not likely at all," but multiple people have asked "When will you offer it?"
- The lab delayed upgrading its instrument until after the fiscal year because of capacity, upgraded it the day before the interview (2026-07-08), and will offer TruPath Genome soon. They have "a lot of orders for that right now."
- Share of volume by end-2026: hard to say, since they have not tested it enough. If it works well and "they can keep that cost down," most whole genomes will move to this workflow because it is cost-effective and includes DRAGEN analysis (Illumina's secondary-analysis software). "No way can beat the DRAGEN."
- As a replacement for long-read work sent to Ultima: unsure. "Maybe, but probably not." Testing is needed, and DRAGEN performance would be the determining factor.

**SomaLogic acquisition (proteomics, the study of proteins).** The expert thinks it "could have worked in Illumina's favor" but that Illumina has not developed the workflow enough. Olink (a competing proteomics company) is "growing too fast" and "very popular." It does not factor into the expert's purchasing decisions.

## Customer support

The expert is satisfied. Illumina has hired new people, is more involved, negotiates prices a little better, is less aggressive on instrument sales and listens more when the lab reports issues. The expert says customer service "has improved, or they're trying to improve it." They attribute this to competitive pressure (Illumina knows the lab will buy a competitor instrument and is already sending work to other vendors) and to the lab's direct feedback that it was "not satisfied with the level of support."

## Industry outlook (expert opinion)

- More vendors will develop new chemistries and instruments.
- Illumina's approach of loading whole genomic DNA without library prep is "a really good approach" because it removes technical difficulty. The expert adds that significant money goes into robotics and QC around library prep that is not usually counted.
- Combining genomics with multi-omics (proteomics, spatial genomics) is a good approach, and "all of the vendors are going to move towards that type of multi-omics outlook."

## Key takeaways and caveats

- Single-lab data point, academic and nonprofit-oriented. Q2 2026 growth was flattered by a weak Q2 2025 comparison and by fiscal-year-end spending.
- Pricing was roughly flat net (+1%) but required a hard negotiation. Tariff handling varied by lab.
- Competitive risk is expressed mainly as cost and turnaround, with Ultima named the main threat and Illumina's service and product maturity cited as weaknesses.
- The expert's own lab is a potential Illumina share loss through a near-term competitor purchase, though Illumina still runs nearly all current projects, and demand for TruPath Genome and methylation is a positive.
