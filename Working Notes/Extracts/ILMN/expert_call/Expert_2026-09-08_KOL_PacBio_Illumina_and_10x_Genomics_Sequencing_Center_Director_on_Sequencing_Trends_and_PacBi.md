# Academic sequencing-center director (Louisville) on CRO price pressure on Illumina, PacBio long-read adoption, 10x vs Illumina in single-cell/spatial, and immune repertoire profiling

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-09-08_KOL_PacBio_Illumina_and_10x_Genomics_Sequencing_Center_Director_on_Sequencing_Trends_and_PacBi.md` · Date: 2026-09-08 · Guidepoint expert call; moderator Andrew Carmen; expert Dr. Melissa Laird Smith, University of Louisville School of Medicine · expert call*

**Summary.** A university sequencing-center leader and CEO of a long-read immune profiling startup says academic demand for Illumina sequencing has been rerouted to low-priced CROs (contract research organizations), gutting small and mid-sized core facilities, while her center plans to buy an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (the call text says "Nova X Plus") for single-cell, spatial and proteomics work. She sees Illumina's integrated multi-omics platform as a growing threat to 10x Genomics, whose Atera system is a pricier, single-purpose machine. She is very positive on PacBio's accuracy and chemistry improvements (SPRQ-Nx, Kinnex), but says PacBio under-markets long-read transcriptomics and under-supports analysis software.

# Expert call notes: Academic sequencing center director on Illumina, 10x Genomics and PacBio (Guidepoint, 2026-09-08)

## Expert background and how to weigh the views

**Who the expert is.** Dr. Melissa Laird Smith is Associate Professor of Biochemistry and Molecular Genetics at the University of Louisville School of Medicine. She is a customer and user of sequencing technology, not a former employee of any of these companies. [Expert: Louisville sequencing center former director, Sep 2026]

- She stepped down as director of the Sequencing Technology Center (STC, the university's shared sequencing "core" facility) in early 2025. This was to prepare for the launch of the Kentucky Center of Precision Medicine, which she now directs.
- The new center continues multi-omics technology development (measuring several molecular layers, such as DNA, RNA and protein, from the same sample), focused on long reads, and adds an "implementation science" pipeline. That pipeline identifies custom targeted technologies, works with the university tech-transfer team to fast-track IP acquisition for those with near-term clinical impact, and moves them into clinical trials and clinical research as experimental outcome variables.
- She also launched a company, **Clareo Biosciences**, which uses long reads for immune repertoire profiling (reading the huge variety of B-cell and T-cell receptors that make up the immune system's recognition toolkit). She calls herself its CEO and has "active collaborations in all six continents."
- She still chairs the STC advisory board, so she sees its business projections. Roughly 77% of the STC's occupancy still comes from her team (a recent calculation); she no longer manages the other 23%.
- She last spoke with the moderator about three years earlier. She has used PacBio since 2012 and describes herself as a PacBio customer for "12 years... I think it's 14 years at this point."
- **Bias disclosure:** on long reads for immune profiling she says, "this is something I've built my career on, so I'm obviously biased." She also has a commercial interest through Clareo.

**Direct experience vs. speculation.**
- Direct experience: her core's Illumina volumes, PacBio Revio and Vega use, 10x single-cell and Visium HD workflows, ArgenTag and Kinnex work, and immune genotyping.
- Indirect or secondhand: how much sequencing leaves campus (inferred from networking and grant support letters), Illumina's announced single-cell/spatial/proteomics plans, and Atera details.
- Not yet tested: she has not run the Illumina single-cell/spatial workflow ("we haven't had a chance to try it with our own hands quite yet") and has not run Atera. She says she has no insight into 10x pricing models or 10x's plans for older instruments.

## Illumina: academic demand and the CRO effect

**Volume fell sharply at small and mid-sized cores.** She says Illumina volume at her core "has dramatically decreased at the academic level at the small to midsized research core." She attributes this "very clearly" to several very competitive CROs (outside service companies) now running standard Illumina protocols at very low prices. [Expert, Sep 2026]
- Examples given: Plasmidsaurus doing RNA-Seq (sequencing of the genes a cell is expressing) at about $50 per sample, and Zymo, which just launched an RNA sequencing business.
- Plasmidsaurus's large Illumina processing plant is in Louisville, so she knows its staff. Her comment: "it's great for them, not great for us."
- Why cores cannot compete: academic cores must recoup salary and fringe costs, and with limited grant budgets researchers want "the most bang for your buck." She says there is "absolutely no way that we can compete on price."

**Demand rerouted, not lost.** She believes "there's a lot more RNA seq going out to these groups than maybe we saw in the past. So I don't think that the demand for Illumina has gone down. I just think that it's been rerouted." Illumina "has always been sort of that staple that kept everything," with samples always in the queue.

**Her core's equipment limits.** The core has only a NextSeq 2000 (a smaller Illumina sequencer), which makes high-depth work, such as 10x libraries, difficult at its scale.
- Investigators often have the core prepare 10x libraries, then ask for the samples back to sequence more cheaply at facilities with NovaSeq systems.
- She notes library preparation for 10x is the highest cost, not sequencing itself, but people are "looking to pinch pennies wherever they can."
- A few groups (she names Michigan) have opened CRO-style spatial transcriptomics (Visium and Xenium on flash-frozen tissue and slides), subsidized and cheaper. Some campus groups are evaluating them. She still sees quality-control concerns, such as shipment risk, with outsourcing.

**How she knows about outside volume.** From connections on campus (people feel more comfortable now that she is not core director) and because the core is still asked to write letters of support saying it could do the sequencing for grants. Grants get awarded "but then we don't see the sequencing," so it is being done elsewhere due to cost. She did not give a percentage decline.

## Illumina: new single-cell, spatial and proteomics plans (her view)

- She is "really excited about leaning back into Illumina." She says Illumina announced that in Q3 it would move proteomics and single-cell and spatial transcriptomics onto the NovaSeq X Plus. (The transcript says "Nova X Plus" and "Illumina X Plus"; context shows it is the same system.) Her Center of Precision Medicine is in discussions to acquire one specifically for multi-omics profiling. She expects to use it by the end of 2026.
- She says: "Illumina, just when you think they're sort of getting quieter, end up raging forward with new developments in these other fields."
- Advantage over 10x: everything is on one platform. She has "a lot of faith" Illumina will develop integrated tools to look at genomics, transcriptomics (RNA) and proteomics in the same cells and same tissue slices. She says Illumina's presence in all those spaces gives it the incentive to build such tools, and her team is partnering closely with them.
- **Tipping point:** what would make her walk away from 10x is whether Illumina can integrate the pipelines: whole-genome transcriptional profiling at bulk or single-cell level plus proteomics on the same instrument, with the DRAGEN system (Illumina's analysis software) overarching. She notes Illumina brought in SomaScan/SomaSeq technologies (rebranded; she does not remember the name; the moderator clarified it is Illumina Protein Prep). She calls it one of the most comprehensive proteomic offerings, compared to Olink (proximity ligation assay) as aptamer-based. If Illumina delivers the end-to-end workflow, "it's going to be an incredible competitor to 10x."
- **PIPseq** is Illumina's single-cell sequencing product competing with 10x. She says Illumina has marketed it for over a year "and it doesn't seem like it's making much of a dent." PIPseq libraries can be made in-house on the bench without capital equipment, but must be sequenced on Illumina; they can then be sent to any service provider.
- **Flexibility claims (Illumina vs Atera):**
  - Illumina's slide is larger and fits in the X Plus, holding more tissue or a bigger tissue array. That allows multiple samples per slide and lower prices.
  - Illumina single-cell input can range from 1,000 cells to one million cells, a range she says 10x does not currently support. Cost can scale with the project rather than being flat.
  - She calls this "a little bit more of a theoretical win in our minds" (not yet tested). She hopes to assess both but is "not sure if we'll have that opportunity."

## 10x Genomics and Atera

**Current use.** Her lab uses 10x single-cell RNA workflows and Visium HD (high-resolution spatial) workflows; 10x has been "a workhorse." She does not own a Xenium. The Center of Precision Medicine had budgeted for one, but it "is now obsolete" as 10x moves to Atera. They are assessing Atera.

**Usage trend.** 10x usage has been "very slowly going up" because of assay cost. Enthusiasm is "at an all-time high," and internal pilot grants help get initial data. Usage grows in proportion to the number of researchers winning grants. She wishes 10x would lower price "a little bit." Her view is "I think 10x is still a workhorse, but I don't know how much longer it will be," given Illumina's moves.

**Atera: pros and cons from her perspective.**
- Physical size: she expected something small like the Chromium box (about shoe-box size) but Atera is about the size of a Revio, "about the size of a refrigerator."
- 10x is moving away from Illumina as a partner; Atera does its own sequencing using proprietary technology she is "still a little bit unclear on." Because it is not integrated, she sees it as more of a single-use system.
- **Price comparison** (her figures, all-in including shipment and installation):

| System | Price | Her assessment |
|---|---|---|
| 10x Atera | about $650,000 | Single-purpose 10x workflows |
| Illumina NovaSeq X Plus | about $1.2 million | About twice the price but "about three times the application capability" |

- She says "the Atera is definitely what 10x is betting on and going to build their future on," and Atera may have capabilities Illumina "just doesn't strive for." For now her center is "prioritizing getting the system that has the most capabilities."

**Chromium customers and obsolescence.** Most people she knows still run the original Chromium controller (not Chromium X or iX). She wonders whether 10x will stop supporting older controllers and chemistries, and whether those customers would follow to Atera. "I think that will be the big test for 10x... And I'm not convinced they will," because 10x is hard to learn and analyze. She has no insight into 10x's actual plans. She is unsure whether Atera will use the same chemistries.

**Stickiness.** 10x has been in the field about 10 years. Once the controller, protocol and pipeline are in place, "it's very hard to consider changing," which she says benefits 10x tremendously. She adds that 10x is "not the easiest technology to learn, and it's particularly not the easiest technology to analyze."

**Limitation.** Current 10x workflows do not work for very large cells or cells with many dendrites (cardiomyocytes, a focus at her university), so only single-nucleus sequencing is possible.

## Alternative single-cell platforms

- **ArgenTag** (startup from Argentina): microfluidic chip for single-cell sequencing with no controller (versus a roughly $100,000 10x controller). It is not an oil-emulsion format, so it yields much longer cDNA and full-length transcriptomes. It works specifically with PacBio and Oxford Nanopore. Processing still costs about $2,500-$3,000 per sample, similar to 10x. She ran the protocol herself and "it worked perfectly out of the gate," which she says she cannot say of any 10x protocol. Her lab is running bake-offs between 10x, Parse and ArgenTag for long-read single-cell resolution.
- **Parse:** no capital equipment but "an incredible amount of pipetting," very labor intensive. Her lab has not run Parse libraries itself, only compared Parse's public data, as it lacks a dedicated liquid handler.
- **Cellanome** (Bay Area startup): lab-on-a-chip doing single-cell phenotyping (fluorescence, secretion) then RNA isolation and cDNA generation. It has no restrictions on cell size, shape or arms, so is one to watch for hard-to-profile cells.
- **Her ranking:** Illumina has "the biggest chance" to take share from 10x given bandwidth and its ability to control cost models and system placement. Alternatives are attractive to new single-cell entrants because they need no capital equipment. 10x's switch away from Illumina as a sequencing partner gives openings to others. She hopes other companies "jump at it."
- **Risk for ArgenTag:** it may struggle if PacBio cannot market why a long-read transcriptome is needed.

## PacBio

**Usage trend.** PacBio business at her core has been "relatively steady." She and her biochemistry department colleagues are the major users and all their grant-funded research uses the PacBio instrument.

**Fleet.** Two Revio systems at the university and one Vega (PacBio's lower-cost benchtop system) at the Clareo lab. The Revios run the SPRQ-Nx chemistry; PacBio released SPRQ-Nx for Vega on August 18, 2026 ("less than two weeks ago" at the call).
- If she bought another, she would choose a Revio for throughput for Clareo's growing customer base, but would keep the Vega for rapid technology development.
- On cost evolution: since 2012 she has watched a genome go from about $200,000 to as low as $500 "depending on how you're pricing."

**Vega effect on external customers.** Vega "continues to democratize access to SMRT sequencing." Some long-term external core customers dropped off because they bought a lower-cost instrument and now sequence in-house. Several institutions (academic and biotech) bought their own Revios or Vegas due to sample numbers and turnaround needs. This lowered her core's volume, but their sequencing did not stop.

**CSP (PacBio's Certified Service Provider program).** PacBio is "an incredible partner." Her facility is known for hard projects and receives referrals from PacBio's website and field application specialists, especially for pilot projects. Agricultural and nonhuman projects are common. She just received an NSF grant to sequence 60 vertebrate genomes and transcriptomes to understand the evolution of the immune system and immune repertoires.

**SPRQ-Nx economics.**
- Gives more data per run with lower input mass (useful for scarce samples from the Smithsonian and zoos).
- On Vega, the benefit is more reads per run; on Revio it is also a cost saver.
- **SMRT cell reuse:** a SMRT Cell (the sequencing unit) costs about $1,000. Instead of one genome per cell, she can pool four libraries and run the same cell four times, paying for one cell and making one pooled library. The same library must be rerun because the original library stays bound; new material repopulates unused wells. She multiplexes more heavily upfront.
- Relevance: large genome projects (the vertebrate project) and the Precision Medicine Center's plan to use long-read sequencing as first-line testing for rare variants, particularly pediatric disease.
- She cites papers from Radboudumc (Netherlands) showing long-read sequencing as a first-line test, rather than after "35 other tests and five years of a diagnostic odyssey," recapitulates the variants found by other tests and identifies new ones. Louisville aims to reprove that framework and implement clinical long-read sequencing.

**Kinnex (concatenated read) and Iso-Seq (full-length RNA isoform sequencing).** She has produced "platinum standard transcriptomes" for non-model organisms, rats, humans and immune repertoires. For immune repertoire, a B-cell receptor molecule is about 1.5 kb, while the instrument can generate reads of about 20 kb, so about 12 molecules can be concatenated per read. This raises depth 8- to 12-fold per sample, making long-read "not only cost competitive, but actually cost benefit compared to some of the short-read applications," giving the same or more reads than an Illumina run at higher accuracy.

**Accuracy.** She cites Q40 versus Q30 (99.99% vs 99.9%), "tenfold higher accuracy," which she says means basically no errors in a 1.5 kb molecule. This matters because B-cell receptors hypermutate to adapt to threats, so each mutation call must be trustworthy.

**Criticisms of PacBio.**
- Iso-Seq protocols like Kinnex "[haven't] really taken off in the way I expected," even though they are powerful. Back-end analysis pipelines are not well set up for users. "That's really a failing, unfortunately, on the PacBio part... taking it 80% there and not all the way through."
- Need to better market the long-read transcriptome.

## Immune repertoire profiling and Clareo's pitch

**Technical case for long reads.** Oxford Nanopore "has come incredibly far" but "it's still not quite there for a molecule that mutates itself," though she watches them as "fast movers." She has used both ONT and PacBio.

**Competitors (her characterization).**
- Adaptive Biotechnologies profiles mainly the hypervariable region, about 100 nucleotides of a ~1,500-nucleotide molecule, which is cheap but incomplete, and gene assignment from fragments is error-prone because databases are poorly populated. She says Adaptive recently shut down its RUO (research use only) profiling business and pivoted to MRD (minimal residual disease testing), leaving "a massive gap" that Clareo wants to fill.
- Repertoire captures the entire variable region of TCR or BCR (the antigen-binding part). Clareo's "profile AIR" technology adds the constant region, the "stem," which she says varies substantially and dictates function (for example NK cell recruitment versus phagocytosis). She says this is visible only with long reads and, on today's market, only with Clareo's offering.

**Why it matters.**
- Links antigen specificity to function and enables full gene strings for monoclonal antibody design.
- Common lab practice clones the variable region into a vector with its own Fc region, which can produce in vitro biology that never exists in the body.
- Single-cell is needed to learn how heavy/light (antibody) or alpha/beta (TCR) chains pair, which bulk profiling cannot reveal.
- Potential uses: directed-function antibodies, prognostic or companion diagnostics (which vaccine a person will respond to), predicting autoimmunity risk and immune-related adverse events (cytokine storm).
- She says "nature has designed a lot of these therapies for us already."
- Long-read data is "future-proofing": it can be reanalyzed as knowledge grows.

**Genotyping findings (her lab's data).**
- Her lab genotypes the immune gene loci in DNA as well as profiling expressed receptors. Analogy: "we're all born with a quiver of arrows."
- Over 3,000 individuals screened. Except for identical twins, no two people shared the same genes, and every individual showed something new. Some apparently healthy people have large deletions, missing about 25% of genes in the region.
- Implication: vaccines designed around a specific antibody gene will not work for people lacking it; databases are very incomplete.
- Plans: the grant renewal aims to collaborate with large long-read whole-genome consortia (Estonia 10,000-sample project, a Thai newborn PacBio genome project, Middle East projects, Germany, US).

## Funding environment

- Despite campus-wide impacts, no large-scale genomics grants at her university were cancelled. Cancellations were mostly training grants targeted at diverse student groups.
- Recent $11 million Center of Biomedical Research Excellence grant on nutrition and metabolic health (she directs its Technology Development Core), with major multi-omics content.
- The Center of Precision Medicine is supported by a congressional appropriation from Senator McConnell, included in the March budget, which has been signed. The funds' release was expected "any day"; she noted his term ending and "radio silence" as a worry but said they had been reassured.
- She says core budgets are limited in the current grant funding environment, driving cost-sensitive behavior.

## AI and compute

- She says AI is "influencing everything." Her hope is that it enables integrated pipelines across three data layers on the same sample, unbiased searches for targets and biomarkers, and modeling of how inherited variants affect immune responses.
- Examples: campus small-molecule core uses AlphaFold and AI protein binding assessment for cancer targets; partnership with Twist, which has partnered with AI for protein folding and binding.
- Data quality: the high-quality genome standard now is T2T (telomere-to-telomere) assembly using Oxford Nanopore ultra-long reads, PacBio HiFi and strand-seq or chromatin conformation; her group recently onboarded Fiber-seq.
- **Compute costs:** systems bought two years ago for $3 million "now cost $7 million" due to data-center and AI demand. She expects compute (properly configured, secure for human data, run by trained staff) to be the bottleneck, and practical applications to be limited by budget and finding the right team.

## Concluding view

She says single-cell and long-read technologies will have "a massive impact on clinical and translational medicine," taking longer than expected, but "that change is coming and it's coming rapidly." "I wouldn't discount them quite yet."
