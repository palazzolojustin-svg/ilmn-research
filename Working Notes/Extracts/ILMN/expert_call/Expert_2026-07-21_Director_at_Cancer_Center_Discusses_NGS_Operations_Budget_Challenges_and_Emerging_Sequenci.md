# Expert Call (Channel Check): Cancer Center Molecular Lab Director on Illumina Volumes, a Mid-Double-Digit Budget Cut, NovaSeq X Plus Fit, Roche Axelios, and TruPath Interest

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-21_Director_at_Cancer_Center_Discusses_NGS_Operations_Budget_Challenges_and_Emerging_Sequenci.md` · Date: 2026-07-21 · AI-interviewer channel check (published 2026-07-24); expert is an associate director of a molecular diagnosis lab at a large NCI-designated cancer center in Pennsylvania (clinical oncology NGS, Illumina short-read plus access to Oxford Nanopore long-read) · expert call*

**Summary.** A clinical cancer-center lab reports Q2 2026 sequencing volume and Illumina consumable spend up about 20% year over year, helped by a hospital policy to bring tests in-house, but essentially flat versus Q1 2026. A hospital budget for next year, passed in July, was cut by a mid-double-digit percent (far more than the 10-20% the lab feared a month earlier), so the expert expects flat-to-lower volume in Q3 and Q4, no new instrument purchases for about two years, and possibly a request for further Illumina discounts. The expert still rates Illumina best on quality and cost per sample, praises DRAGEN, sees Roche's Axelios as a potentially big threat in one to two or more years, and would be very likely to try Illumina's TruPath long-read approach if it ran on the same machines.

# Expert Call Notes: Cancer Center Molecular Lab on Illumina (interview 2026-07-21, published 2026-07-24)

## Format and the expert's background

This is an AI-conducted "channel check" (an interview with a customer or other market participant used to gauge demand for a company's products). The interviewer is an AI; the expert's perspective is labeled "Doctor/Medical Professional." The interview was held 21 July 2026 and published 24 July 2026. The questions are a standard Illumina checklist covering volumes, consumables, pricing, inventory, competition, new products, support and purchase plans, so many answers are direct customer experience and a few are opinion or speculation (flagged below).

**Who the expert is** [Expert: associate director, molecular diagnosis lab, Pennsylvania cancer center, Jul 2026]:
- They are associate director of the molecular diagnosis lab at a large cancer center in Pennsylvania. They describe a dual role: operational oversight of clinical short-read sequencing (reading short DNA fragments, about 150 bases each) from Illumina for oncology NGS panels (next-generation sequencing tests that check many genes at once), plus access to an academic research core that uses Oxford Nanopore long-read sequencing.
- The facility is a high-complexity CLIA-certified lab (CLIA is the US regulatory certification for clinical labs) at a National Cancer Institute-designated cancer center. Customers are almost 100% clinical, serving the oncology division across the health system: solid tumors (lung, skin, breast, colorectal) and blood cancers. The lab does somatic mutation profiling (finding mutations acquired in tumor cells).
- They have many years of NGS assay experience. In a former lab they used Illumina and Thermo Fisher's Ion Torrent, with more experience on Illumina.
- The expert refers to "the last interview," implying they have been interviewed before in this series.
- Their stated framing: clinical diagnosis is growing "but growing modestly," and "our real pressure is on cost per sample" because the hospital budget is flat (and, as described below, then cut).

## Installed equipment and use

- **Two Illumina instruments** [Expert, direct experience]:
  - NextSeq 2000 (a mid-size sequencer): used for the targeted oncology panel and RNA-seq (measuring gene expression).
  - NovaSeq X Plus (a high-capacity sequencer): bought roughly one and a half years ago (later in the call the expert says "one year ago"). Planned uses are a ctDNA assay (liquid biopsy, detecting tumor DNA in blood) and a new comprehensive cancer profiling panel of about 1,500 genes for tumor profiling.
- Both machines are validated for all assays, based on a bridge study (a comparison study across the two machines), so every assay can run on either. The long-term intent is to move all assays onto the NovaSeq X Plus because of its large capacity.
- **Capacity problem:** the lab still cannot fill the NovaSeq X Plus. It is negotiating with its academic research team (population genetics) to run their samples and use the spare capacity, extending the lab's assays to non-clinical samples.
- **No other sequencers.** Adding a machine requires validation and state registration, which takes time, and the two Illumina machines already suffice. The Oxford Nanopore machine belongs to the research lab and is used for pilot studies and special markers. The lab has no plan to buy from PacBio or any other vendor.

## Q2 2026 volumes and drivers

- **Year over year (Q2 2026 vs Q2 2025):** roughly a 20% increase in both sequencing volume and money spent on sequencing-related reagents [Expert, direct experience].
- **Quarter over quarter (Q2 vs Q1 2026):** "almost no increase." The expert says they volunteered this again as "one special thing."
- **Main driver of the 20%:** a hospital policy requiring pathologists to send NGS orders first to the in-house molecular lab, and to outside commercial labs only if the in-house lab lacks the assay. Previously the lab handled only lung, skin and (the expert thinks) sarcoma; high-volume cancers such as breast and colon went to Caris Life Sciences (an outside commercial lab). The lab says it had lost "probably like half or maybe one-third of the volume" this way; the policy brings volume back.
- **Second factor: bioinformatics change.** The lab previously used an old version of QIAGEN's QCI pipeline (software that turns sequencer data into a clinical report), which needed much manual work and lengthened turnaround time. It brought in another commercial software vendor that cut manual work, left more time for scientific interpretation and report summaries, improved report quality and reduced turnaround time.
- **Versus expectations:** the interviewer characterized Q2 as falling a bit below expectations, and the expert agreed ("Yes. That is the major reason"), citing the flat quarter-over-quarter volume.

## Budget cut and outlook

- **Full-year 2026 vs 2025:** the expert expects "still the 20% increase or maybe less," with almost no increase in Q3 and Q4 and possibly lower volume [Expert, forecast].
- **Why:** the lab's budget for next year, passed in July 2026, was cut by "a mid double digit percent." The expert says it is a big change and "I don't know what is going to happen," attributing it to the whole health system trying to cut costs and to the molecular lab having spent a lot in the last year.
- **Ways to cut:** reduce staff, cut assay costs, cut some reagents. The expert says a mid-double-digit cut is "hard" to absorb and implies they may have to cut tasks or "something significant."
- **Change versus 90 days ago:** the expert is significantly less optimistic. A month earlier they expected a cut of 10%-20% at most, which they thought could be met with small cost reductions. They did not think sequence volume would change much then because sample volume was rising.
- Interviewer's summary (not clearly stated by the expert): volume could come down in the second half. The expert said "Nobody knows what's going to happen," so this is a prediction, not data.

## Consumables, pricing and inventory

- **Spend:** reagent cost tracks volume: about +20% year over year in Q2 2026, flat quarter over quarter. Illumina gave no further discount versus last year.
- **Price change in past 90 days:** none; prices "remained the same." The NovaSeq X Plus purchase already came with a big reagent discount.
- **Possible future negotiation:** the lab has not asked Illumina for more discount but may do so given the budget cut.
- **Flow cell tradeoff (flow cells are the consumable chips samples are loaded on):** larger flow cells hold more samples and cut cost per sample, but clinical samples need fast turnaround, so waiting to accumulate enough samples conflicts with turnaround. Bringing in research samples, even at a very low price to the research team, would fill flow cells and still save cost on clinical samples while keeping turnaround.
- **Inventory:** sufficient, because the lab always orders large batches of flow cells and reagents; no problems.
- **Lead times:** unchanged versus Q1.

## Competition and platform preferences

- **Illumina position:** "Illumina is still probably the best right now" on sequence quality and cost per sample [Expert opinion].
- **Complete Genomics (BGI):** might be slightly cheaper on reagents and instruments, but the lab would not spend another roughly $500,000 on a new machine, given validation and registration burdens, "unless it is a huge difference."
- **NovaSeq X Plus fit (expert's own opinion):** after the budget cut, the NovaSeq X Plus is "a little bit of a problem." For a ctDNA assay of about 100 genes, the expert would consider trading the NovaSeq X Plus for two more NextSeq 2000s (ending up with three NextSeq 2000-class capacity, or one NovaSeq X plus a NextSeq 2000 in other framings), to get enough capacity and faster turnaround.
- **Cost gap:** per the expert, a whole exome (sequencing of all protein-coding genes) costs a low-three-figure dollar amount less on the NovaSeq X Plus than on the NextSeq 2000 (the expert mentions "more than $200" as the NextSeq 2000 figure; the wording is garbled). The NovaSeq X Plus is cheaper but "not that big" a difference. For clinical labs, saving one or two days of turnaround matters more than that cost saving: "turnaround time trumps cost savings" (interviewer's phrasing, which the expert supports).
- **Roche Axelios (the interviewer named "AXELIOS one"):**
  - The expert says Illumina reads about 150 base pairs, while Roche's new sequencer reads about 1 kb (1,000 bases). They call it a "big jump" versus short-read, not equal to true long-read (Nanopore etc.) but solving some short-read problems.
  - It is new, needs full validation and clinical acceptance; the expert believes Roche acquired the company "early this year," will spend heavily to win research labs first and then clinical labs, and that acceptance would take "at least one year or two more years."
  - Historical caution [Expert, from memory]: about 10 years ago Roche bought the 454 sequencing company and closed the lab after two or three years. The expert says big companies may change their minds when a technology loses money.
  - Threat level asked over two to three years: "Still now, that could be a big threat."
- **Element Biosciences / others:** the expert believes they are still short-read, so no big difference, maybe slightly cheaper than Illumina. They know "a little" about PacBio.
- **Oxford Nanopore:** the expert follows it because they have access. They like that one run can give single nucleotide variants, structural variants and DNA methylation, ideally in one report for physicians, but "the Nanopore still have some problem" (unspecified).
- **Platform trends:** Illumina remains dominant.

## Illumina products and ecosystem

- **DRAGEN (Illumina's bioinformatics software for secondary analysis, i.e., turning raw reads into variant calls):** "super good" and effectively free when using Illumina reagents and machines. Before, with the QIAGEN pipeline, the lab had to build its own server and could process only one sample at a time; DRAGEN runs analyses in parallel, cutting turnaround. The lab now worries only about tertiary analysis (interpretation) and reporting. The expert urges Illumina to invest more in this for clinical labs.
- **Protein sequencing / SomaLogic:** the expert is interested in Illumina's protein sequencing; protein sequencing has historically been hard (2D gels, MALDI-TOF, assembling peptide sequences against databases). The lab discussed a pilot about half a year ago. The interviewer linked this to the SomaLogic acquisition.
- **SomaLogic acquisition impact:** "hard to predict." Likely good for the research market near term; for clinical, probably no change for at least two years because the technology is far from a clinical assay. It could be a factor drawing people to Illumina if rivals (they mention Element or BGI) lack it.
- **TruPath Genome:** the expert had never heard of it; the interviewer described it as technology extending reads beyond short-read, bridging to long-read. After that, the expert said they would be "probably very likely" to use it. Their reasoning: bringing the Nanopore workflow into the clinic requires registration and validation, but a long-read option on Illumina (maybe just a different flow cell) would let them pool short- and long-read libraries in one workflow, serve as both an upgrade and a Nanopore replacement, and remove the need for Nanopore. They also (mistakenly or loosely) recalled Illumina's failed PacBio acquisition, blocked by government. This interest is hypothetical, prompted by the interviewer.

## Equipment plans and support

- **Purchases:** no plan to buy any sequencer in the next 12 months, probably not in two years either; no change in the last 90 days.
- **Support:** satisfied. No communication with Illumina in the last quarter; in late 2025 (they say "two fall from the 2025") Illumina staff came on site and quickly solved problems with DRAGEN or the machine during the lab's launch of an assay.

## Market outlook (expert's views)

- Two clinical strategies coexist: (1) low-cost focused panels covering only the genes or variants needed for therapy choice, preferred by some community hospitals and small clinics; and (2) one comprehensive test (single nucleotide variants, copy number, structural variants, fusions), preferred by large health systems and academic centers. The lab offers both.
- Other approaches (DNA methylation, RNA-expression-based drug selection, single-cell analysis) are future directions but less mature than DNA or RNA sequencing; single-cell is still research and hard to translate clinically.

## Key takeaways for Illumina

- Demand: up about 20% year over year at this customer, flat quarter over quarter; pricing and lead times unchanged.
- Risk: a mid-double-digit hospital budget cut (decided in July 2026) could flatten or reduce second-half volumes, trigger a discount request, and prompt a possible NovaSeq X Plus swap for NextSeq 2000s. This is one customer's view.
- Strengths: quality, cost per sample, DRAGEN, support; switching costs from clinical validation and state registration protect the installed base against cheaper rivals.
- Watch items: Roche Axelios (expert: potentially big threat, but a one-to-two-plus-year path), Nanopore for multi-omic long-read, and Illumina's long-read (TruPath) and protein offerings.
