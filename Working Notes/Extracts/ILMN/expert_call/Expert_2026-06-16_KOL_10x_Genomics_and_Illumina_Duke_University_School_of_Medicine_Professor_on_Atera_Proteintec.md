**Summary.** An academic core-facility director who attended the AACR 2026 launch of 10x Genomics' Atera in situ (in-tissue) spatial platform explains why he sees it as a major step beyond Xenium, and why he is raising money to buy one (about $500,000) with delivery hoped for January-February 2027. ## Overview in the document's own summary

The summary block says the new platform (Atera) improves on existing technologies in sensitivity, throughput and cost, and that whole-transcriptome profiling is the major advance. ## Atera: what it is and how it compares with Xenium

Atera is 10x Genomics' new in situ sequencing platform. In situ means the molecules are read inside the intact tissue slice rather than after the cells are broken apart. Spatial genomics measures gene activity while keeping track of where in the tissue each signal comes from. Single-cell genomics separates cells and measures each one, which loses the tissue location. The expert's description [Expert: Duke core director, 2026-06-16]:

- **Overall view.** He sees Atera as "a significant improvement over the existing in situ sequencing platform like the Xenium platform."
- **Whole transcriptome.** The whole transcriptome is the full set of expressed genes. Atera can profile all of them, about 18,000 targets, instead of Xenium's targeted 480-gene panel. He compared this with Xenium Prime 5K (about 5,000 genes).
- **Sensitivity.** He said it is more sensitive than Xenium Prime 5K. Based on 10x's internal testing, it is "something like 1.3% more sensitive than the Chromium single cell." He added that this "obviously needs to be tested in the field." He later said that assuming it is at least as sensitive as Chromium is the key requirement for a whole-transcriptome platform.
- **Throughput.** It handles 4 slides at once instead of 2. The imaging field is larger, about twice the size, so more samples fit in the field.
- **Consumable cost.** The consumable price (the reagents used per run) is "around 60% of what the Xenium comparable would" be. In the Quantitative Insights table, the summary lists reagent cost at 60% of the comparable existing platform. He clarified that cost is both a per-sample and an experiment-level matter. Total reagent cost per run may be slightly more, according to details distributed on launch day, but the doubled field holds more samples.
- **Why whole transcriptome was not much pricier.** One might have expected it to cost much more. His view is that not all genes are expressed in a given sample.
- **Rebuilt optics and chemistry.** His understanding is that the optics were completely rebuilt. He hopes this avoids optical crowding (too many signals overlapping when many targets are read at once). The chemistry is still padlock-probe based, but it has been reworked for speed. Padlock probes are DNA probes with two arms that bind next to each other on a target RNA.
- **Speed.** A whole-transcriptome run is purported to take about 5 working days. A 1,000-target custom panel will be faster than what is available on Xenium now.
- **Roadmap.** 10x said at the launch that single-base in situ sequencing will be possible on Atera "sometime next year." He suggested this could address the ability to detect mutations, which is something Illumina's sequencing-based spatial approach offers.
- **Rebuild.** In closing, he said 10x rebuilt the Xenium "from pretty much the ground up" for Atera, which he called a "pretty forward-looking effort."
- **Workflow.** The workflow is expected to be similar between Xenium and Atera, though the chemistry is modified. He expects hands-on time to fall. A new machine setup takes some getting used to.
- **Availability.** Production is early stage. He understands there is a production run of 25-30 machines that are already assigned. 10x is not loaning machines out for trials. Shipping has not started.

## The expert's core: usage mix and spending

Mix of work. Until last fiscal year his core ran about 60% single cell, 20% Visium and 20% Xenium. Over the past 12 months single cell fell significantly, and the mix is now roughly a third each. He called it "a big shift."

Spend with 10x. He quantified consumables spent with 10x as follows.

| Period | Consumables spend with 10x | Comment |
|---|---|---|
| FY25 | About $1.2 million | Prior year |
| FY26 | About $600,000 | About 50% drop |

- The decline is attributed to "pressures coming from the administration and their funding of science." This refers to US federal research funding pressure.
- The $600,000 is split roughly evenly between Visium, Xenium and Chromium.
- Visium and Xenium were "pretty level" and "pretty stable." The big drop came from single cell.
- His interpretation: a combination of grant-funding financial pressure and "perhaps indicates a trend where people are moving away from the single cell more into the spatial profiling."

AACR poster observation. He looked at about 60 posters or presentations at AACR 2026:

| Technology | Approximate share |
|---|---|
| Visium or Xenium (10x spatial) | About 70% |
| CosMx or GeoMx (Bruker) | About 15% |
| Immunohistochemistry or multiplex immunofluorescence | About 15% |

He added that this year there were roughly equal numbers of single-cell and spatial posters, which he sees as evidence of "the trend away from single cell and more into the spatial context." He noted that the majority of spatial offerings are from 10x. (This is his informal sample, not a formal survey.)

## Cannibalization and replacement

- He thinks Atera will replace Xenium, since the workflow is similar.
- He sees Chromium and Visium being cannibalized "if anything," because Atera is whole transcriptome as they are, is more sensitive than Chromium, and Chromium is known to be more sensitive than Visium.
- For human and mouse work he expects people to move to Atera.
- The 3-prime Visium HD will keep a role for non-human, non-mouse species such as rats, macaques and other non-human primates, because it just amplifies the poly A tail of RNA. Visium HD gives near single-cell resolution.
- Integrating a Xenium or Atera whole-transcriptome dataset with a Visium whole-transcriptome dataset is difficult, "not impossible." The resolution differs, and he has not seen third-party software that integrates them. His advice is to finish existing Visium projects with Visium and start new projects on Atera.
- Xenium and Atera data are easier to integrate with each other because the chemistry is similar.
- Single cell still has a role. Examples he gave:
  - Chromium multiome (RNA plus ATAC-seq). ATAC-seq measures how open the DNA is, which reflects gene regulation. He uses the analogy of DNA as a concertina that closes to block gene expression or opens to allow it. Epigenetic change often comes first, then RNA change, then protein change.
  - Version 2 of the multiome chemistry is expected to be better than version 1, which "we found a little bit lacking."
  - Blood-based cancers, where spatial context is not needed.
- Atera "is not going to be a panacea for all your experiments."
- Scale comparison. A tissue section he routinely examines has 100,000-200,000 cells, versus 10,000-20,000 cells in a typical single-cell experiment. A billion-cell single-cell atlas could be done by pooling, but it is very expensive because of the sequencing needed. With Atera, 10 tissues of 100,000 cells each gives 1 million cells in one experiment.
- In closing, he said "the whole transcriptome is the thing that will replace the single cell."

## Why spatial matters scientifically

- Defining cell states (not just cell types) takes many more markers, and cell states carry the nuance of what is happening in a tumor or tissue microenvironment.
- Spatial context shows what cells near vasculature, near a necrotic tumor core, or around a multiple sclerosis (MS) lesion are doing. In Alzheimer's disease, it shows the effects of amyloid beta plaques and neurofibrillary tangles on surrounding cells. "You can't get that from single cell."
- Other use cases he gave: primary versus recurrent brain tumors; tumor type representation after chemotherapy and radiation; pre- and post-drug intervention changes in cell type frequency and state; immune cell infiltration and T-cell exhaustion.
- He said Xenium and Atera are a more reliable way to look at this than Visium.
- He also acknowledged "a little bit of having the shiny toy in the shop so people will come and use the core."

## Total cost of ownership and funding

- Instrument price: about $500,000. The moderator said that was what he had heard, and the expert said "That's my understanding."
- Service contracts cost around 10-15% (of instrument cost, implied), depending on inflation and financial pressures, and must be factored into what the core charges users.
- His cost analysis across Chromium, Visium and Xenium found that about 77% of the cost passed to core users for Xenium comes from reagents. A lower reagent cost, larger field and higher throughput, plus less hands-on time, should lower overall cost to core users. He believes lower prices will lower the bar for access and increase demand, with an uptick of people moving from single cell to spatial. His conclusion: "the TCO for the platform should be okay and is headed in the right direction."
- Funding approach. They are raising funds to buy one rather than evaluating a loaner. Routes:
  - Philanthropic money (being used to offset the purchase).
  - Repurposing existing grant funding where small equipment purchases are allowed.
  - Institutional funds.
  - Federal equipment grants are not being pursued. The next deadline is about October, and the award decision would take about another 10 months, meaning roughly 18 months before getting the machine. He does not want to wait: "It is a bit of an arms race when it comes to these sorts of technologies and conferring the competitive advantage for grants and papers."
- He says he needs Atera to be competitive: "you really have to... get it in-house to be competitive." He points to the combination of whole transcriptome, larger field, higher throughput and lower cost. He noted CosMx supposedly offered whole transcriptome at release but "didn't do it very successfully."
- Timing. If funds were secured "this week or next week," he expects delivery in about January-February 2027. Shipping has not started.
- Fleet plans. The core has 2 Xenium instruments. He may keep 1 for research-level diagnostics (developing an RNA panel as accurate as pathology-level diagnostics) or swap both for a single Atera.
- A new platform means new workflow, possibly accessory instruments, and a need to justify capex "over something that may not be tremendously different to what you already got." He will consult the core's sequencing lead.

## Proteintech Genomics (10x acquisition)

- The moderator said 10x recently purchased the Proteintech Genomics division. The expert called it "a very important rounding out of the central dogma," because RNA does not equal protein.
- He ran an alpha-phase test of the Xenium 25-protein module. Some proteins agreed completely with RNA; others differed. He attributes this to timing: RNA may be elevated and later drop off by the time protein is measured. This offers a "really synergistic view."
- Diagnostic pathology labs use panels of proteins. A tried-and-tested diagnostic protein panel could be used on a tissue sample, while RNA can identify novel proteins that characterize a disease state better. The detail may also catch tumor cell types missed by H&E (standard stained-slide pathology), immunohistochemistry or immunofluorescence.
- Competition. Lunaphore and Akoya (which he believed is now part of Conterix, "I think so") have moved from protein toward RNA, whereas 10x is moving from RNA into protein. If 10x can profile 40 or 50 proteins, there is more reason to choose a 10x platform over Akoya or Lunaphore, which profile only proteins. "With the Proteintech and the Atera, you can look at both RNA and protein in a spatial context."
- Demand. His group does multi-omics itself. In their Alzheimer's project they use immunofluorescence on the same tissue (not serial sections) to find plaques and tangles, then place RNA profiling around them. In brain tumors they study proteins defining tumor microenvironment heterogeneity. After whole-transcriptome discovery, he wants to confirm the protein is expressed, though this needs a good stain.
- He agrees (answering "Yeah, absolutely") that Proteintech should help 10x develop multi-omic assays so users need not go to third parties. In an early Xenium experiment they moved tissue from Xenium to another platform (a transcribed "fusion from a coil", likely a different instrument), and doing so is complicated without a designed workflow. The alternative is serial sections stitched together, which "isn't always easy."

## Competitive landscape

**Bruker (CosMx, GeoMx).**
- The expert thinks CosMx "lost a step" when its original owner went bankrupt and Bruker bought it. He does not know the IP or legal status of the chemistries, but says they "haven't really modified the chemistry per se." The summary table similarly cites a lack of chemistry updates and legal issues.
- Bruker's share of AACR posters was about 15% (see above).

**Illumina.**
- His core is benchmarking Illumina's spatial product, which he calls the "3 prime fresh frozen spatial platform." The moderator named it as StrataMap, and the expert agreed. It is the so-called mid-2026 availability product.
- Study design: Visium HD 3 prime versus Visium HD Pro (spelled "Pro base" in the source) versus the Illumina 3-prime product, chosen because they have the most similar chemistry. StrataMap has 1-micron features versus 2-micron for Visium HD. Both are sequence-based profiling platforms.
- They are testing on a mouse preclinical breast cancer model. Results are not yet available.
- Practical points. An Illumina user needs only a gasket and access to a sequencer, so no new platform is required. Cost may be an issue if heavy sequencing is needed, as may resolution or sensitivity.
- His expectation: it will probably come out "something like the Visium HD." He will see how it benchmarks.
- Strategic logic as he sees it. Illumina will want an end-to-end workflow (generate data on StrataMap, sequence on Illumina, back-end analysis), "book-ending" how profiles are generated. If it is less sensitive, more expensive or lower throughput, he would "question the need for doing sequencing." The one clear plus of sequencing-based methods is that they give sequence, so mutations can be identified in cells, which matters for lineage tracing or mutation detection in specific cell types.

**Element.** He knows less about it. He saw nothing about it at AACR. He called direct in-tissue sequencing an "interesting approach" giving cellular resolution, and will watch whether it is targeted or whole transcriptome.

**Singular.** He said Singular, which marketed itself as a quick-turnaround sequencing platform, "pivoted to being now a spatial company." Its issue is it looks at only 300 targets, and he says that even 480 targets is too small, based on work in his lab. "I don't know how they'll be competitive in the spatial field."

**StereoSeq (MGI/BGI).** He saw nothing at AACR. Geopolitical issues remain with accessing the technology, and Duke colleagues have had data sparsity issues.

**General.** "There's always market for an alternative," but he thinks scientists' comfort with 10x chemistry will make it easier to move to Atera than to adopt "a bit of an unknown." He would welcome another company prying 10x away from its lead in spatial.

## Implications for sequencing demand (Illumina)

- Chromium and Visium both rely on sequencing at the back end. Visium libraries at his core are sequenced primarily on Illumina.
- Duke's sequencing is a separate core. A shift to in situ profiling (Atera or Xenium), which reads molecules in tissue and needs no sequencing step, means fewer libraries sent. In his words, "ultimately we see less sequencing being done on the Illumina platforms."
- Cost pressure. They have considered moving off Illumina machines to Ultima (not at Duke, but at other cores around the country) because of lower cost for Chromium and Visium libraries. This is considered "simply because of the decreased price."

## Roche Axelios and long reads

- Axelios is Roche's sequencer. The moderator described it as giving slightly longer reads than Illumina.
- The expert has not considered it yet. He does not know its read length. He does not expect it to be as long as a PacBio read. An incremental length increase would have little influence on his work.
- His long-read project. Single-cell long-read analysis on fresh frozen tissue. He makes 10x Chromium libraries and, rather than fragmenting them for Illumina, sequences them on PacBio (the source says "Connex"; he later mentions their Revio) to find novel isoforms (new versions of known genes). Average cDNA read length is about 850 base pairs. They then put novel isoforms in spatial context using padlock probes, with one arm on a known exon and one on the novel exon, relative to Alzheimer's or MS pathology.
- He thinks long reads "still probably belongs with the PacBio and the Oxford Nanopore." They also have an Oxford Nanopore (ONT) instrument in a specific lab and have reached out to ONT about workflows.
- What would make him switch to Axelios: existing workflows serve scientists well, so Axelios would need to be significantly faster, cheaper or longer, or address accuracy or cost issues with PacBio and ONT or throughput issues with Illumina. "It would have to be that sort of change for people to move over." He speculated Axelios could offer faster or more accurate coverage, but does not know.

## Concluding views [Expert: Duke core director, 2026-06-16]

- Spatial profiling, RNA or protein, "is really coming into its own now." There is a switch away from single cell unless research depends on it.
- Spatial reveals what cells are doing in context, which single cell cannot replicate.
- Atera is "going to be more than an incremental change" and "a significant improvement over what is available in the market at the moment," on cost, throughput and whole transcriptome.
- He hopes this leads to "faster, quicker, cheaper diagnostics in the clinic."
