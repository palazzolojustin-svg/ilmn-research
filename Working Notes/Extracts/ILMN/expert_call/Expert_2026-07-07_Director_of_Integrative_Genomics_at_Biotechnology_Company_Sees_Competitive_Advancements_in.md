# Expert call (channel check): Director of Integrative Genomics at a California biotech on Illumina volumes, discounting, competition (Element, Ultima, Roche AXELIOS), TruPath and SomaLogic, Q2 2026

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-07_Director_of_Integrative_Genomics_at_Biotechnology_Company_Sees_Competitive_Advancements_in.md` · Date: 2026-07-07 · AI-interviewer channel check (expert network platform), published 13 Jul 2026; expert is a consultant, Director of Integrative Genomics at a biotechnology company in California (unnamed) · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer-side lab director with 20+ years in sequencing says their Illumina sequencing output and consumables spending rose about 20% year over year in Q2 2026, driven by volume (single-cell, multi-omic and AI/ML data demand), not price. Illumina applied an extra 30%-40% discount (no list price change) on the NextSeq 2000 largest flow cell used for single-cell work, which the expert reads as a response to cheaper competitors. The expert sees Element Bio (mid-output) and Ultima (very high output) gaining favor, Roche AXELIOS as a likely clinical niche, Illumina as behind in single-cell and spatial assays but strengthened by TruPath and SomaLogic, and capital purchases as budget-constrained.

## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

- The expert is a Director of Integrative Genomics at a biotechnology company in California. They oversee all genomics research and operations at the company and have more than 20 years of hands-on next-generation sequencing (NGS, the technology that reads DNA/RNA in bulk) and genomics experience, continuing to the present [Expert: Director of Integrative Genomics, Jul 2026]. The platform describes them as having visibility into sequencing technology assessment and purchasing oversight, and labels their perspective "Consultant".
- Relationship to Illumina: a current **customer**, not a former employee or competitor. The interview took place on 7 Jul 2026 and was published 13 Jul 2026. It was run by an AI interviewer, and the expert's experience is current (Q2 2026 spending and pricing are described from direct experience).
- The lab mainly serves the company's internal scientists. It supports work from experimental design and small bulk RNA-seq (measuring gene activity across a pooled sample) up to single-cell and spatial genomics (measuring gene activity cell by cell, or where in a tissue). A small wet-lab team does library prep (preparing DNA/RNA for the sequencer), custom library prep, assay development and sequencing runs. It uses Oxford Nanopore and Illumina platforms in-house and outsources to CROs (contract research organizations, outside labs hired to run work) for platforms it does not own.
- Caveat: this is one lab's view. Its figures describe its own volumes and discounts, not the market.

## Platforms the lab uses

- **Illumina in-house:** MiSeq (low throughput, e.g. CRISPR screening assays) and NextSeq 2000 (mid throughput, daily use, medium-size and fast-turnaround projects).
- **Illumina via CRO:** NovaSeq X Plus and other platforms for large experiments such as single-cell, whole genome sequencing and methylation analysis. The lab does **not** own a NovaSeq X.
- **Competitors:** Oxford Nanopore in-house for long reads and its unique library methods (direct DNA and direct RNA sequencing). Ultima Genomics and Element Bio ("TechBio" in the transcript) are used through CROs as needed.

## Sequencing volume and output

- **Drivers of volume (Q2 2026):** The main positive is single-cell and multi-omic (several data types from the same sample) projects. Single-cell tools now capture more cells per experiment and allow heavy multiplexing (pooling samples in one run), both of which raise read requirements. Larger datasets for AI/ML and large language model work are also pushing demand. The expert said some of the uplift is company-specific project timing.
- **Bottleneck:** Although sequencing cost per unit is falling, it "is still quite expensive when you start adding up all these single-cell experiments."
- **Seasonality:** Q2 2026 volumes were generally higher than Q1 2026, which the expert tied partly to company-specific new or existing projects.
- **Q2 2026 output vs Q2 2025:** Total gigabases (billions of bases of sequence data) from the lab's Illumina instruments rose roughly 20% year over year. It met the expert's expectations.
- **Full-year 2026 forecast:** The expert expects Illumina output to be up about 20%-25% versus 2025, for the same reasons (more single-cell/multi-omic work, company-specific project upticks, appetite for large datasets).
- **Change in outlook vs 90 days earlier:** Stayed the same or "gone up very slightly." The expert knew volumes would rise but thought it might be "a little more than I expected," because of larger dataset demand.

## Consumables spending and pricing

- **Consumables** are the reagent kits and flow cells (the consumable chip sequencing runs on) needed for each run.
- **Spend:** Q2 2026 spending on Illumina consumables and reagents was "slightly increasing" versus Q2 2025. The expert said it tracks data output, with a positive correlation, and agreed the expectation is a similar ~20% year-over-year increase.
- **Volume, not price:** The expert said the increase is "mostly due to the sequencing volume" and "definitely only based on the volume increase." Consumable prices have not risen because sequencing is getting cheaper; prices fell or stayed flat.
- **Discount in the past 90 days (direct experience):**
  - List price did not change. An extra discount was applied to one of the lab's most-used consumables.
  - The item is the NextSeq 2000 largest-output flow cell used for single-cell applications, previously "extremely expensive." Its price is now close to, or the same as, the second-largest flow cell, so the lab can generate the data it expects without spending more.
  - Size of the discount: about 30%-40% (the expert's estimate).
  - Why: Illumina never explicitly communicated a reason. The expert's guess is that competitors' cost per million reads (or per unit of data) has fallen significantly and that the discounted price now matches competitor prices. The interviewer summarized this as a competitive response to lower-cost platforms; the expert's reasoning is speculation.
- **Inventory:** The lab holds "sufficient" consumables, always somewhat more than needed but not excessive, because delivery is very quick.
- **Lead times:** "Quite steady for last six months, at least," with no complaints.

## Equipment pricing and competitive dynamics

- **Pricing environment for instruments:** The expert had not looked at the past 90 days specifically. Before that, there were trade-in incentives and price-matching on some consumables. From a user perspective, vendors have been more aggressive in price, negotiation and promotion.
- **Ultima Genomics:** For very large-scale experiments needing many reads, "a lot of people are using Ultima Genomics" because its price is very competitive.
- **Element Bio (mid-output):** For a new NextSeq 2000-class instrument, the expert feels buyers are more interested in Element, whose price is "more equivalent to a NextSeq 2000." Reasons given:
  - Instrument costs are not far apart, but Element's consumable price is more competitive than Illumina's, though Illumina is "mindful" about discounting.
  - Element sequences at very high quality and has other functions: cytoprofiling (profiling cells), in situ sequencing (reading sequence inside intact tissue), with more spatial capabilities on its roadmap. A platform that does more modalities is "in a way, the better investment."
- **Budget climate:** There is less appetite for CapEx (capital expenditure on equipment), and many CROs offer very high-throughput sequencing with fast turnaround.
- **Why preferences are shifting:** The field is moving to multimodal assays (sequencing that also captures proteins, spatial data or multi-omics on one platform). Quality remains non-negotiable: "Even if you can do five different things, if the quality is bad, it is not great options anyway."

### Expected share shifts over the next 12 months (expert opinion)

- **Element Bio:** "Definitely" has an edge with its multimodal offering, and probably still leads in a new mid-output purchase.
- **Illumina:** Has a "great roadmap" including protein, TruPath (its long-read technology) and spatial modalities, plus the advantage of being the largest installed base ("one of the biggest sequencing platform that everyone owns"). NovaSeq retains a competitive edge through higher throughput, more flow cell options and more modalities.
- **Ultima and Roche AXELIOS:** Both will gain traction. Ultima is very cheap and high throughput, although NovaSeq has even higher throughput. Roche's platform is interesting for its claimed flexibility and high accuracy (duplex reading, reading both DNA strands).
- Overall CapEx acquisition "is really up to economy as well."

### Roche AXELIOS

- The expert said they have not fully grasped its effect. It is a large upfront investment, and in academic settings adoption may not be easy when other sequencers exist.
- A niche likely exists in clinical settings that need flexible output control or fast turnaround. For general basic R&D, speed is not as critical. It is "hard to judge just yet because it is a big commitment."

## Purchase plans and budgets

- **New sequencer in next 12 months:** "Unclear."
- **Factors:** Budget (except for mid-throughput platforms), economic uncertainty, the ready availability of competitively priced CROs, possible additional headcount, how to incorporate data generated on the previous platform if swapping, and timing.
- **Change in past 90 days:** Purchase plans have "little decreased," because of budget and greater care in evaluating instruments before committing CapEx.
- **Sources of pressure ("both"):**
  - Economy: biotech and pharma spend more conservatively, and interest rates and uncertainty affect decisions.
  - Internal: fewer start-ups over the past year or so, and data infrastructure limits. More data demand (single-cell, AI/ML) means the company must find "a smart way to make data," since generation requires supporting infrastructure beyond consumables.

## New products and strategy

- **TruPath (Illumina):** The expert is excited by the technology, including long-read sequencing with minimal library prep, and by protein modality and more flexible sequencing options. Because the lab has no NovaSeq X, using it would be harder; likelihood of use is "likely if there's a right demand" (interviewer: "somewhat likely, depending on demand"). The technology is called "very groundbreaking."
- **Competitor products of interest:** In situ sequencing, especially Element Bio. The expert described how it could change Perturb-seq (single-cell CRISPR screens): today one makes separate guide-RNA libraries, sequences and maps back; in situ, one could read guide RNA directly from a cell in spatial context and do whole-transcriptome sequencing without disturbing tissue morphology ("very powerful"). 10x Genomics, while currently a visualization tool in this area, has discussed in situ sequencing on its roadmap in a webinar.
- **SomaLogic acquisition (Illumina's proteomics, i.e. protein measurement, purchase):** It adds value because it adds a multimodal capability. But users who see Illumina as a nucleic acid sequencing company are not well informed about protein use; "more the wow value has been added" than knowledge of how it will be used. It does not necessarily increase the expert's Illumina use now, since the primary use remains nucleic acid sequencing, but protein is an area the expert might expand into.
- **Future trends:** Protein sequencing, in situ sequencing, simplified workflows (shorter hands-on time, less library-prep manipulation), and multimodal, spatial and single-cell analysis with multiple data layers from the same sample.

## Illumina's positioning

- **Strong:** In whole genome sequencing and similar areas, Illumina is "well-positioned and very competitive."
- **Behind (expert opinion):** In single-cell and spatial, Illumina is "a bit behind" because its assays "are not as well streamlined as other platform or other application," with room to catch up to the pace of competitors.
- **Who leads:** Mostly application companies rather than sequencer makers. Among platform companies, Element Bio (multimodal cytoprofiling, in situ sequencing). Among others, 10x Genomics has "huge influence" in single-cell and spatial.
- **10x relationship:** Partnership or competition is possible but uncertain. 10x single-cell workflows still use sequencing at the end, and other spatial platforms are only spatial, so unless Illumina develops a streamlined workflow leveraging its platform, direct competition is unclear.
- **Complete Genomics:** Added by the expert unprompted. It also enables spatial omics, was recently acquired by "another Swiss company" (per the expert), and has quality described as "quite superb" based on literature the expert has read (hearsay). The expert said it "can become the meaningful competitor" and is an emerging company to watch.

## Customer support

- The expert is "quite satisfied" with Illumina tech support overall, and support has been consistent recently.
- Account management and connecting to the right people are "excellent."
- Phone tech support for troubleshooting "has not been great" and limited in extent, because many support staff are less experienced than before. The expert does not know the cause but "feel[s] there are a lot of new people." Usage is low because the lab rarely has issues.
- Impact: no real delays since the lab has used Illumina for a long time, but email troubleshooting is more tedious and slower ("friction issue than a hard stop," in the interviewer's words).
