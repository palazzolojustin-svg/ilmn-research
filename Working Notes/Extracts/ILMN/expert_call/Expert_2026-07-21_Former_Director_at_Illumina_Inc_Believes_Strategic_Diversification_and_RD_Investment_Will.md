# Former Illumina Director of Commercial Excellence on Illumina's Durability, Competitors (Element, Roche SBX, MGI/BGI, Ultima, PacBio, ONT), Grail, Diversification and Reimbursement

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-21_Former_Director_at_Illumina_Inc_Believes_Strategic_Diversification_and_RD_Investment_Will.md` · Date: 2026-07-21 · Expert-network interview transcript (published 23 Jul 2026; interview 21 Jul 2026); buy-side (investor-led) client interviewer; expert is a former Director, Commercial Excellence and Sales Enablement at Illumina · expert call*

**Summary.** A former Illumina commercial-enablement director (left roughly two months before the call) says Illumina is still the clear leader in high-throughput sequencing thanks to accuracy, installed base, service and software, though Element is the most real threat and Roche's SBX will start chipping away at NovaSeq X. The expert describes weaknesses (highest price, longer sample prep, leadership turnover after Grail, free giveaways by MGI/BGI), explains why bioinformatics was bundled free, and says clinical reimbursement is the biggest headache. The expert is more confident in Illumina's position than a year earlier, and favors partnering with pharma in the clinical realm.

## Expert background and how to weigh the views

The interviewee is a former Director, Commercial Excellence and Sales Enablement at Illumina (the leading maker of DNA sequencing instruments). The interview was conducted by a buy-side (investor) client on 21 Jul 2026 and published 23 Jul 2026. The expert's earlier career was in hospital administration, then spinal medical devices (previously Director of Clinical Professional Development at NuVasive, where they ran labs in New Jersey and San Diego and training programs, reporting to the Head of Global Sales), and then Illumina.

- Recency: the expert says "I left a couple months back" (roughly May 2026) and left "before it all wrapped up" on the SomaLogic acquisition, so knowledge of the most recent events is thin.
- Tenure: "the couple years I had been there" spanned the Grail leadership turnover.
- Role: Illumina hired the expert to build clinical-selling strength, because its sales force "wasn't equipped to sell in those clinical settings."
- Limits: the expert repeatedly says they are not a scientist (e.g., on long-read versus short-read science, "just enough to be dangerous"). Commercial, service, sales and hospital-side comments are direct experience; comments on Roche, Ultima, Thermo, legal matters and the future are secondhand or speculative, and are flagged below.

## Illumina's market position and durability

**Overall view.** The expert says Illumina "definitely still" is the leader. Historically it had "research market dominance" in academic and NIH-funded labs and large genomic centers doing population sequencing. The expert says that in high-throughput sequencers (machines that process very large volumes of DNA), "it's only Illumina. It really is." [Expert: former Illumina director, Jul 2026]

**Competitive threats, in the expert's ranking.**
- Element Biosciences was, as of the expert's departure, "the most real threat." The expert adds that Roche "will start chipping away, if you will, the high throughput, at least with NovaSeq X" (Illumina's current top-end instrument).
- Of the smaller challengers, the expert expects "one of those two is going to get absorbed by the other, for sure," and describes "five little people chipping away" that will "start slowly to make a dent." The expert later says this is not a "tidal wave" shift (the interviewer's summary, which the expert accepted in spirit).

**Strengths the expert cites (direct experience).**
- Accuracy: "They have the lowest error rates."
- Largest installed base and scale. Customers look at the number of publications that used Illumina sequencers around the globe. "You're not buying an instrument, you're buying it into the organization."
- Ecosystem: reagents (chemical kits), software, pipelines, validated workflows and a strong bioinformatics (data-analysis software) system.
- Regulatory experience built over years, supporting clinical customers.
- Service and support: engineers respond "under a minute" to calls; Illumina will run customers' samples at its own sites (San Diego or Baltimore) if an instrument is down; and Illumina can remotely monitor system health (not the customer's data) to schedule maintenance, e.g., service needed in three months. The expert notes this monitoring is "a bit tricky to talk to the customers about."
- Business model: "We're making our money from keeping the instruments running," i.e., consumables (reagents bought for every run) rather than one-off machine sales.

**Weaknesses the expert cites (direct experience).**
- Leadership and trust: "the entire C-suite leadership got turned over with that debacle of Grail," causing a culture shift and some loss of trust among scientists and R&D staff that is "still working through."
- Price: Illumina is "the most expensive, probably," with "high cost, premiums."
- Workflow: longer sample preparation than Element ("probably a little bit quicker"); some argue Illumina is more complex.
- Free competition: MGI and BGI instruments were given away for a while. "You really can't fight free there, so we had to figure out how to battle that."
- Element's economics: "they beat us on the lower operating cost," with "good competitive chemistry and simple workflows." The expert says all labs are evaluating Element because "it's actually cheaper than Illumina."

**Change in confidence.** The expert says that two years ago they would have said "Man, Roche is going to destroy us," but that is not the case now. Roche (see below) was "the initial punch in the face." The expert says Illumina's stock price "has ticked back up," recent acquisitions "continued to show their market dominance," "no one's running away from them," and "I feel way more confident now… than I did one year ago, that Illumina's still going to be king of the mountain." The expert adds "the science has always been strong." These are opinions, not data.

## Competitors in detail

### Roche SBX (new sequencer)
- The expert says Roche "is out and about now" but "we have not heard much about it." Two years ago the headline numbers "look[ed] amazing" and "had Illumina shaking in their boots," but Illumina is "a little bit more confident now."
- The interviewer reported hearing from scientists that Roche delayed the launch repeatedly, that "something's not right," and that Roche was not transparent on matters such as sample size. The expert agreed this is plausible: early-access labs are told to run tests "our way," so results look right, and the open questions are adoption, number of end users, availability of bioinformatics and biostatisticians, and the massive data volume.
- Lesson from NovaSeq X rollout (direct experience): security, firewalls and IT integration were "always a big issue, especially with hospitals." The expert speculates Roche will "fix it on the front end," since many people at rivals "started with Illumina, so they know the good, the bad, the ugly."
- Foundation Medicine (a big testing lab owned by Roche) currently uses Illumina machines per the interviewer's other experts; the interviewer asked whether it might switch to Roche SBX. The expert said monopoly concerns would not arise "yet" because Roche lacks market share.

### Element Biosciences
- Seen internally as the most real threat; cheaper to operate; simple workflows; "a couple out there" in the field. Labs are evaluating it on economics.

### MGI / BGI (China)
- Expert claims (partly secondhand): they "literally changed the names just so they could continue to sell in various areas"; a high-throughput sequencer was dropped off free at a US academic research center, and the lab later had to refuse it because of US regulatory and geopolitical restrictions. The expert thinks "it got cleaned up."
- "Illumina was banned from working in China for at least a year or more because of all these little battles with them."
- Strengths: lower sequencing costs, speed, rapid innovation, a large international footprint where they "grew a lot." Weakness: limited US adoption, and "without the U.S., it's hard to really grow the genomics space."

### Ultima Genomics
- "We were always more worried about Element." The expert says Ultima's sequencers looked "so big and bulky," seemingly needing an attached second system, which raises lab-space concerns (impression, not detailed knowledge).

### Thermo Fisher
- Focused on clinical diagnostics, PCR and more targeted sequencing, per the expert; not discussed as a major high-throughput threat.

### PacBio and Oxford Nanopore (long-read)
- Illumina "tried to acquire them [PacBio] for a little bit." The interviewer suggested the blocker was dominance across both long- and short-read markets; the expert did not confirm the reason.
- Expert's (self-described non-scientist) view: long reads suit clinical tests needing repetitive or long sequences; long-read has a smaller installed base and higher cost. PacBio "leads that race a little bit more" than ONT, which is "not supposed to be as accurate" and had "a lot more variable" workflows. Labs dislike pipetting-heavy, multi-step workflows; plug-and-play wins. ONT is noted as having good long reads and being more portable.

## Grail and Illumina's diversification

**Grail summary (expert's recollection; public-knowledge level).** The expert says a government body or the FDA (the expert is not specific) said Illumina could not acquire Grail because it would be monopolistic; leadership went ahead anyway and had to divest it "three to four years after the acquisition." Leadership "saw the dollar bill" of an oncology market cited as "$8 billion, or whatever it may be," and did not heed legal advice. There were court battles that "disappeared, so lucky for them."

**Why the expert still sees the clinical opportunity as the future.** Personalized medicine, companion diagnostics (tests that match patients to drugs), MRD (minimal residual disease, detecting leftover cancer cells), liquid biopsy (blood-based rather than tissue testing), pharmacogenomics, population genomics and newborn sequencing. Pitch: one quick sample can identify what is wrong with a hospitalized baby without repeated pokes and drugs: "the right therapy for the right patient at the right time."

**Fluent / single-cell and the repeat-risk question.** The interviewer noted that 10x Genomics dominates single-cell analysis using Illumina sequencers, so Illumina offering its own single-cell platform (via the Fluent acquisition) could raise similar exclusion or monopoly concerns. The expert agreed it is "a great question," said "we're going to definitely see what happens," and that they "don't know the legal ramifications." From an operations standpoint, selling a validated assay on the largest installed base is "such an easy sell" and "plug and play." The expert contrasted the valuations of assay/test companies such as Guardant with Illumina: "the valuations are crazy compared to Illumina and the companies building the machines." Roche's own choices on partnerships versus in-house development will be "even more interesting."

## Bioinformatics (DRAGEN) and multiomics

- Bioinformatics was "the biggest argument I took on for almost a year" at Illumina. The expert believes DRAGEN, Illumina's data-analysis software, "is the best," "but you're making pennies on it."
- Pricing detail: Illumina was trying to charge about $40,000 for the software on a roughly $1 million machine. The expert argued to bundle it in rather than sell it, and "they ultimately ended up doing that."
- Customer benefit: if software does the analysis, labs need not hire another bioinformatician, "a huge saving for them." With AI, bioinformatics should show "a lot of clear progress" and help in understanding pharmaceuticals and cancer treatments.
- SomaLogic (proteomics, i.e., protein measurement; the interviewer said it was acquired last year): the expert was unsure of timing, said multiomics (combining DNA, protein and other data) "is truly… the future of how they're going to grow," and that SomaLogic is "where they're trying to go with that," but left before it closed and "lost track."
- R&D: Illumina "is going to continue to invest in their R&D," which the expert believes is "one of the highest rates" of R&D support, though "you can't do it all yourself" so acquisitions are needed (opinion, no figures).

## Clinical adoption and reimbursement

- The expert calls getting into the clinical realm and ensuring reimbursement (insurers paying for tests) "the biggest headache, and it continues to be." The issue is whether enough lobbying effort exists "on the Hill" to get oncology and rare-disease tests reimbursed. "The commercial success depends on that."
- Illumina must also keep the ecosystem (R&D, academic institutions) happy and partner with big pharma, large labs and hospitals.
- Interviewer's input: testing companies (Tempus AI, Caris, Guardant, Foundation) say reimbursement is improving, but tests are not very profitable; selling accumulated data to pharma is more profitable. The interviewer asked whether Illumina might build a multiomics database to sell.
- Expert's answer (speculative): this is like any new market, as with Grail early on, which was not reimbursed so wealthy individuals paid out of pocket. Illumina must weigh building data to sell versus high-cost tests, cash payers, and finding a break-even point so the masses can be reimbursed. "I think staying in that clinical realm, especially with pharma, getting in bed with pharma is a good move for them."
