## Illumina's historical position and business model

**Direct experience / history [Expert: former Illumina staff scientist, Jul 2026]:**
- In the early days "there was only one player, and that player was called Illumina." After it joined with Solexa (it bought the chemistry for amplifying DNA on a surface), Illumina had "the monopoly." Early alternatives from others failed because they were too expensive.
- Control rested on consumables, workflows and library prep (the step that turns a sample into something a sequencer can read), mostly developed in-house from scratch with some academic help. Illumina also built sequencers needing less sample.
- One of the "most intelligent moves" was recognizing that sequencers generate huge data volumes and building a way to analyze them: the DRAGEN data-analysis system, which "put Illumina at the forefront."
- The business plan stayed constant: launch a new instrument, then sell the consumables needed to run it. The expert's analogy: "I'm selling you a car, but I'm the only one that can provide you with the gas." That model was "fantastic" and drove growth until new entrants arrived.
- Illumina combined research and product development under one umbrella. The expert sees this as smart, because research-grade experiments used for a paper must be rebuilt as products shipped to thousands of customers, and complaints damage reputation.

**Hearsay.** The expert says rumors (they say "more than rumors"), roughly 8 to 10 years ago, held that Roche tried to buy Illumina but could not afford it. The expert does not know why it did not happen.

**Layoffs.** The expert was at Illumina during a layoff. They had a team of 12, and it was cut to 3. They describe workforce ups and downs driven by the numbers and the need to "pay back to your investors."

## Competitive landscape and who is ahead

**Overall view [Expert opinion].** Illumina "started walking while the other players started running." Many rival leaders are ex-Illumina people who know how to navigate genomics. Illumina "is feeling that heat." The expert calls Illumina's decisions "very slow and erratic."

**Single-cell, epigenome, spatial transcriptomics.**
- Illumina recently released a new product, but others are "way ahead" in capabilities and resolution. 10x Genomics is named as ahead, and other platforms also compete.
- 10x Genomics entered spatial transcriptomics, and Illumina "didn't know what to do." Illumina had expected it to be easier after its single-cell RNA-seq platform, but spatial is "a completely different beast" because it involves tissue embedded in a medium, which means cell biology rather than purified DNA/RNA biochemistry, where Illumina is strong.
- The expert says that if asked which spatial platform to use today: "Not the Illumina one." They would pick 10x Genomics or BGI.
- Later the expert says Illumina is "still the pioneers" in single-cell and should focus there.
- BGI and 10x "started early... running instead of walking."

**Long reads (sequencing much longer DNA/RNA stretches per read).**
- Element Biosciences and PacBio are named as near-Illumina competitors. Their machines are "probably not" as good as Illumina's but "good enough" to be very close. PacBio also offers long reads.
- Expert claims long reads need less computing power because there are fewer reads (millions, not the millions-and-millions of short reads), and that quality is as good as short-read assembly.
- Expert's forecast of leaders: PacBio in long read; 10x Genomics in spatial transcriptomics (unless Illumina, Element or another surprises); Roche is "the one that I will be seriously concerned about."

## Multi-omics and proteomics

**Industry trend.** The expert says multi-omics (combining several types of molecular measurement) is embraced by clinical and medical fields: a diagnosis often uses more than a single SNP (one-letter DNA change) or deletion, and may need protein levels or the epigenome (chemical marks on DNA).

**Illumina's offering [Expert: assessment from what they have seen; not an insider view of current work].**
- Early stage and, at this point, "not strong enough."
- Weak points are depth and reproducibility. Illumina's depth "you start scratching on the surface, but you do not get into it."
- Illumina recently acquired SomaLogic (a proteomics company). The expert says the two need to "merge" in terms of biology and chemistry, not only as companies, and some unknowns remain.
- Core difficulty: Illumina staff are nucleic-acid chemists, and marrying that to protein-detection biochemistry is like "mixing water and oil." Integration is partial, which lowers resolution and reproducibility. This is true for other companies and labs too.
- Expert says colleagues (companies unnamed) tried with decent budgets and got only "a partial answer," which is fine for research but not for clinical diagnostics, where you need to be "98.5% at least sure."

## Long-read vs short-read for clinical and rare disease (GeneDx discussion)

**Technical reasoning [Expert].**
- Short reads (Illumina) require much analysis time and sufficient depth: "100X or 60X minimum" (coverage, meaning how many times each position is read).
- Long reads can be up to about 2 kb (2,000 base pairs) per the expert, versus roughly 400 to 500 for Illumina "if you are lucky." More power per read.
- For rare diseases driven by changing isoforms (different versions of a gene's RNA transcript, one of which becomes more abundant), long reads give a better view of the transcriptomic landscape. For SNPs and amino-acid changes, short reads are fine. For isoforms, RNA abundance or RNA topology (orphan or rare diseases), the expert would "definitely" build the workflow on long reads. Same logic for spatial transcriptomics.
- Short- vs long-read is "apples and oranges." Ideally long-read rechecks short-read results, but cost is the problem: clinical settings need low cost per test because of insurers or universal healthcare.
- Long-read challenge: library prep must preserve sample integrity (long molecules can break from pipetting forces), so sometimes it cannot be automated. Automation is possible with the right sample-prep instrumentation, and some companies offer it.

- Confidence that GeneDx (or similar big labs) adopt an alternate platform: "I would say 98.5%." Evidence: large clinical-rooted labs and papers in Nature, Cell and Science are moving to long-read platforms.

## Automation and workflow (direct experience)

**Where automation matters [Expert].**
- Beyond roughly 100 samples, consider automation. Several hundred samples per day: the manual workflow "is going to burn through your budget." About 40 samples per day: a cheap platform such as Opentrons (used at the expert's first start-up) may do.
- Automation is "significantly cheaper" than staff pipetting and cuts human error, though human checks are needed at critical points. Vendors named: PerkinElmer, Tecan, QIAGEN, Hamilton, Opentrons. The decision is an "equation" of sample input, workflow cost and what you charge the client.
- Running the sequencer needs an operator for initial steps and power or voltage problems; once running it is fine. Downstream analysis is easy with a good bioinformatician.

**The expert's case study (roughly three years ago, PacBio long-read; no NDA, details not company-named).**
- Setup: three operators prepared the same contrived samples (known answers), 10 samples each, 30 in total, as a proof of concept.
- First automation result: deviation from the known answer was 5% to 15% with humans, but close to 40% with the instrument. "Deviation" meant false positives and false negatives against a known threshold, in the expert's example above or below a cutoff.
- Root cause (after fishbone and design-of-experiments style troubleshooting): instrument calibration. On one platform the Z-axis was off, so the pipette tip hit the bottom instead of sitting above it and aspiration was incorrect, and the top-left and bottom-right corners of the deck were also miscalibrated. A second platform had to be bought.
- Time: about nine months of samples, experiments and engineer discussions. The expert's general rule: about a year, likely testing two automation systems. Reputable vendors will help to protect their reputation.
- Outcome: it worked. After fixing the hardware, deviation fell below 10% (nine-point-something). Then improving the library prep protocol and assay development cut the error to about 1.8% (1.77%), a 98.2% confidence interval, "enough to be able to start moving into CLIA" (US clinical lab certification) and writing papers.
- The client called the 40% figure "very high"; the expert agreed.

## Illumina's outlook and market structure

- [Expert opinion] Illumina "is not going to disappear" and is working "24/7" to stay at least one of the big players. But the pie must now be shared.
- Analogy: Illumina is Tesla ("was" the standard); alternatives are now equal or better (BYD, Ford, electric Ferrari), but Tesla still has a good reputation. The future is probably not as bright as 2001, but "they are going to be doing good."
- Strategic advice (personal, "I am not the CEO"): stop chasing "a bazillion different things." Focus on instruments, single-cell (where they are pioneers), and clinical genomics, and "start running" there. The expert says the lack of focus and erratic moves are what made them "fail" in a way. They also note that investors and the board influence such choices.
- Market structure forecast: genomics will resemble PCR machines in the late 1980s and early 1990s, which now have many vendors. The expert expects about 10 big genomics players sharing the pie, and elsewhere says "six, seven, up to 10 companies."
