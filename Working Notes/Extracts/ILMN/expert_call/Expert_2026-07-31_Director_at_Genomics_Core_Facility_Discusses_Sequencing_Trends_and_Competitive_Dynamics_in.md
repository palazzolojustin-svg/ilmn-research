# Channel check with the scientific director of an academic genomics core facility (Georgia) on Illumina demand, Ultima competition and equipment plans

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-31_Director_at_Genomics_Core_Facility_Discusses_Sequencing_Trends_and_Competitive_Dynamics_in.md` · Date: 2026-07-31 · AlphaSense channel check interview (AI interviewer); unnamed expert, scientific director of a sequencing core laboratory in Georgia; published 2026-08-04, interviewed 2026-07-31 · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer of Illumina, the founder and scientific director of an academic sequencing core, says the core's Illumina volume is flat to slightly up only because of a long-term HIV-community contract, while regular project volume is down. Lost work is going mostly to low-cost large providers such as Novogene (80-90%), but Ultima's share of lost work has grown from about 10% to about 20% over two years, and the expert expects this to accelerate. The expert remains happy with Illumina's product quality but is disappointed by sales support, unkept discount promises and lack of pricing flexibility, and plans to buy a MiSeq i100 Plus. No Illumina product is generating excitement for them.

# Channel check: academic sequencing core director on Illumina and competitors (interview 2026-07-31)

## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

- The expert is the scientific director of a sequencing core laboratory (a shared university facility that runs sequencing for other researchers). The bio describes them as a "Director at a Genomics Core Facility in Georgia" with visibility into direct oversight of sequencing and purchasing. [Expert: core director, Jul 2026]
- They founded the core about 15 years ago and are still running it, so their experience is current. They are a **customer** of Illumina, not a former employee or competitor.
- The core has used mainly Illumina sequencing technology. Its expertise is bulk RNA-Seq (measuring gene activity in a pooled sample), single-cell RNA-Seq (gene activity cell by cell), and more recently spatial transcriptomics (gene activity mapped to location in tissue). It has recently expanded into PacBio long-read sequencing.
- Staff is about 10-12 people: bench scientists, PhD managers and directors, and PhD-level bioinformaticians.
- Customers are mainly academic: the university community, the HIV research community, and researchers doing non-human primate work.
- The expert does not handle day-to-day customer support themselves (their managers do), so support-quality comments are partly second-hand.
- Direction of sentiment (per the AlphaSense summary): worried about Ultima's growing pressure; satisfied with Illumina product quality; disappointed with recent sales support and pricing flexibility.

## Instruments in use and planned

- Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (current high-throughput workhorse; the expert cites about $1 million price).
- Illumina NovaSeq 6000 (older high-throughput model), which the core plans to sunset (retire).
- An old MiSeq (small benchtop sequencer), described later as a 2015 unit.
- Competitor platform: PacBio Vega (a benchtop long-read sequencer, which reads much longer stretches of DNA), cited at $175,000.
- Planned purchase: Illumina MiSeq i100 Plus.
- The core has done no Ultima sequencing at all. The expert says they have been "largely resistant to using my core for Ultima sequencing."

## Drivers of the core's sequencing volume (Q2 2026)

The expert named two main factors, based on looking at the data in aggregate and from direct experience. They said it is hard to cover all minor factors.

1. **A long-term contract** to provide sequencing services to the HIV research community. It drives many current projects and much current work.
2. **A downturn in library-sequencing projects from external labs.** Two causes:
   - A gap of about a year to a year and a half in acquiring and installing the NovaSeq X Plus, during which the core had to keep using the NovaSeq 6000. Because the X Plus is cheaper per unit of data, customers sought vendors that could offer X Plus prices.
   - Increased pressure from Ultima Sequencing (a low-cost sequencing competitor) on campus, pushing investigators to send projects directly to Ultima or to Ultima providers, such as the University of Minnesota.

### Where lost volume is going

- Roughly 80-90% of the volume the core has lost is going to big centers such as Novogene, which offer cheaper sequencing. [Expert estimate]
- Ultima's share of the lost work started around 10% about two years ago and has crept up to about 20%. The interviewer summarized this as growth "from around 10% to about 20% over the past two years." The expert described the Ultima effect on volume as "significant."

## Output, spending and forecast

- **Q2 2026 vs Q2 2025 output (gigabases, i.e. billions of DNA bases read):** The expert "would guess" it slightly increased, driven by the contract. They estimated that counting only regular core projects as in prior years, output would be down. So the contract masks an underlying decline.
- **Annual trend:** 2023 and 2024 were the core's largest years. 2025 was "a slight decrease of about 20%." (The expert's wording is "slight" but the figure is about 20%.) 2026 numbers are not yet available.
- **Versus expectations:** Q2 2026 output is meeting expectations. Including library preparation services, the core typically sees "high six figures to low seven figures" of dollars per quarter, and appears on pace. These figures are total project cost billed to investigators, not gigabase output.
- **Full-year 2026 vs 2025:** The expert believes Illumina output will be approximately the same, because of the contract work.
- **Change in outlook over the last 90 days:** They did not know whether the forecast is more or less optimistic than 90 days ago, and said nothing had changed in the last 90 days. Over the last six to twelve months, however, rising Ultima pressure and penetration on campus has given them "significant concern."

## Why the expert expects Ultima pressure to accelerate

This is the expert's own reasoning, partly direct observation and partly speculation ("I imagine").

- Ultima has reached out to important stakeholders, such as principal investigators (lead researchers) with large program grants, and convinced them to send libraries to Ultima as a proof of principle that RNA-seq and single-cell RNA-seq can be efficiently switched from Illumina.
- Another core on campus that mainly outsources (it used to be this expert's group) now sends work to Novogene, has been outsourcing samples to Ultima, and serves as a hub.
- A further on-campus outsourcing core has been working with Ultima to beta test. The expert imagines it will at some point show decent sequencing results at lower cost than Illumina and keep pushing projects to Ultima providers.
- Expectation: Ultima pressure will "Accelerate" rather than erode slowly.

## Consumables and reagents

- **Spending, Q2 2026 vs Q2 2025:** Increasing, driven by the contract work. The increase is "mostly volume-driven."
- **Pricing:** "We have not had great success in negotiating volume-related discounts in reagents." The expert was not sure whether Illumina's consumable and reagent prices changed in the past 90 days, and does not feel Illumina's pricing flexibility has changed much.
- **Inventory:** Sufficient.
- **Lead times (past 90 days):** Stayed the same.

## Illumina relationship, sales and support

- The expert is "slightly disappointed" with their ability to strike arrangements with Illumina. In some cases they took delivery of instruments on Illumina's schedule to help Illumina meet its local quarterly targets, on the promise of discounts or small machinery (wording as in the transcript), and those promises were not honored.
- This has not significantly affected the relationship or willingness to work with Illumina. "I feel that Illumina still provides a very excellent product."
- Staff feel support is less effective than in the past. A longstanding Illumina representative left the company about a year ago. Newer reps tend to be more junior with less ability to work out deals.
- Support score (the expert's guess at their managers' view): about 6.5/10 overall. Instrument maintenance issues would be about 8.5/10, and the expert believes managers feel they get adequate support on field application scientist (FAS) and servicing matters. Drags on the score are turnaround time, slower sales support, and agreements not honored.

## Equipment pricing and competitive environment

- **NovaSeq X Plus:** About $1 million is "reasonable."
- **MiSeq i100 Plus:** Expected to be an effective tool, but a price "over $100,000" will limit wider adoption. A lower price would "democratize sequencing a little bit more" and put Illumina in many more labs. It would also help cores run large library batches on the i100 first, before moving to the NovaSeq, for more efficient sequencing.
- **PacBio Vega** at $175,000: "okay" price point. The expert is unclear on Oxford Nanopore's pricing or fit.
- **Price concessions in the last 90 days:** None seen from Illumina or competitors on equipment.
- **NIH equipment grants (direct experience as a reviewer):** The expert reviews equipment grants for the NIH. They have seen more smaller labs applying for Element sequencers, which they feel are too highly priced, and have seen no proposals for Ultima sequencers in those requests.
- **Share shifts:** Several smaller cores are switching to Element, probably because of lower capital cost. They have not yet seen many cores switching to Ultima, but expect universities to buy more Ultimas as they understand the power of sequencing combined with basic science and clinical genomics.
- **Why labs switch:** For small labs, capital cost of mid-tier sequencers is the main driver. Ultima adoption is driven by "vastly superior sequencing cost."

### 12-month share outlook [Expert opinion]

| Platform | Expected share trend |
|---|---|
| Element and other small platforms | Consistent |
| Illumina instruments | Maintain current share |
| Ultima | Increase |
| PacBio | Relatively flat |

PacBio is expected to have a bigger role in specialized scientific applications but not become widespread in basic research because of price. The expert has only recently seen literature justifying long-read sequencing in clinical genomics.

## Equipment purchase plan

- The core plans to buy an Illumina MiSeq i100 Plus within 12 months. Plans have not changed in the past 90 days.
- Reasons (the transcript also calls it "iSeq 100" in places, apparently meaning the same i100 Plus):
  - The 2015 MiSeq has poor read-two quality, poor cost per base and slowness.
  - Clients needing longer reads, such as 16S microbiome sequencing, need it.
  - Shallow-sequencing big libraries before running them on the NovaSeq.
  - The read length suits antibody repertoire sequencing. BCR (B-cell receptor) repertoire sequencing is currently outsourced to a lab with a NextSeq, and they want to bring it back in-house.

## New products and innovation

- Illumina: The expert is "not really aware of new products from Illumina that are driving any innovation" and nothing on the roadmap has caught their attention.
- **TruePath Genome:** The expert did not know it at first. The interviewer described it as combining short-read sequencing with additional data to resolve difficult regions. The expert is "Not at all likely" to use it. Reasoning: for the complex immunoglobulin loci, they use PacBio on whole genomes or enriched loci, with 10 kb circular consensus sequencing, assemble contigs directly without inference, and validate with repertoire RNA sequencing. "I don't believe that short-read data without long-read coverage over repetitive regions can adequately or efficiently resolve complex areas of the genome."
- **Competitors:** They do not consider short-read competitors to be competitors for Illumina other than Ultima and its cost per base, and have not investigated alternatives.
- **Roche AXELIOS:** The expert is familiar with it. The Roche team recently presented to them and other large stakeholders interested in clinical genomics, with a "fairly persuasive pitch." They do not believe it will compete in basic science. The main advantage is speed, which could affect clinical genomics labs, particularly newborn testing. Within the next year they do not think it will be significant. If it gains traction and FDA-validated assays, it could become a reasonable alternative to Illumina. [Expert opinion; "Very early to tell"]
- **SomaLogic acquisition:** The expert asked what SomaLogic does (the interviewer explained it is an aptamer-based protein profiling company, SomaScan). They said it is hard to say whether it helps Illumina, as they know proteomics less well than genomics; it is unlikely to change their use of Illumina.

## Expert's view on the future of sequencing

- Cost per base is the driving trend. Impactful basic science increasingly requires genomics for high-impact papers and grants. It is "a little bit of a have and have-not field": small labs struggle to get sequencing done and big labs do it easily.
- Pressure in clinical genomics will keep pushing sequencing cost down. The main trend is increased pressure for cheaper sequencing.

## Caveats on source quality

- Single-expert, single-site view of an academic core in Georgia; figures are estimates and guesses (e.g. "I would guess," "I don't know").
- The expert's revenue figures are core project billings, not Illumina revenue.
- Several answers (price changes, forecast change, SomaLogic, TruePath) were given with limited knowledge.
