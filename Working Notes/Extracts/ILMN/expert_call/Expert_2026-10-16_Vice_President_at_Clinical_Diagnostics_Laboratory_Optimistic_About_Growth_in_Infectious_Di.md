# Channel check with a VP at a Texas CLIA-certified infectious disease reference lab (all-Illumina clinical fleet; Roche Axelios, TruPath, pricing, reimbursement)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-16_Vice_President_at_Clinical_Diagnostics_Laboratory_Optimistic_About_Growth_in_Infectious_Di.md` · Date: 2026-10-16 · AlphaSense AI Channel Check (AI interviewer); expert is a customer, Vice President at an independent commercial clinical diagnostics laboratory in Texas; published 2026-10-20, interviewed 2026-10-16 · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer-side expert at a CLIA-certified reference lab running about 50,000 infectious-disease samples a year says all clinical sequencing runs on Illumina (MiSeq i100 Plus and one NovaSeq X), chosen for reliability, service and acquisition cost. Output grew roughly 5%-10% year over year in Q3 2026, with 5%-15% expected for 2026 and 3%-5% for 2027. The expert is open to evaluating Roche's Axelios one as a complement but says CLIA validation burden is a big switching barrier. Consumable prices rose across the board, offset by efficiency gains, and reimbursement is flagged as the main growth constraint.

## Expert background and how to weigh this call

- The expert is a Vice President at an independent commercial reference laboratory in Texas that is CLIA-certified. CLIA (Clinical Laboratory Improvement Amendments) is the US certification regime that lets a lab report patient test results. The lab serves hospitals, physician offices and some direct-to-consumer testing. [Expert: customer, VP at clinical diagnostics lab, Oct 2026]
- The AlphaSense bio says the expert has visibility into laboratory operations and physician communication. The summary says they oversee high-throughput infectious disease sequencing workflows and instrument fleet management.
- Relationship to Illumina: a customer, with current hands-on experience (interviewed 16 Oct 2026, covering Q3 2026). The interviewer was an AI, so questions follow a fixed script and some prompts contain facts supplied by the interviewer, not the expert (flagged below).
- The expert could not give sequencing output in gigabases (units of sequence data). They said only that the lab runs about 50,000 clinical samples a year. Several figures are approximate recollections: they said "I don't have the exact numbers on hand."

## Lab profile and fleet

- Workload: 100% infectious disease testing on the clinical side. None is oncology, hereditary or rare disease, reproductive health or residual disease monitoring.
- Short-read sequencing (reading short DNA fragments, Illumina's strength): multiple MiSeq i100 Plus instruments (Illumina's benchtop sequencer) plus one NovaSeq X (Illumina's high-throughput production sequencer).
- Long-read sequencing (reading long DNA fragments): a PacBio Sequel IIe, used only for research-use-only (RUO, not for patient diagnosis) testing.
- Clinical mix: 100% short-read. The RUO offering "quite often" uses PacBio. Vendor share of output did not change in Q3 2026 in either short or long read.
- Share by vendor: short-read clinical is 100% Illumina. Long-read RUO is 100% PacBio.
- The lab moved from the MiSeq to the MiSeq i100 Plus "about a year and a half ago" and bought several i100s then (the expert also called it "last year"). So no clinical sequencer updates are expected in the next 12 months.

## Demand drivers, headwinds and tailwinds

- Headwind: past supply chain issues hurt output.
- Tailwinds: more efficient sequencing chemistry; platforms that generate far more data per run; and rising incoming sample volume, which the expert says has historically increased with "wider adoption in the medical fields."
- Fastest-growing infectious disease applications (expert opinion): those where there is a pain point in the existing workflow, such as cultures that are often negative because the sample is hard or patients were on antibiotics before collection. Samples from sterile body sites also grow fast because interpreting an NGS result is less difficult.
- Slowest: samples from body sites with a normal microbiome, which complicates interpretation.
- Direction of the clinical mix: "more data is better data." More sequencing gives better resolution and access to more of the genome, possibly strain-level resolution with full genomes.
- Barriers to more long-read/whole-genome clinical work: higher cost (fewer samples per run on current chemistries, deeper sequencing needed) and a heavier bioinformatics burden.

## Why the lab is all Illumina

- Acquisition cost of the machines is "favorable compared to some of the other ones" they have looked at or had in the lab.
- Reliability is "the big point." The lab keeps multiple machines for redundancy, and sees more responsiveness and better collaboration from Illumina service staff.
- Throughput: "We're always looking to increase throughput... You got to be able to fill the flow cell, otherwise your cost per sample is pretty high." (A flow cell is the consumable slide on which sequencing happens.)
- Oxford Nanopore: evaluated about five years ago and rejected because error rates were too high for their work. The expert said that may have changed but they have not re-evaluated.

## Roche Axelios one

- Not adopted and not evaluated: no platform in-house and no data run through their pipelines, so no opinion on performance. The interviewer supplied the descriptions (reusable sensor, RUO launch).
- Interest: the published technical specs "look interesting." The expert said the reusable sensor "will be very useful," and Roche's long history matters because reliability is crucial. If pricing were better or chemistry more efficient after comparisons, Roche "would certainly be a good counterpart to our Illumina systems," not a replacement, "at least at first." "Everything I've seen looks pretty strong."
- Timing: the lab is interested in evaluating it, but heavy ongoing assay development could get in the way over the next 12 months. They are "always trying to look at what the new toy is."
- Threat to Illumina (speculation): "I don't know how threatened Illumina feels." More competitors are appearing and "Roche is certainly not one to ignore," but Illumina is "still pretty comfortable" because clinical users face heavy validation. A lab with a validated Illumina machine will run it "until you can save a lot of money by making a switch, or you can improve your turnaround time, or you can just improve the quality of your data significantly."
- Validation burden (direct experience): bringing on a platform takes a long time with a broad test menu. Every sample type must be validated on the new machine, "hundreds of samples" per type, at "a huge cost." The lab would only switch for a significant decrease in cost per base or per sample, or improvements in turnaround time or genome coverage. The expert did not say validation would stop them from considering it.

## Illumina products

### NovaSeq X
- One unit, validated under the lab's CLIA certificate for a single sample type only.
- Reasons for limited use: more costly; it is used for shotgun sequencing (reading all DNA in a sample), whereas the i100 Plus is used for targeted sequencing (amplifying specific regions first). Targeted is cheaper because it needs less depth, has simpler and faster bioinformatics, and gives quick turnaround to the typical clinical answer, an organism ID. Using shotgun when not needed "is not the most economical thing."
- Current use: run once or twice a week (it has two flow cell sides), reserved for "boutique" projects needing whole-genome coverage and access to bacteria, viruses, parasites and fungi together. The expert sees growing interest in such broad approaches and expects "more use out of that machine" in the next few years.
- It is very expensive and the lab has only one. Redundancy matters because "if you go down, you've got patients waiting on you."
- The interviewer cited Illumina commitments of up to 40% more NovaSeq X flow cell output and about 30% faster whole genome sequencing. The expert said faster turnaround "would certainly help."

### TruPath Genome
- Interviewer context: launched Feb 2026, library-prep-free whole genome workflow, list price about $395 per sample.
- The lab does not use it. Whole genome sequencing generally needs a cultured isolate (bacteria, fungi), but the lab gets direct clinical samples and often competes with culture. If culture yields an ID, the hospital stops and runs susceptibility testing (AST) unless doing infection control tracing.
- NGS is often used precisely because culture failed: "You can't sequence what you don't have." So TruPath is largely irrelevant to them.

### SomaLogic and multiomics
- The expert was not familiar with SomaLogic (interviewer: Illumina closed the acquisition in Jan 2026; a proteomics company). So no effect on their Illumina volumes.
- Multiomics is "absolutely something that's interesting" and will matter more over time, since NGS alone cannot answer all questions and transcriptome or proteome data could supplement what infectious disease doctors get. Not a plan for the next 12 months, but worth watching.

## Capital spending and instrument terms

- No plans to acquire, replace or upgrade clinical sequencers in the next 12 months.
- No acceleration, delay or cancellation of placements in Q3 2026.
- 2026 instrument capex is lower than 2025 (no percentage given) because of the i100 purchases.
- Instrument discounts and placement terms were unchanged in Q3 2026. Vendors say the lab is "at present, maxed out on our discounts."

## Consumables, pricing and inventory

- Q3 2026 consumable spend was about flat year over year: efficiency from the i100 systems lowers cost, offset by inflation.
- Prices are "a little bit higher," with "increases across the board." Extraction kits "went up a good bit." No percentage given because it varies by workflow step. The lab pays more per unit but uses a little less, with more volume and more efficient chemistry.
- Reagent discounts and commitment terms were unchanged versus the prior quarter.
- Inventory: past shortages taught the lab to hold about one month of consumables at all times. That has proven sufficient "as long as nothing catastrophic happens."
- Lead times were unchanged overall, with occasional short backorders.

## Vendor support

- Very happy with Illumina service, which is why it is the clinical fleet.
- PacBio: the Sequel IIe is expensive, has no backup, and "has a history of going down." PacBio has not been able to get a field service technician out within 24 hours, and downtime would likely be "a week or more," so the lab has reservations about clinical use.
- Support levels were unchanged in Q3 2026. One sales rep moved to another field; the transition was "pretty seamless."

## Reimbursement and other risks

- "Reimbursement is a tough spot" for clinical NGS. The expert believes (not certain) oncology is reimbursed better than infectious disease. Labs must know how they will get paid before entering the space.
- Better industry guidelines leading payers to reimburse more is the main upside to the 2027 growth baseline.
- Other constraints named: validation cost and time, redundancy and capital cost of high-end instruments, interpretation of microbiome-heavy samples, and bandwidth for new platform evaluation.

## Caveats

- This is a single customer from a niche segment (infectious disease only, Texas, one NovaSeq X). Percentages are rough recollections, not records.
- Speculative views: Axelios threat to Illumina, NovaSeq X growth, oncology reimbursement, and whether Nanopore error rates have improved.
- Interviewer-supplied facts (TruPath price, SomaLogic close, NovaSeq X roadmap, Axelios features) were not verified by the expert.
