**Summary.** A former Illumina product-development leader (about a decade at the company, not customer-facing) says Illumina is very well positioned in clinical sequencing, especially oncology and minimal residual disease (MRD), thanks to its installed base, reputation and scalable NovaSeq platforms. He sees Roche as the most credible new challenger but says its real-world traction is unproven. He argues that turnaround-time and price-per-genome claims need to be judged on the full sample-to-answer workflow, that validation costs make switching existing assays unlikely, and that the main long-term risk is a disruptive technology such as nanopore applications or protein sequencing.

## Illumina's roadmap and strategic shift

- **Market share erosion.** When the expert joined, Illumina had the largest share of any sequencing company, "in the region of 80%". Over the following eight years that share has eroded. [Expert, Oct 2026]
- **From innovation to commoditization.** Illumina's launches focus less on "pure innovation and excellence in sequencing" and more on the "commoditization of sequencing" (making it a routine, lower-cost business) so that it can face increasing competition. The expert says the current roadmap reflects this.
- **Research to clinical mix shift.** When the expert joined, most sales went into research. The contribution of clinical and clinically related work has since increased "dramatically", which changed the focus of launches.
  - Earlier on, Illumina made general sequencing products.
  - Now it focuses on minimal residual disease (MRD, detecting tiny traces of cancer DNA in blood after treatment) and oncology applications.
  - The expert says Illumina has called oncology its major growth area over the last three or four years.
- **Multi-omics push.** The expert says Illumina, like other sequencing companies, is trying to move beyond genomics to multi-omics (looking at DNA, RNA, protein and everything associated with a disease).

They want diagnostics for oncology, infectious disease or general human disease. They want to put in a sample and get a yes/no answer on whether a disease exists and what state it is in.

## Turnaround time

- **Headline numbers (expert's recollection).** Roche has cut turnaround to eight hours, and the current Illumina turnaround is 23 hours. The client later contrasted 1 hour or 8 hours versus 23 hours. [Expert, Oct 2026]
- **Can Illumina respond technically?** The expert said they cannot comment at a technical level. They noted "a lot of marketing" and that turnaround matters differently in different markets.
- **Practical difference in a medical lab.** Eight hours means a run started before going home yields an answer the next morning; with Illumina the answer comes a little later. For oncology and whole-genome applications (not rapid diagnostics), the 8-versus-24-hour difference "is not as great" as it would be for point-of-care use, where qPCR and older technologies can give a sample-to-answer in an hour.
- **Clients and clinicians also need time.** In response to the client's questions on NICU, cancer monitoring and rare disease, the expert said patients must be prepared, with counselors and a consultant to discuss and interpret. The client added that follow-up appointments may be days or a month away.
- **Where speed matters.**
  - Sepsis, where every hour is critical: "that's just not realistic at the moment" for sequencing.
  - Some infectious disease screening: not very helpful at current speeds.
  - Newborn genetic tests: still done with old technologies, not NGS, because "NGS is just not quick enough to react over the baby's first few days".
  - NIPT (non-invasive prenatal testing, screening fetal DNA from a mother's blood): a very large segment of current sequencing use that would benefit from speed, though sequencing is the only technology that delivers the needed results.
  - Pathogenesis/infectious disease would also benefit.
- **The client's point on volume.** The client noted that research is a minority of Illumina volume but probably still around 35% to 40% of total volume. The expert did not give a specific view on research turnaround importance.
- **Measure the full workflow.** Companies, Roche included, often cite only the sequencing step. More data is emerging on sample to answer (from patient sample to result).
  - PacBio and ONT require very long DNA, so library prep can be complex and slow. Runs can be short, but the skilled labor needed is enormous.
  - Roche integrates its sequencer with a liquid handler and on-board compute, so its time-to-answer depends on all of them. But capital expenditure is higher because the liquid handler and sequencer are Roche-dedicated, whereas Illumina has been "a little bit more open source".
  - The expert called Illumina's own data "quite clear".
- **Direction of travel.** All sequencing companies are trying to make sample-to-answer as quick as possible. The expert thinks nanopore-style sensing (an almost instantaneous result from the first few bases, rather than flow cell and fluorescent detection used by Roche, Illumina and Element) "is where sequencing needs to go". The expert is "absolutely convinced" Roche, Illumina and other pharma and diagnostic companies are exploring it. This is conviction rather than inside knowledge.

## Accuracy and quality scores

- The Q score is the confidence that each base (DNA letter) is correct. The expert says it is used as marketing. It started at about Q10 (a low score). "I think Illumina just said that they are looking at Q70 or above."
- MRD needs the highest accuracy because only a few molecules of interest exist in blood or the sample is small and valuable. A single change could decide the clinical path.
- For prostate or breast cancer, where larger samples exist and other diagnostic signals confirm disease, a lower Q score can still be confident.

## Long-read and synthetic long reads

- ONT and PacBio long reads (reading a single long molecule) are high quality. Many sequencing labs have a PacBio machine, often an ONT too, and cross-check long-read results against current output.
- In research, the expert says single-molecule long reads give confidence that you are looking at the actual genome, not an assembly of fragments, "which perhaps has never been seen before. That is just the way forward."
- **Illumina's synthetic long-read advantage.** Illumina offers a kit usable on an existing sequencer. There is some bioinformatic input to present synthetic reads like single-molecule reads. Customers avoid investing "$500,000, $1 million into a completely new machine", retraining staff and a new workflow. The expert says Illumina "definitely has the advantage" there, in academia or medical settings.
- Roche initially claimed an intermediate-length read capability but the expert hasn't "heard so much about that recently" and does not know if it is still on the roadmap.
- The expert did not grade Illumina's long-read response beyond this, nor discuss weaknesses specifically.

## Switching costs, validation and the regulatory moat

- **Assays approved vs LDTs.** The number of FDA-approved NGS assays is "quite low" and includes TSO500, Illumina's kit used on its IVD-capable machines. The number of laboratory-developed tests (LDTs, in-house tests) used by medium to large hospital labs in the US, Europe and elsewhere is "much larger". The expert attributes this to Illumina's historically "quite open access attitude" toward library prep up to the point of loading DNA onto the sequencer, which created a large ecosystem of assays. The expert said this is on the edge of their expertise and without actual data.
- **Cost of switching existing assays.** Verifying and validating against the previous standard "can be hugely expensive" and would apply to any diagnostic technology change. Changing existing assays "is not going to happen" in the expert's view.
- **New applications and disease areas** are where setting up a new workflow becomes viable.
- A large service laboratory could not contemplate switching without "an enormous financial imperative": an "extremely cheap machine, extremely cheap consumables".
- Large centers constantly evaluate new technologies, but "change seems to come around quite slowly."
- The client asked about switching for MRD; the expert's answer was general and not MRD-specific.

## Risks and disruptive technologies

- The expert says there is potential for another change, but more likely "a disruptive technology that just changes the nature of how we look at these things".
- Historical analogy in diagnostics: immunochemistry was the gold standard 15 years ago, then the qPCR era, and now NGS. "There is always going to be something that is going to be completely disruptive."
- Watch list (expert's opinion):
  - Nanopore applications "are the way forward".
  - Protein sequencing: "if you could do it, is probably the way", and would be disruptive, though "it might not be cheap".
- Proteomics landscape (expert, partly hearsay):
  - Jay Flatley (a former Illumina leader) and many former Illumina employees are "apparently" working on a proteomics startup.
  - Spin-outs from the University of Oxford, London, and (the expert thinks) Stuttgart show interest.
  - Thermo Fisher currently dominates proteomics via mass spectrometry, which needs large pure samples, with data that is hard to interpret, slow and highly skilled.
  - The client noted Illumina acquired SomaLogic and that Thermo has a related effort (the client named "LabLink", as transcribed).
- Vision: a doctor could look at a patient's proteome and identify an unusual blood protein linked to a cancer, which is "much more immediate" than a gene that "could or could not be switched on".
