# Channel check with a Children's Hospital Chief Genomics Officer (CLIA lab, 100% Illumina short-read, Oxford Nanopore long-read) on Q3 2026 sequencing demand, pricing and competition

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-15_Executive_at_Childrens_Hospital_Optimistic_About_Sequencing_Growth_and_Technology_Integrat.md` · Date: 2026-10-15 · Channel check platform (AI Interviewer); expert is a VP Chief Genomics Officer at a Children's Hospital in Colorado (customer); published 20 Oct 2026, interviewed 15 Oct 2026 · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer at a pediatric hospital genomics lab, which is CLIA-certified and runs 100% of clinical short-read work on Illumina NovaSeq X Plus, says sequencing volume grew about 13%-18% year over year in Q3 2026 and about 15% for full-year 2026, with more modest growth expected in 2027. Consumable spending fell about 10% because of the move from NovaSeq 6000 to the cheaper-per-unit NovaSeq X Plus reagents, even as volumes rose; per-unit prices were unchanged. The expert sees Roche AXELIOS one as a "wait and see" item (CLIA validation alone costs roughly $100,000-$200,000), views Oxford Nanopore as additive to Illumina, not a replacement, and stresses that bioinformatics and ecosystem, not instruments, drive loyalty. Illumina support improved in Q3 after a restructuring.

## Expert background and context

**Who the expert is.** The expert is a VP Chief Genomics Officer at a Children's Hospital in Colorado, with oversight of a genomics program that uses sequencers from Illumina and Oxford Nanopore (ONT, a maker of long-read sequencers). They are a current customer, so their experience is recent and first-hand [Expert: Chief Genomics Officer, children's hospital, Oct 2026]. The interview took place on 15 October 2026 and was published on 20 October 2026. It was conducted by an AI interviewer as a "channel check" (a structured survey of customers about demand, pricing and competition at a vendor).

**The lab.** The lab is part of a pediatric hospital. It serves inpatients, outpatients and the hospital's network of care partners. It is CLIA-certified (CLIA is the US federal certification that lets a lab report results used in patient care), so it operates as a clinical diagnostic lab, not a research-use-only lab.

**Data volume.** Taking all test offerings together, the lab produces roughly 500 TB of data per year.

**Direct experience versus speculation.** Most answers are first-hand descriptions of the lab's own volumes, spending and fleet. The Q3 and full-year 2026 growth figures are realized results. The 2027 outlook, the view of AXELIOS one's competitive threat and the comments on proteomics are the expert's opinions. The expert has not used AXELIOS one.

## Demand environment

**Tailwinds (expert's view).**
- Professional organizations are now recommending whole genome sequencing (reading a person's entire DNA) as a Tier-1 (first-line) diagnostic test.
- More precision medicine therapeutics require detailed genomic profiling of patients before they qualify for treatment.
- The hospital is a vibrant academic medical center with many industry-sponsored and physician-led clinical trials, all of which add volume.

**Headwinds.**
- Payers (insurers). The lab gets pre-authorization and has built "really robust pre-authorization workflows." However, payment and reimbursement "is lagging and is often very labor-intensive."
- Talent and operational complexity: hiring and growing the team and maintaining competency across "such a complex operation."

## Test mix and growth by application

- Residual disease monitoring is not performed, and prenatal screening is not performed. Prenatal diagnostics are done and are counted within rare disease.
- Between the two pillars the lab does, the split is about 70% rare disease and 30% oncology (tumor profiling). Other activities sit in the innovation lab (a separate non-clinical development unit), not the clinical lab.
- Over the next 12 months the expert expects rare disease to grow much faster than oncology, because population-driven rare disease as a category outpaces oncology.
- Oncology could grow through cancer predisposition (inherited cancer risk) testing, but the expert would count that under "screening," a new category the lab is looking to open.

## Fleet and short-read versus long-read

- Short-read is entirely Illumina. Long-read is entirely Oxford Nanopore.
- Clinically, 100% of output is short-read. Long-read remains in the innovation space, mainly because "all of the CPT codes" (the billing codes used for reimbursement) are well suited to short-read, as are the lab's clinical workflows.
- Expected evolution: long-read will enter as targeted clinical questions, where there is a clear indication and the answer "is very likely to be only resolved by long-read." Long term the expert expects a tiered strategy, with short-read in "pole position" and long-read as a follow-up test if short-read gives no resolution.
- Within short-read, the lab moved three years ago from low-throughput instruments to exclusively the high-throughput Illumina NovaSeq X Plus. It still also owns a NovaSeq 6000 (the older Illumina high-throughput model).
- No other short-read vendors are in the mix. Reason for exclusivity (expert's view): "Genomics has become commoditized to the point where the sequencing is no longer the battleground, but the support and the bioinformatics." Illumina "right now stands as the only vendor that can really provide a full pipeline of products," which makes it easier to adapt and customize.
- Vendor share did not change in Q3 2026 in either category.
- Next 12 months: the expert expects ONT to take more clinical sequencing space as new algorithms and decision trees are built, and new processes opened. Asked whether this comes at Illumina's expense, they said: "I definitely see it as additive."

## Utilization trends

The table summarizes the lab's sequencing output growth as reported by the expert.

| Period | Result | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Q3 2026 vs Q3 2025 | Grew 13%-18% depending on category; exceeded expectations | Driven by adoption in more service lines and clearer pre-authorization pathways |
| Full-year 2026 vs 2025 | About 15% | Largely realized already, not a prediction |
| 2027 vs 2026 | "More modest" increase than recent years (no figure given) | See below |

- Q3 trends did not change the expert's 2026 view: results "stayed within variance."
- Main driver of 2026 growth: adoption in service lines where genomics traditionally had little foothold. The expert again stressed that back-end reimbursement stays "very tedious and manual."
- 2027 reasoning: the expert says they have forecast modest growth "for the last two years, and we keep growing." The deceleration reflects partly conservatism (not wanting to ignore the outside environment's signals) and partly saturation. Service lines already using genomics are saturated, so the lab then "grow[s] as they grow their operations." Internal service lines have probably hit their growth rate, though a couple of service lines are still adopting. Drivers of 2027 growth are continued adoption and realized benefits within service lines and with outside partners.

## Competition: Roche AXELIOS one

- Roche's AXELIOS one (a new sequencer using a reusable sensor, launched as research-use-only) has not been adopted. The lab prefers to see how the market matures, calling it "a very new and novel approach" where "every approach has caveats, like we've seen with PacBio." The lab is still at the "in silico" (desk-based, paper) evaluation stage.
- Threat assessment (opinion): there will be "waves." The first wave is "the shiny new toy with the cool white papers," with the long implementation and true benefits coming later, so the competitive outcome is hard to predict. "Illumina is not a sitting target"; it moves in several directions, including an optical long-read offering and multi-omics (measuring several types of molecule). The expert does not expect "tick for tack" (one-for-one matching) but a contest over "who can occupy new grounds with a proven technology."
- Cost of clinical use: a typical CAP/CLIA validation (CAP is the College of American Pathologists accreditation) for a clinical lab, without a strong vendor partnership, costs about $100,000 to sometimes $200,000, separate from instrument cost. The expert also flagged integration with the lab information management system (LIMS), tertiary interpretation (the software that turns variants into clinical findings) and quality assurance outputs: "This is not a trivial lift."

## Illumina products and roadmap

- **NovaSeq X roadmap.** The interviewer noted Illumina's commitment to raise NovaSeq X flow cell output by up to 40% and speed up whole genome sequencing by about 30%. The expert said: "We are committed to the NovaSeq X platform. We have fully integrated those instruments into the ecosystem." The roadmap lets the lab "lean even more into" its predicted growth, "knowing that our costs will be able to be managed appropriately."
- **TruPath Genome** (launched February 2026 per the interviewer; removes library preparation from the whole genome workflow; about $395 per sample list price per the interviewer). Not adopted clinically. It is being used in the innovation lab to understand a clinical adoption pathway, especially for critical patients where acuity and timeline are essential.
- **TruPath versus ONT** (the expert intends to separate this question "in 2027"): TruPath offers minimal hands-on time and high-quality short-read with optical linking for long-range information. For ONT, getting confidence for a whole genome takes a timeline of 72 hours, much longer than Illumina, but ONT gives methylation (chemical marks on DNA that affect gene activity). For targeted questions ONT may be preferred; for unknown cases needing large unbiased data sets, "TruPath looks the winner." The interviewer summarized them as complementary at this stage.
- **SomaLogic** (acquired by Illumina, closing January 2026 per the interviewer; a protein measurement business). The expert sees it as a long-term strategy and "a very thoughtful and well-thought-out move" for an innovation tool. Proteomics is "at its nascency," and questions such as soluble versus membrane-anchored proteins, isoforms and resolution must be defined before clinical use. The lab expects to begin evaluating it in the innovation lab for fit for purpose.

## Instrument purchases and capital spending

- Plans: the lab sees acquiring another NovaSeq X, driven by volume and multi-omics needs. It is undecided whether this replaces (trade-in) or adds to the NovaSeq 6000. Any ONT platform upgrade depends on ongoing innovation lab evaluations.
- No instrument placement plans were accelerated, delayed or cancelled in Q3 2026.
- Timing: instruments are large purchases and "not continuous year in, year out." The current fleet is acquired, and the next incremental jump is likely late 2026 or early 2027.
- 2026 sequencing instrument capex is tracking lower than in 2025 (no figure given).
- Instrument discounts, incentives and placement terms were unchanged versus the prior quarter.

## Consumables (reagents) and pricing

- **Spending.** Consumable spending (the reagent kits needed for each run) in Q3 2026 decreased about 10% versus Q3 2025. The expert said the cause was neither volume nor price changes but the transition from NovaSeq 6000 to NovaSeq X Plus: "Volumes have risen, but the favorable price of the X reagents drove the decrease."
- **Unit pricing.** After the interviewer clarified the question (price per unit, excluding mix), the expert said prices for comparable consumables from both Illumina and ONT were "the same" in 2026 versus 2025. (An earlier answer, that pricing was "higher because our volumes have gone up," was a misreading of the question.)
- **Incentives.** Unchanged quarter on quarter: "Every quarter there is always an incentive. The incentives change, but the number of incentives is the same."
- **Inventory** is sufficient. **Lead times** for consumables were unchanged in Q3.

## Vendor support and service

- Satisfaction is "all very positive": the lab is in "a really stable place" with Illumina (its largest vendor) and in a "collaborative and supportive space" with ONT.
- Support levels increased in Q3 2026 versus Q2, specific to Illumina. The expert said Illumina restructured its support for access to different tiers, with better triaging up front and faster support.

## Emerging technologies and closing points

- The expert watches optical mapping (imaging long DNA molecules) and whether it has a near-term clinical place. They also watch niche technologies that address single targeted questions in unique ways, such as very low cost or flexible throughput. Because "rare is common" in a pediatric hospital, different approaches are sometimes needed. Lab automation is another major area of interest.
- Key message: "the biggest aspect I will always flag to any company is the ecosystem, the bioinformatics. The instruments alone is not enough to sway a customer to part ways."

## Takeaways, as stated by the expert (not editorialized)

- Illumina's position at this lab rests on ecosystem and support, not hardware alone.
- Volume growth is strong (about 15% in 2026) but expected to slow as service lines saturate.
- Lower consumable dollars at this account reflect NovaSeq X economics, not price cuts, since unit prices were unchanged.
- Competitors (ONT, Roche AXELIOS one) are seen as additive or as unproven so far, and clinical validation costs and integration burden slow switching.
