# Channel check with a customer-side synthetic biology leader at a small private biotech on sequencing demand, vendor mix (Illumina, Oxford Nanopore) and Illumina product roadmap (Q3 2026)

Interview 2026-10-02, published 2026-10-09. · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer at a small, mid-range, research-use-only biotech lab says its sequencing output rose at least 100% (likely more) year over year in Q3 2026 and for full-year 2026, driven by a 300-400% jump in philanthropic grant funding and far more customer projects than expected. All of its short-read sequencing is on Illumina (MiniSeq and MiSeq in-house; NextSeq and NovaSeq outsourced to CROs) and all long-read is on Oxford Nanopore via Plasmidsaurus. The expert sees no vendor switching, no instrument purchases, about 5% annual consumable price increases, great Illumina support, and expects 2027 output to be flat to up 10-15% in the base case, with upside tied to a company decision on headcount and space.

## Expert background and how to weigh the evidence

- **Who the expert is.** A synthetic biology leader at a privately held, commercial-stage biotechnology start-up. The perspective is that of a **customer** of sequencing vendors, not a former employee or competitor. The interviewer was an AI. [Expert: synthetic biology leader at a biotech start-up, interview 2026-10-02]
- **Lab scale.** The expert describes the lab as "mid-range", generating "thousands to tens of thousands" of gigabases (Gb, a unit of sequencing data volume) per year.
- **Regulatory setting.** The lab operates under research-use-only (RUO) conditions. It does no clinical sequencing and releases no information to patients.
- **Recency and directness.** Statements about the lab's own volumes, funding, vendors, spending and Illumina support are first-hand, covering Q3 2026 and the preceding quarters. Statements about AXELIOS, the market-share outlook, and the value of the SomaLogic deal are the expert's opinion. The expert had not used NovaSeq X, AXELIOS or TruPath, so reactions to those are speculative. The expert says past employers used PacBio for specific use cases, before Nanopore "had really come online".
- **Caution.** This is a single small customer whose growth is unusually high because it is an early-stage start-up. It should not be read as representative of Illumina's broader customer base. The expert itself ties the growth to start-up dynamics.

## The lab and what it sequences

- **In-house instruments.** Short-read instruments (machines that read short DNA fragments, here Illumina's) are the small MiniSeq (relied on "heavily") and a "legacy" set of MiSeq instruments. These are valued for small throughput and fast turnaround, and are used across both R&D and customer project modules.
- **Outsourced higher-throughput work.** Deeper sequencing on Illumina NextSeq and NovaSeq is done at CRO partners (contract research organizations, outside labs hired to do work). Typical uses are discovery RNA-seq and genetic profiling of cell lines the company develops. The lab usually supplies RNA or DNA and has the CRO do library preparation (turning samples into a form the sequencer can read). It has sometimes done library prep itself but found outsourcing "both time and cost-effective".
- **Single-cell.** The lab has an in-house 10x box (10x Genomics single-cell library instrument). Libraries get an initial check on in-house MiniSeq/MiSeq, then go to CRO partners for deeper sequencing.
- **Long-read.** The lab does substantial nanopore sequencing (long-read technology from Oxford Nanopore), primarily outsourced to Plasmidsaurus. Plasmidsaurus takes in DNA, does library prep and sequencing, and returns detailed reports. The expert says turnaround is overnight and cost is "$10, $15 per sample", making outsourcing "a no-brainer".
- **Purpose split.** About half of sequencing supports internal R&D (understanding the company's tools). The other half supports external customer projects, specifically understanding the genetic profile of cell lines generated for customers. The expert stresses this is "not sequencing-as-a-service".

## Funding and demand drivers

- **What drives output.** The expert names: (1) the number and scope of external customer projects, (2) funding and grants for internal R&D, and (3) as a minor factor, the cost of sequencing and volume discounts under the vendors' pricing structure. A bigger R&D budget tends to mean data generation on higher-throughput platforms rather than a small sample subset on a low-throughput sequencer or mostly nanopore.
- **Funding mix of sequencing.** The expert gave the following split.

| Funding source | Share of sequencing budget |
|---|---|
| External customer projects (paid by customers per scope of work) | about 50% |
| Federal grants (the kinds open to commercial entities, "not the majority of grants") | about 10-15% |
| Philanthropic grants (one primary philanthropic organization that co-developed and funded early and internal R&D) | about 35-40% |

  The expert adds that the different sequencing types and throughputs are "pretty evenly distributed" across these funding mechanisms.
- **Grant funding in Q3 2026 vs prior quarter.** Grant funding "increased quite a bit... maybe like 300%, 400%". The reason: earlier grants went well and the philanthropic funder, "very happy" with the work, put more money behind it to enable more scope. The expert called it "a doubling down based on positive readouts". This raised R&D work and the scope of external projects, and so sequencing.
- **External customer demand in Q3 2026.** Inbound customer engagements rose, as expected given the work put into the commercial pipeline, but "the amount of the increase was much larger than was anticipated". The number and scope of projects was "far greater than we anticipated", which directly raised sequencing scope. The expert described the external response as "extremely positive".

## Short-read vs long-read mix

- **By gigabases:** about 60-70% short-read and 30-40% long-read, because NovaSeq/NextSeq runs for deep profiling and RNA-seq are very data-rich.
- **By sample count:** flipped, about 60-70% of samples go through long-read (nanopore), mainly whole-plasmid sequencing, and 30-40% through short-read. The expert credits Plasmidsaurus's fast, cheap offering.
- **By cost:** flips back toward short-read. Each long-read sample costs about $10-15, whereas each deeply sequenced short-read sample is in the "hundreds of thousands of dollars range" per sample (as stated in the transcript; likely meaning the cost of deep runs is far higher per sample). The expert's point: the answer depends on "how you are slicing the pie".
- **By project type:** External customer projects use a consistent workflow in which nanopore plasmid sequencing is essential. Exploratory R&D projects lean more on short-read.
- **Next 12 months:** Expected to stay "pretty consistent". R&D projects run 9-18 months and customer projects 6-18 months, and the current mix serves that work. More headcount, space or capacity would raise total output but not necessarily the mix. A shift would need "a major difference in turnaround time or cost", or a change in the scientific questions needing more long-read. The expert prefers workflows that are "well-known, and we understand, and we trust".

- **Visibility.** Scoping-to-kickoff-to-execution takes three to six months, so the lab has a good sense of throughput one quarter ahead. Grants and customer scopes behind Q3 work were discussed "a number of months ago".
- **Drivers of growth.** More headcount, more customer traction and projects, more philanthropic R&D funding, and growing technical maturity of the platform and customer engagement. All work uses sequencing in some capacity.
- **Sentiment.** The expert is "super optimistic". Q2 and Q3 2026 specifically strengthened that view as funding traction, customer visibility and project starts materialized. The expert was not pessimistic at the start of the year.

### 2027 outlook

- **Capacity ceiling.** 2026 sequencing is "basically maxing out" the lab's headcount and space. The majority of throughput comes from longer-duration projects expected to run through 2027, so 2026 is "pretty representative" of 2027. The expert sees no scenario of decreasing capacity, since active projects must be completed.
- **Base case.** About the same as 2026, "maybe 10%+, 15%+" if the company takes some risk on active projects to add capacity.
- **Upside case.** If the company invests in space and headcount, sequencing growth would track the capacity increase. For example, headcount up 20-30% with associated space could add a further 30-50% of throughput. This would not start in Q1 2027; realistically Q2 or mid-year, and the full-year effect would be prorated.
- **Decision timing.** Expansion is a company-level decision requiring input from higher-level individuals. It "will become clearer over the next three to six months". The expert says interest and demand exist and expansion "is very possible", but visibility is limited.

## Vendor share and competition

### Short-read: 100% Illumina

- All short-read sequencing, internal and external, runs on Illumina. The expert cites familiarity and ease of use, trust in outputs, and Illumina being "the dominant player and only game in town for short-read sequencing for a really long time". To the expert's knowledge the CROs lean "pretty heavily" on Illumina, with some capacity on the newer short-read instruments (Roche, Element, Ultima were named by the interviewer).
- **Throughput mix.** The "vast majority" of in-house work is on lower-throughput instruments, for frequent runs, fast turnaround and control with no third-party middleman. High-throughput work is one-off experiments and end-stage, low-sample, high-depth work, so a smaller fraction. The expert expects this to hold for at least a year.

### Long-read: 100% Oxford Nanopore

- All long-read is Oxford Nanopore (ONT), mainly whole-plasmid sequencing via Plasmidsaurus. Past employers used PacBio for specific use cases, before nanopore matured. The expert says the lab is "happy with the current provider".
- **Triggers for switching to PacBio.** Only a project with a technical requirement ONT cannot meet. The expert also notes that if Plasmidsaurus changed technology the lab would likely follow, but "that isn't really on the table". Whole-plasmid sequencing has become "more ubiquitous" over recent years.
- **Short-read covers most needs** apart from whole-plasmid sequencing.

### Share changes

- No change in Q3 2026 or since the start: Illumina has always been 100% of short-read and ONT 100% of long-read.
- Next 12 months: no expected change. Only triggers would be Plasmidsaurus leaving nanopore, or CROs offering compelling platform switches that are easy to analyze and significantly cut cost or turnaround.

### AXELIOS (Roche) and the competitive threat

- Not adopted and not interested, since current instruments supply needed data. The lab would consider it only if its current modalities were no longer fit for purpose.
- The expert's view on threat: for many people Illumina is "the incumbent... what's comfortable. It's what's known." On the R&D side Illumina "continues to be pretty dominant". Without clear technical differentiation (new questions answerable that were not possible before), competitors "will continue to take some fraction of the market share, but I would be surprised if Illumina fell from the majority position."
- Caveat: unless there is a massive cost difference, "which arguably some of the new short-read sequencing players are able to compete on". The expert still sees no massive disruption and says "Illumina will always be around... At least that's my opinion."

## Illumina products and corporate moves

- **NovaSeq X.** Not adopted in-house, and the expert is unsure whether the CRO uses it. No plan to bring high-throughput instruments on-site given infrequent use. Told Illumina committed to raise NovaSeq X flow cell output by up to 40% and increase whole-genome sequencing speed by about 30% (interviewer's statement), the expert said "Definitely. That sounds awesome" and would ask the CRO whether it has and can use NovaSeq X. In-house ownership stays uneconomic due to frequency of use.
- **TruPath Genome.** The interviewer said Illumina launched it in February 2026, removing library prep from the whole-genome workflow at a list price of about $395 per sample. The expert: "it sounds good" and would consider it "when the technical need arises". On whether it could replace some long-read work, "I don't know... Would need to consider more deeply"; any workflow change needs a clear reason, and lower turnaround and cost are positive factors.
- **SomaLogic.** The interviewer said Illumina closed the acquisition in January 2026. It "did not move the needle at all" for the lab's usage, which was already heavily Illumina. The expert still called the deal "smart": it lets Illumina sequencing address protein-level questions in addition to DNA and RNA, which "wasn't previously possible on the Illumina platform or on any short-read sequencer". The expert could see it bringing in customers with protein backgrounds, raising R&D throughput, and increasing revenue and customer count with a new reagent and kit type. The lab has no technical need now but is aware of the catalog and would consider it.

## Instruments, capital spending and incentives

- No plans to acquire, replace or upgrade sequencers in the next 12 months. Higher-throughput work will continue at CROs, and current capacity is expected to meet needs.
- No placement plans were accelerated, delayed or canceled in Q3 2026.
- Capital spending on sequencers is flat, effectively zero, in both 2025 and 2026 (instruments were bought "a few years ago") and likely zero in 2027.
- Instrument discounts and incentives in Q3 2026 were about the same as the prior quarter, and are a non-factor: the lab follows its requirements, and it absorbed higher sequencing volume with existing instruments.

## Consumables, pricing, inventory and lead times

- **Consumable spend (reagent kits needed for each run).** Q3 2026 spend was up "about maybe 30%-40%" vs Q3 2025, "mostly volume", reflecting more in-house sequencing. There was no stocking or destocking, only ordering as needed (just-in-time).
- **Price.** Comparable consumables rise "about 5%" every year, which the expert calls "pretty standard". It caused no concern or change in buying.
- **Vendor discounts or commitment terms** in Q3 2026 were about the same as the prior quarter; vendors "work with us pretty closely".
- **Inventory** is "sufficient". **Lead times** were about the same as the prior quarter.

## Vendor support

- "I think Illumina is great in terms of support. No issues." Support levels in Q3 2026 were "the same" as the prior quarter.

## Key risks and caveats noted by the expert

- Growth beyond 2026 is capped by headcount and space unless management approves expansion, a decision not yet made.
- Start-up dynamics mean small fluctuations in work streams up or down.
- Long-term competitive risk to Illumina would come mainly from cost differences in newer short-read players; technical differentiation is the other route, and none is yet seen by the expert.
- Reactions to NovaSeq X, TruPath and AXELIOS are not based on direct use.
