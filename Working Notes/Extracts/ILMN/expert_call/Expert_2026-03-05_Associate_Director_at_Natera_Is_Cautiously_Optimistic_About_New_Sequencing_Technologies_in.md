# Natera Oncology Lifecycle Director on AGBT 2026: NovaSeq X for MRD, Roche's SBX/AXELIOS, Element, and Switching Barriers

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-03-05_Associate_Director_at_Natera_Is_Cautiously_Optimistic_About_New_Sequencing_Technologies_in.md` · Date: 2026-03-05 · Expert-network transcript (buy-side investor-led call; published 2026-06-03, interviewed 2026-03-05); expert is an Associate Director, Oncology Lifecycle Management at Natera · expert call*

**Summary.** A current Natera oncology executive, who helps decide on onboarding Illumina machines, gives a customer's reaction to AGBT 2026 (Illumina's annual sequencing conference). They are impressed by NovaSeq X's ultra-low error rate (Q70) and "MRD at scale" claims, see Roche's cheap SBX/AXELIOS sequencer as best suited to new labs, and say large established labs like Natera rarely switch from Illumina. They stress that all the data shown is analytical, not clinical, validation.

## Expert background and how to weigh the views

- **Who:** Associate Director, Oncology Lifecycle Management at Natera. Natera is a large diagnostics company that sells blood-based cancer tests (ctDNA, circulating tumor DNA, meaning tumor DNA fragments floating in the blood). The expert runs the strategic direction, profit and loss (P&L) and commercial success of Natera's oncology portfolio.
- **Relationship to Illumina:** Customer. The expert is responsible for managing numerous Illumina machines and has taken part in evaluating and deciding on expanding and onboarding such machines.
- **Career:** Senior Manager, Oncology Lifecycle Management (left that role August 2025, i.e. promoted); before that Senior Scientist, Translational Medicine (left March 2025). Experience is therefore current. The expert can speak to product lifecycle management, translational medicine, diagnostic commercialization and biomarker development.
- **Format:** Consultant perspective, interviewed by a buy-side (investor) client on 2026-03-05, right after AGBT (the Advances in Genome Biology and Technology conference). Published 2026-06-03.
- **Direct experience vs. speculation:** Direct experience covers Natera's lab scale, the difficulty of changing sequencing providers, and past dealings with Roche's sales incentives. Views on AGBT presentations are from attending and reading. Views on Roche's timing and pricing, Element and the future market are partly recalled or speculative, and the expert flags this ("don't quote me on that").

## Biggest AGBT takeaways

- The expert named two highlights [Expert: Natera associate director, Mar 2026]: Illumina's NovaSeq X technology (its high-throughput sequencer), which has broad use in ctDNA and MRD detection (minimal residual disease, i.e. finding tiny amounts of leftover cancer after treatment), and how Roche positioned its AXELIOS sequencer (built on SBX technology) against Illumina, both technically and commercially.
- Digital pathology (AI-driven analysis of tissue slides) also drew the expert's personal interest, though outside the call's scope (see the end).

## Illumina NovaSeq X and MRD

### What Illumina presented
- Illumina positioned the product around quality, limit of detection (the smallest amount of tumor DNA reliably detectable) and accuracy, which the expert called "the buzzwords that you would like to hear about MRD."
- A slide was titled "MRD at scale." It used Illumina's dual-strand technology and claimed detection down to a very low level, below 5 ppm (parts per million); the expert recalled 2.5 ppm.
- Illumina claimed nearly 100% specificity (the expert said 99.9% with 95%, apparently a garbled figure), compared against older technology, and said everything was detectable even at very low sequencing depth.
- The expert said this is highly applicable because the MRD field is moving to whole genome sequencing, higher-plex tests (more targets per assay), structural variants and phased variants, plus methylation, all of which need extremely low parts-per-million limits of detection.

### Q70 read quality
- Q70 is a quality score meaning roughly one error in 10 million bases; the expert contrasted Q30 at about one in 1,000. The expert linked Q70 directly to the 2.5 ppm claim.
- The expert's opinion: "if you don't have ultra-sensitive, below five ppm, you're going to be out of the market automatically" in ctDNA testing, but not now; "in a couple of years." It will not be a "27 project, most likely not, but 2030 and beyond." Within five years it becomes "standard of care." Customers driving this are physicians and centers, and the academic community, rather than patients.

### Timing of availability (expert estimate)
- Commercial use: realistically 2027 (companies were trying to push genome by the end of 2026).
- Clinical trials: ongoing trials cannot change assays midway, so new studies on this technology likely start in 2028. Commercially and for new studies, wide availability is "a year and a half from now."

### Caveat: analytical vs. clinical validity
- The expert said the field is a "rat race" in which every company claims analytical validation. Vendors diluted cell-line samples to very low levels and still detected cell-free DNA molecules, but "nobody knows how this is going to perform in a real clinical sample."
- "This is analytical validity, not clinical validity." The field has "created like a loop" of chasing low ppm, implying clinical benefit that nobody has shown.

### Larger flow cells and throughput
- The expert sees "a lot of value" for whole genome and higher-plex assays, which is where precision oncology is growing.
- High throughput lets one machine run both cfDNA (cell-free DNA) and methylation work, which many machines cannot. Natera has a tissue-informed test (needing whole exome or whole genome) and a methylation assay; today they are run separately, and this would let them be operationalized together, even from the same patient, cutting cost and time at scale.
- The expert wants to see it in action first. ctDNA companies (Natera, Guardant, Personalis, Exact) hold a dedicated conference every three months and will discuss it; the expert will "wait and see."

### Flexibility and batching
- Illumina's workflow flexibility and batching is "a welcomed initiative… only benefits. There's no downside," but the expert is conservative on commercial operations.
- Natera tests two to three million samples per year and automates heavily, so anything new risks disruption. The expert wants real-life detail on what "sequencing efficiency across workflows" means and wants someone "brave enough" to share commercial-volume reactions: "great punchlines" that need real-world proof.

## Roche AXELIOS (SBX)

- **Technology view:** "Less sophisticated than NovaSeq X." Roche made no big low-detection claims, but the technology should suit whole genome MRD and cell-free DNA via phased variants (groups of mutations on the same DNA fragment), which would imply a good limit of detection automatically.
- **Price:** Roche is aiming at $150-$250 per genome (the client said $150-$200). The expert thought this could let Roche capture a lot of market with a "quick and dirty and precise" and reliable cheap option.
- **Client pushback:** the client noted large customers already get about that price from NovaSeq X. The expert conceded: NovaSeq X is about $250-$300 per genome from what they had seen.
- **Expert's preference:** "consistently, I would select Illumina because of the infrastructure and because you know the reagents, you know the people. It's very difficult to change your sequencing provider."
- **Who would adopt:** new players (start-ups, small academic centers, regional precision labs, new labs built from scratch), not established ones. Big labs with commercial infrastructure should stay with Illumina and perhaps add NovaSeq X machines. Roche is aggressive with incentives to switch providers (expert's past experience), so the expert expects incentives here.
- **Large players:** switching on the strength of analytical data alone is "a very high risk" when a company has commercial infrastructure and FDA approvals in progress. A small pilot is plausible, then waiting to see what competitors and small labs publish. "Illumina makes it very, very difficult for you to change. That's actually good because you want stability."
- **Flow cell reuse:** Roche's pricing reportedly requires reusing flow cells about 20 times. The expert could not find more information. Reuse was "a major no" in Natera's past R&D and commercial setup, while acceptable for biobanks or retrospective work. The expert cited published papers saying accuracy drops by a couple of values for about every hundred reuses. Contamination means background DNA, which masks real signals and hurts bioinformatics, so contamination translates into lower accuracy and sensitivity. The expert is "not fully convinced."
- **Timing and price (recalled):** Roche's website says "contact us." The expert expects research-use-only (RUO) availability by end of summer 2026 (possibly August) and broader or commercial use around January-February 2027. A list price of $150,000 was recalled with a "don't quote me on that."
- **Natera's purchase intent:** not soon. Natera is scaling labs for "tons of commercial volume" and does not see Roche as a major target within one or two quarters, though it wants to give all technologies a chance eventually.

### Can Roche catch Illumina?
- "Roche will catch up at some point," though not at the same scale. The expert wants to see Roche's answer on ultra-low-level MRD sensitivity: it will decide whether Roche catches up or "play catch up all the time."
- Other deciding factors: technical response, marketing, and partners (for example securing a big academic institution "like a Broad Institute-type" partner).

## Element Biosciences

- "The technology is great. It's a step towards the right direction," and what the expert expected from them.
- The expert wants data on ultra-low ppm detection and accuracy like what Illumina showed for NovaSeq X. For a hypothetical new lab in 2029 buying 50 machines, "a couple of moving parts" would still need to be seen.
- Timing of more data is uncertain. AACR (about a month and a half away) is a chance, and the expert expects vendors to use AACR, ASCO and ESMO this year, since everyone asks about ultrasensitive MRD limits of detection. The expert may talk to a contact there.

## Digital pathology (outside main scope)

- AGBT showed AI algorithms that analyze a slide and high-plex chemistry that profiles the tumor microenvironment in real time; the expert called this "where the future of pathology is," valuable for immunotherapy and targeted therapy.
- Tools using AI and lasers can find the most cell-rich slide region to cut for ctDNA and MRD tissue testing, reducing tissue failures that otherwise ruin sequencing.
