# Yale Genome Center Executive Director Shrikant Mane on 2026 NIH-funding headwinds, Roche Axelios, 10x Atera spatial platform, Illumina long reads and proteomics

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-04-29_KOL_Illumina_10x_Genomics_and_Roche_Executive_Director_of_the_Yale_Genome_Center_Shrikant_Mane.md` · Date: 2026-04-29 · Guidepoint expert call (moderator Andrew Carmen); expert Shrikant Mane, Executive Director of the Yale Genome Center and Professor of Genetics, Yale School of Medicine (academic KOL, customer viewpoint) · expert call*

**Summary.** A large academic genome-core director says sequencing demand at his facility is falling (about 10% over the first 9 months of the period he discusses, led by DNA work) because NIH grant money is not being released, and that core labs he contacted are in the same position. He says Roche's Axelios is not a threat to Illumina's NovaSeq X (Illumina is already about $150 per genome and Axelios has unresolved library-prep, pipeline and error-rate issues), is enthusiastic about 10x's new Atera spatial platform but will not buy it soon, and is impressed by Illumina's new synthetic long-read data. He expects funding to stabilize only after roughly 3 to 4 years.

# Yale Genome Center expert call, 29 April 2026 (Guidepoint)

## Expert background and basis of the views

- **Who:** Shrikant Mane, Executive Director of the Yale Genome Center and Professor of Genetics at Yale School of Medicine. The moderator was Andrew Carmen of Guidepoint, the expert-network firm that arranged the call. Mane said his background was unchanged since their previous conversation, which Carmen said was "about a year" earlier. The document labels him an "Academic KOL - Physician Scientist" (KOL means key opinion leader, an outside expert whose views carry weight in the field).
- **Relationship to the companies:** He is a **customer and user**, not a former employee. He runs a core facility (a shared university lab that sequences samples for researchers on a fee-for-service basis). He has a direct, hands-on view of NovaSeq X (Illumina's high-throughput sequencer), PacBio Revio (long-read sequencer), an Element sequencer, and 10x Genomics Xenium (spatial platform). He also has CosMx (a spatial platform now owned by Bruker), NextSeq and MiSeq (smaller Illumina machines), and he oversees the mass spectrometry (protein-measuring instruments) service.
- **Recency:** Current. He is describing his own facility's activity over roughly the past 6 to 12 months, plus conversations that week (an advisory committee meeting "yesterday", i.e. 28 April 2026, and a meeting between 10x and a colleague, Parag, the same day).
- **Mix of direct experience and speculation:** Facility volumes, staffing, pricing, instrument counts and utilization are direct experience. Comments on competitors' strategy (Bruker's plans, Roche's customer base, Ultima's chemistry, Illumina's proteomics timeline) are his inferences. He often reports hearsay from colleagues and vendors, which is flagged below.
- **Compliance note:** The moderator asked him to avoid commentary on stocks, valuation and investment recommendations. The call is confidential. The attached summary lists primary companies TXG, ILMN, ROG:SW and PACB, and associated Element Biosciences, Lucid Genomics, ONT, Ultima Genomics, OMIC and Deerfield Management.

## Headline view [Expert: Yale Genome Center director, Apr 2026]

Mane said that across sequencing, spatial and single-cell work, activity is "either going down or status quo," and that sequencing is "definitely going down." He attributes this almost entirely to NIH (National Institutes of Health, the main US government biomedical research funder) funding disruption, not to competition or loss of customers. New products from Illumina and 10x have generated enthusiasm, but "that's not reflecting into increased usage."

## Sequencing demand at his facility

- **Size of the decline:** When asked for a percentage, he first said "not a lot, about 5%." He then said that, based on the first 9 months (of the relevant period, as presented to his advisory committee), demand is "in fact decreasing about 10%." The call summary table also records "decreased by approximately 10%."
- **Historical comparison:** In a normal year, demand grew 10% to 15%, and a new platform could lift it by about 20%. From 2024 onward the trend was steady, and now it is declining. Later he said that since 2009 his data output, sample numbers and user counts rose every year, an "onward trend till 2024."
- **No quarterly read:** He declined to give a Q1 2026 versus Q4 2025 comparison, saying genome-center volumes do not move by quarter, because months fluctuate with large projects.
- **DNA down, RNA slightly up:** In the data he presented to his advisory committee (provost, dean, notable faculty users and technology-development people), only DNA volume fell. DNA applications such as genomes, exomes and methylation (a chemical mark on DNA read as a gene-regulation signal) are down. RNA applications "have actually slightly increased." He called this "shocking" to him. The RNA increase is on the Illumina platform, in his account of general usage.
- **Illumina spend:** His budget has fallen for two reasons: lower volume, and the fact that Illumina now gives him discounts, which it did not earlier. His Illumina spend "used to be $6-7 million" and "has gone down to $3-4 million."
- **Platform split:** About 99% of sequencing runs on Illumina, because of cost. Element is rarely used.
- **Counting applications:** About 30% to 40% of his NovaSeq X work is "counting" applications (measuring how many fragments are present, where base-level accuracy matters less). Even in those, he says, many people look for variants in RNA data, so accuracy still matters.

## Why demand is down: NIH funding and budgets

- **Dependence on NIH:** More than 90% of his work is funded from NIH budgets. Some investigators have private funding.
- **Money is appropriated but not released.** Congress approved the budget in February 2026 and the NIH budget was in fact increased, so "NIH has the money." But NIH is still not releasing it. His own grant, described as 99.9% funded after being told "6 months ago" to submit paperwork, had been waiting about 5 to 6 months with no payment and no date.
- **His reasons for the delay (speculation):** NIH lacks staff, there were two government shutdowns, and the new NIH director is asking program officers for many things.
- **Behavior:** Uncertainty makes researchers hold on to money. He gave an example: with $200,000 left, he would be "very, very frugal."
- **Multi-year grants:** NIH changed to funding grants multiple years up front. His university normally receives about $1 billion a year from NIH and received $1.3 billion this year, yet is "in trouble" because the money went to only 25% of people, while 75% got nothing. He said NIH is funding only about one-third of new grants. People with scores that used to guarantee funding (10% to 12%) are not being funded, and 5% to 6% scores are not getting grants. He called it "growing pain" and noted NSF (the National Science Foundation) does the same.
- **University backstop is limited:** The university steps in when you have no money, but "they don't step up for a long time."
- **Other pressures on Yale:** The university raised salaries for postdocs and junior scientists, hitting lab budgets. Yale has laid off many people. He said the Trump administration is taxing Yale's endowment, costing it $400 million a year, and that core facilities used to receive endowment money. Indirect-cost overheads have been "untouched," which he said would otherwise have been "a disaster."
- **Fewer trainees:** Major universities, including Yale, did not accept registrations last year (not announced publicly), because training grants are absent. He assumes fewer people means less research.
- **Other funders:** The VA (Veterans Affairs) budget has also been significantly cut.
- **Breadth:** He contacted at least 20 core labs, and "they are all in the same situation." He says "everywhere is the same."
- **Project mix:** He does not know how NIH priorities are shifting. He believes funded work is a mix of immunology, genomics and proteomics, and does not expect a shift away from animal-model work. He described the NIH director as "very sensible" but bound to "listen to his boss."

## Facility operations and staffing

- **Staff:** The facility has 38 people. Over the last 6 months he did not fill positions when staff left, which the summary records as 3 staff members laid off. If his proteomics grant arrives on time, he expects to maintain staff; otherwise "I might have to reduce 1 more staff."
- **Instruments:** About a year before the call he bought a second Illumina sequencer (NovaSeq X), when the facility had one machine and a backlog of "almost 4 months." That was "even before Trump was elected" in his words, and demand had historically grown. After the postdoc salary rise and funding cuts, "even though we have 2 machines, we are technically using only 1." He still pays service contracts on both.
- **Utilization quote:** He said the machines are "being utilized fully 90-95%," but that sentence was made in the context of the spatial machines (the transcript is ambiguous here).
- **Competitiveness:** His fees are 14%, 15% and up to 30% cheaper than any other core or commercial lab he surveyed (a legally required survey). He is cheaper than Novogene (a Chinese sequencing service company), whose discounted prices are still higher. Non-internal (outside) users pay 30% extra. He therefore does not think users are sending samples elsewhere. His read is that "money is not there."
- **His finances:** He received $10 million 15 years ago when he started the center and still has "$9 million in the bank," so money is not a constraint for him personally. His caution is about demand.

## 10x Genomics and the Atera spatial platform

### Current 10x business
- 10x is "doing pretty well, even though their business is not increasing," thanks to new Flex assays (a single-cell gene-expression product) and a new spatial machine. 10x staff are on campus "every day."
- **Spend trends:** Spatial spend is steady. Single-cell is "going down like 5%, 10%." Spatial is mainly on Xenium. He also owns CosMx, which is maintaining but not increasing.

### Atera
- **Timeline:** 10x shared it with him almost a year before the call and under a confidentiality agreement through AGBT (a genomics conference) until April, when it became public. General release is expected later in 2026.
- **Price:** About $500,000 for the machine (moderator's figure, which Mane accepted); he also referred to investing "$600,000, $500,000." He expects at least a $50,000 discount.
- **Advantages (his view):** Higher throughput: 6,000 targets in about 2 days versus about 3 weeks on Xenium or CosMx. Better quality. Cheaper to run than Xenium. Multiplexing: Xenium is limited (about 500 targets) versus 1,000 to 1,500 "easily." A large usable area of about 5 square centimeters, which he agreed matters for targeted work, would allow several samples per slide. He said "5 samples, it's 5 times less cost." Technology is transferable from Xenium.
- **Limitation:** Whole-transcriptome runs lose sensitivity because the technology is imaging based. He expects people to run 5,000 to 8,000 targets, or run the whole transcriptome for discovery and then focus.
- **Why he is not buying now:** The funding environment, plus a need to confirm users will use it, because his two spatial machines are full. "The timing is wrong." Mane said "I would like to buy it" and that he will have to eventually because "this is older technology."
- **Signal on demand (inference):** 10x came to see him twice, which he reads as meaning "not many people are buying it."
- **Plan:** Pilot it, understand data quality and limitations ("the sales guy don't tell you everything"), then email investigators with pros and cons, and ask if they want whole-transcriptome work. He said "for the next 4 years, I'm not buying Atera," and would spend that time evaluating and talking to customers. He added that early platforms bring many issues, and that a 50% price discount would make an early purchase sensible.
- **Competition in spatial:** He says there is no competition for his facility in spatial, as few facilities have these tools. He already has three platforms. He worries about Bruker (owner of CosMx): its people do not visit, and he did not see a response to Atera at AGBT. He was always impressed by Resolve (spatial platform) but said Bruker, which acquired it, is silent, so it is either shelved or secret. He wants to know Bruker's plan before spending $500,000 to $600,000.
- **Others:** Cellanome and Chronomics "are nowhere in the picture yet." He questioned the Seranom platform (started by Jay Flatley; Sean Levy of Element joined) for single cells: machine cost about $700,000 to $800,000, expensive to operate, and he sees no demand. Akoya is "stuck with 100 antibodies" because of cross-reactivity.

### Element AVITI24 conversion
He got a grant for an Element sequencer, so he pays only the service contract. He can convert it to spatial AVITI24 for about $140,000 and planned to email users the following Monday. He said AVITI24 does live cells, cell suspension and monolayer, cytoprofiling and in-situ sequencing (RNA, DNA and protein on the same slide), and matters for functional screens such as CRISPR.

## Roche Axelios and competition for NovaSeq X

Mane said Axelios is not a threat to NovaSeq X ("Nope").

- **Price:** Illumina is already at $150 a genome (the price given to his lab). He would have been "a little bit interested" if Roche kept $100.
- **Technical issues (his view):** It is a brand-new platform with problems in library prep (the step preparing DNA for sequencing), analytical pipelines and error rates.
- **Turnaround argument:** Roche's selling point is 4 hours. He says that does not matter, because a cancer specialist appointment takes 3 to 4 months, so "4 hours versus 2 days is not going to matter at all." Data quality and cost should come first. He is disappointed by Roche's hype, and Roche lacks good tech support in his region.
- **Accuracy:** He is "not convinced you don't need accuracy." Simplex (single-pass) and duplex (higher-accuracy) modes differ in price. Clinical diagnostics need high accuracy, "which they don't have."
- **Who buys:** The only user he knows is Regeneron, whose facility head formerly worked for him and said they bought it for counting applications. He guesses it came at $100 a genome. Roche's Axelios product manager formerly managed NovaSeq X+ at Illumina.
- **Illumina strength:** Robust tech support. He has no incentive to move because he has spare capacity.
- **Ultima:** Already at $100 a genome with its pipelines sorted, but its chemistry is "basically flawed." If buying for counting, he would pick Axelios over Ultima because Axelios keeps other options. He questioned why one would buy Vega (Ultima's smaller instrument, per the moderator; "the same chemistry"). Ultima's CEO now says he will not push a purchase because Mane does not need it.
- **Element AVITI:** His preferred alternative: low cost, about $100 a genome, quality "nobody doubts," takes Illumina libraries. The problem is lower throughput: he would need 3 or 4 machines to match his output. He is in no hurry, as he wants to recover NovaSeq depreciation for 2 to 3 years, and AVITI is "not available now," giving him a year to a year and a half. AVITI data is "as good or better than Illumina"; people wait a week on Illumina rather than pay more for AVITI, which shows money worries. Late in the call he said he would pick Element over Axelios for counting.
- **Collaborations:** The PacBio/Ultima tie-up with Lucid Genomics has "no value"; it is "a marketing issue." Purchases depend on reliability, quality and cost.

## Long reads: PacBio, Illumina TruePath

- **Revio use:** One Revio, used at about 30% of capacity, bought because the funds would otherwise have been lost. Users are happy and it has led to many publications. There is "not much demand for long reads," and they are always more expensive. "Short reads are actually answering 90% of the questions."
- **PacBio presence:** PacBio no longer visits him.
- **Illumina TruePath (synthetic long reads, a method that infers long-range information from short reads):** Data seen at AGBT and in one-to-one meetings is "exceptionally good," as good as or possibly better than PacBio. Illumina sequenced his samples; he did not run it. Cost: PacBio about $2,000 a genome (his figure) versus about $500 for this. The protocol is not yet released; he hopes for beta access and wants to compare Illumina, PacBio and Oxford Nanopore on the same sample. Earlier Illumina long-read protocols "sucked."
- **PacBio's remaining edge:** Full-length transcript sequencing, not new genomes or phasing. If Illumina develops that application, "then PacBio is interesting" (speaking as a challenge to PacBio).

## Proteomics and multiomics

- Mane sees growing interest in **proteogenomics** (using genomic readouts to measure proteins), especially Olink, over mass spec, which he says is insensitive, unspecific, and needs highly specialized staff. Olink uses dual antibodies with DNA barcodes that must hybridize to call a protein, which raises specificity; DNA amplification adds sensitivity; it is cheaper and automatable. He just received a grant for Olink.
- He would have liked SomaLogic but found the company horrible to deal with. Illumina took it over after his grant award. He thinks both technologies raise Illumina sequencing utilization.
- **Illumina's proteomics entry:** "Really late." He expects a couple of years to bring the SomaLogic product fully to market, citing PIPseq (acquired about 1.5 years earlier), which is offered but "not good enough." He does not think Illumina will slow it, because they are "very methodical" and do not release unready products.
- **Illumina praise:** "Illumina is single-handedly responsible for reducing the sequencing cost." He cited whole genome costs of about $10,000 on early GA machines versus about $200 now, with new machines roughly every 4 years.

## Outlook

- He does not expect improvement this year: "it hasn't happened in 10 months," with little hope for the last 2 months.
- He expects stabilization of funding in roughly 3 to 4 years ("3 years later, I think things will be okay"), and closed with: "I'm hopeful, I think after 2 years things will change."
