# Expert call with PacBio's Head of Corporate Development on growing competition facing Illumina in the evolving NGS market

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-24_Head_of_Corporate_Development_at_Pacific_Biosciences_Believes_Illumina_Faces_Growing_Compe.md` · Date: 2026-07-24 · Expert-network transcript (publication dated 2026-07-28), interview with a competitor-side expert (Head of Corporate Development at Pacific Biosciences), conducted by a buy-side (investor-led) client; names not given · expert call*

**Summary.** A PacBio corporate development executive, speaking from about 10 years in next-generation sequencing (NGS), argues that Illumina's leadership is being challenged from several angles at once: low-cost rivals (Ultima), clinical-speed rivals (Roche SBX), long-read and multi-omic rivals (ONT, PacBio, 10x), and national champions in China. Illumina remains the safe, scaled, lower-cost default for targeted, cost-driven and clinical work, and the expert expects switching to be slow (a couple of years at least in clinical). The main exposure is in AI-native, multi-omic, research and development (R&D) work, where Illumina's closed, modular, capital-heavy architecture is a disadvantage.

## Expert background and how to weigh the views

- **Who the expert is.** The expert is the Head of Corporate Development at Pacific Biosciences of California (PacBio), a long-read sequencing company and an Illumina competitor. They are also GM/Executive Sponsor and Head of Corporate Development for Biopharmaceuticals and AI at PacBio, reporting to the C-suite. They lead business development partnerships and work on transactions across tools, data partnerships and research. [Expert: Head of Corporate Development, PacBio, Jul 2026]
- **Earlier role.** Before the current job (the bio mentions Tinker Therapeutics), the expert was Chief Financial Officer at Galatea Bio Inc., leaving June 2025. There they led finance, fundraising and strategic corporate development for a clinical genomics and data-platform-focused biopharma. [Expert bio]
- **Stated expertise.** Corporate and business development for biopharma and AI, venture investing and due diligence, strategic transactions and capital formation, molecular diagnostics and liquid biopsy (blood-based cancer tests) using NGS, instruments and multi-omics platforms, cell and genetic/RNA therapies, and animal health. [Expert bio]
- **Self-described experience.** "About 10 years in the NGS space," across diagnostic applications and biopharma, "primarily as an operator, but also in corporate development" for startups and for public Illumina competitors. [Expert, 2026-07-24]
- **Perspective and recency.** The expert is a competitor-side voice, so their views may lean toward alternatives to Illumina. Experience is current (they hold a PacBio role in July 2026). The interview was 2026-07-24 and was published 2026-07-28. The interviewer is a buy-side investor.
- **Direct experience versus speculation.** The expert is explicit about limits. They cannot say how Ultima's deals actually went ("I can't speak so much as to their actual ability to service those things"). ## Overall view: Illumina is threatened from many angles

- The expert says Illumina's long-held leadership in NGS (reading DNA at scale) faces "increasing competitive threat." The cause is an evolution in the market driven by AI and new technologies for data processing and compute. [Expert, Jul 2026]
- The threat comes from several places:
  - Hardware (instruments).
  - Illumina's multi-omics strategy (measuring DNA plus RNA, proteins and other molecules together).
  - Illumina's end-to-end software offerings (primary, secondary and tertiary analysis, meaning raw-signal processing, alignment and variant calling, and interpretation).
  - Illumina's cost and margin profile.
- Over roughly the past 10 years, the cost of sequencing has compressed a lot. Some of that has been read as commoditization (products becoming interchangeable) and greater accessibility. The expert says there is "almost like a floor" to this trend. As it continues, business models shift and new applications emerge, so there is "both threat and opportunity."

## Where Illumina is most and least vulnerable (by customer type)

### Most vulnerable
- The expert says the most exposed customers are on both the research and diagnostic sides, and are:
  - Developing applications based on multi-omic technologies.
  - Running computationally intensive pipelines that need more raw access to data.
- The expert's reason is that Illumina "has chosen a more integrated closed software architecture," and its tools, reagent kits (the consumables bought for each run) and products are "highly modularized," with wide breadth, particularly in short-read sequencing (reading short DNA fragments).
- What the expert has seen is a shift toward multi-omic and "AI-native" applications. Examples given are model training, fine-tuning and inference, agentic workflows and retrieval-based systems that need access to more raw information.
- Illumina faces "a lot of threat" in these cases:
  - Building and training a foundation model.
  - Designing and validating a multi-omic diagnostic assay.
  - Wanting higher-quality data and richer context.
- The expert says they hear clinical-side people talking about other technologies, and pharma R&D people looking at technologies with higher resolution, single-cell, single-molecule focus, higher quality, and "multi-omic-native, AI-native, open-model, open-access" infrastructure. Illumina "didn't design their business model for that, or historically hasn't." The expert says they do not know the exact pace of migration, though "there's a big wave obviously happening in the AI world."

### Least vulnerable
- Illumina is strongest for "very focused, targeted, cost-oriented applications" where it has scale few life-science tools companies have, and for customers who want an off-the-shelf, robust, "safer" solution and infrastructure.
- In those cases Illumina has the scale, infrastructure and wider menu, so "it's hard to make a case for a different company, because Illumina is the safer, likely lower cost option."
- For emerging applications, novel development and R&D, the expert says customers across channels are more likely to look at technologies built for those uses.

## Illumina's diversification into multi-omics and its constraints

- The interviewer asked whether Illumina came late to multi-omics (it acquired SomaLogic, a protein measurement company, and Fluent, single-cell technology) and whether it should have stuck to sequencing platforms.
- The expert says the move is "generally in the right direction." Illumina has been "fairly proactive and a first mover in many ways as far as large institutions go." [Expert, Jul 2026]
- The downside, per the expert:
  - Illumina invested significant capital in these bets and "doesn't have the agility or the versatility to adapt quickly to market conditions."
  - The tech stack behind DRAGEN secondary analysis (Illumina's genomic analysis software), the interconnected analytics and bio-insight platform, the instrument fleet design, the kits, and the omics capabilities added through acquisition have "locked up a lot of resources" and created longer-term fixed commitments.
  - Competitors can adapt faster because they have not committed as much capital.
  - It is hard for a large organization to run transformative efforts in a rapidly shifting landscape. Illumina carries "technical burden," with investments that "may be obsolete or losing value or depreciating quickly." This is the expert's opinion.

## Competitors

### Ultima (low cost, high throughput)
- The expert calls Ultima an example of a company offering ultra-low-cost, high-throughput sequencing. Its technology may fundamentally enable lower cost per sample-to-insight.
- Weakness: the company "isn't as robust organizationally" to handle large-scale projects, which its products target. This makes winning such deals hard. The expert cannot speak to how Ultima's deals have gone, only to deal flow they have seen.
- Relative to an Ultima-like cost competitor, the expert says Illumina has "traded off perhaps quality for cost," turnkey solutions for cost, and breadth of products for cost. (The wording is ambiguous; the expert is comparing the two approaches, and it is unclear in which direction each trade-off runs.)
- Where the market goes, the expert expects Ultima to capture a growing number of customers focused on broad access, such as new diagnostics or very large data-generation initiatives, where cost adds up. For very large-scale whole-genome research programs, Ultima becomes a target partner because of its low cost basis, "despite not having the breadth or the quality or the robustness."
- Illumina's offsetting strengths: it is much larger and more mature in business development and sales, with broader network and reach across verticals, more recognized brand and presence in many more markets. By "sheer visibility and access, they end up capturing in that net."

### Shift to large centralized sequencing labs and service providers
- The expert says a business-model shift is a second force. Sequencing is now being "clustered" in large-scale labs and cores that serve many end customers, rather than "disaggregated" across many reference labs. Many reference labs previously went out of business when volumes slowed and they were underutilized.
- With the right workflows, segmentation, IT infrastructure, laboratory information management systems (LIMS) and analysis infrastructure, big cores can run on behalf of many secondary customers, "which is much more cost-efficient."
- Such a lab, whether an Ultima customer or an Illumina customer, becomes a service provider rather than the vendor having a broad fleet across many end users.
- Illumina's business model "wasn't initially designed for that." Many labs have grown smarter about outsourcing or using third-party labs rather than building their own, since building a full end-to-end system no longer makes financial sense when you can contract with favorable economics. The expert says that was not the case a couple of years ago.

### Service quality and compute cost (response to a startup co-founder anecdote)
- The interviewer relayed that a startup co-founder chose Ultima because its teams were on-site often and adapted to the startup's applications, rather than "dumping instruments and supplies" on customers.
- The expert agrees service quality is "pretty big," but says value is shifting toward the end-to-end pipeline and the compute and cloud infrastructure deployment.
- Wet-lab setup has become accessible: instruments have spec sheets and protocols, costs and labor are known and fixed, and third-party logistics providers work across many companies. Everything outside sample prep, library prep and sequencing is "pretty turnkey" with a smart operator. The US regulatory environment has also helped. Complexity varies by platform, research versus clinical use, and large versus small scale.
- The complexity the expert sees is in optimizing cloud and compute infrastructure, because "a lot of the costs, especially for chips and memory, have skyrocketed." With multi-omic data volumes, companies worry about storage, compute, recompute and token costs.
- Software around Illumina and Ultima "haven't really adapted quickly to this new reality."
- Illumina takes a hands-on approach with in-house turnkey solutions and a designer toolkit, "but even that is pretty complicated," and it has a robust implementation system. Ultima is also very hands-on, but the expert questions scalability and quality of delivery: time from contract to first data to analysis and interpretation.
- The expert "suspect[s] and believe[s]" Ultima's time is "pretty significantly longer" than a new Illumina fleet deployment. They say "time is money in biotech," and an extra three to six months means burning costs while waiting, with multiple optimization iterations after that. (Speculation, not measured.)

### Element
- The interviewer says Element gets the most attention as a strong competitor.
- The expert believes Illumina is "well-positioned against Element, given just the strategy that Illumina has taken on." They do not know how sustainable Element's position is and do not see "a core differentiation that long-term can out-compete" Illumina.
- The expert then lists other competition types:
  - Depth and resolution specialists, citing 10x and a company transcribed as "Five Prime" (probably a transcription error; the company is unclear), focused on different analytes and higher-fidelity data. These are "a very focused, specific area of competition."
  - Cost competition from Ultima.
  - Platform-like competition from Oxford Nanopore (ONT), focused on long-read (reading long DNA stretches) and multi-omics, simpler, and also competing on cost.
- A lot of competition "boils down to" non-hardware cost: compute, cloud, streaming and analysis. Solving these allows much lower platform-wide cost.
- Scale matters. A company with Illumina's customer base and sample volume will pass cost benefits through to customers as lower-cost products and services.
- "It remains to be seen who will emerge to be able to build a market that big." Illumina's moat is "how broad their access is" and its long tenure as the go-to, including in clinical.

### Roche SBX
- Interviewer: Roche's SBX (a new sequencing platform) has a lot of hype, but people say real-world performance must be proven outside Roche's own labs.
- The expert thinks it is "a good solution," with speed and turnaround-time advantages (from their understanding) that matter in clinical settings, where Roche is "laser-focused."
- Research customers (bioinformaticians, co-scientist-enabled workspaces, trusted research environments) care less about turnaround and may care less about cost because budgets are larger. Clinical customers care about turnaround for time-sensitive applications, clinical actionability, clinical utility and the evidence base (publications and case studies).
- Many end customers care only about those things. The expert says Roche focused on a couple of research areas that translate quickly into large clinical markets and is "doubling down."
- Timeline: "a couple of year lag" before more shows up. Illumina's clinical customer is "so sticky for so long that it's hard to move," but in rare disease, neurology and infectious disease, turnaround, cost, multi-omics and quality collectively "start to move the needle."
- Roche has other systems and, like Thermo, can leverage its broad portfolio for customer access, a big differentiator. The expert "wouldn't be surprised if Illumina starts to head in that direction too, of just expanding beyond NGS proper."

### Roche, Foundation Medicine and in-house sequencing
- The interviewer asked whether Roche might use its own sequencers for Foundation Medicine (a cancer genomic testing company Roche acquired, assumed to use Illumina sequencers), as suggested by another expert.
- The expert doesn't know how much that drove the acquisition. Foundation was "really novel at the time," and Roche did a related data deal around then. The expert's view is that the data bridge from diagnostics to therapeutics was a key rationale.
- Since then, over 50 liquid biopsy and molecular diagnostics companies have emerged, including Grail, Guardant, Freenome, Exact (said to be part of Abbott, "I think") and Caris, mostly in oncology. They compare platforms on cost, quality, assay design, clinical utility, sensitivity and specificity, and operate independently, so choices are "not tied to Illumina."
- At the time, Illumina was "the most accessible, clinically validated or proven technology" with the best evidence base, and most biomarkers in those panels were discovered and run on Illumina pipelines. Using Illumina was "really intuitive." The expert says that matches their experience with such startups.
- Today there are many more options. Public resources exist for building these products, so differentiation requires new analytes and biomarkers, lower-cost solutions, or narrower sub-markets and indications.
- Foundation then had "probably one of the most robust integrated clinical genomic databases out there in oncology," with patient registry, trial matching and recruitment, data mining for therapeutics, and economies of scale from being inside a larger organization with companion diagnostics (tests that guide a drug's use).
- Roche building in-house sequencing could give its diagnostics unit better cost access, but the expert doesn't think that "carries a ton of weight" for a diagnostics R&D organization. What matters is interoperability between the data sets generated.
- GeneDx, Guardant, Grail, Caris and Tempus all now have a diagnostics-to-therapeutics and biopharma bridge, using data assets for discovery, clinical trials and AI. This is "a really large emerging business." Tempus has been "front and center" and built the bridge "really well," talking about a "participant lifetime value stack." Foundation and Roche were early movers. The expert doesn't know whether internalizing an instrument platform is a "needle-moving strategy."
- The interviewer added that other experts also said the acquisition was aimed at the data, and that revalidating a clinical workflow on a new platform is not simple.

### Chinese competitors (MGI/BGI, GeneMind)
- National regulations and restrictions on data transfer, IP transfer and collaboration or investment with China have created "a natural opportunity" for China-based companies to build everything domestically.
- China has internalized compute and cloud development to the point where it is "on pace to compete with the rest of the world," with multidisciplinary tech transfer and close collaboration. Illumina "doesn't have that access within America."
- The expert doesn't see MGI doing much novel R&D. It is an "integrated national solution" with access to a very large population, centralized and structured data, on-site sequencing, and a concentrated pool of investors that cannot invest elsewhere. This positions it to reach significant scale.
- The expert's characterization: Chinese companies are "a fast follower with a lot of efficiencies" and have large compute and cloud backing.
- Forecast: "in the next five years" more national leaders will emerge as countries realize the value of their population data and restrict data export, licensing, sample movement and consent. Examples named: Africa and Taiwan.
- An international strategy is a big focus for Illumina, and it has been "very successful" at it; ONT also.
- The expert is "not as familiar" with these companies' products or IP, but has heard there is "not really a big differentiation angle" for a US user. The advantage is operating more easily within country.

### Long-read versus short-read (ONT, PacBio, convergence)
- The interviewer asked whether long-read, if more accurate, could displace short-read, and whether the two could merge into one platform.
- The expert thinks convergence is "eventually possible" ("that is my belief"), while saying they are not a PhD-level technologist.
- Today, large-scale efforts and complex cases often compare both, and labs "reflex" to a higher-resolution long-read option for complex samples, so work is a hybrid mix.
- As long-read costs drop, scalability and quality improve, a larger share of sequencing across areas goes to long-read. Long-read and multi-omic solutions see things short-read misses, such as structural variation (large rearrangements of DNA) and other variant classes that short-read misses or sees only a fraction of. Illumina is "trying to fill those gaps."
- Evidence cited: a Google Next 2026 presentation (public, per the expert) said multi-omics unified pipelines improve diagnostic accuracy "something like 20%." (Recalled approximately.) The expert also says publications show long-read-specific variant classes are "the stronger signal" than short-read-specific ones for a number of biomarkers, so for clinical actionability there is real-world evidence long-read makes sense "if it's affordable and able to be implemented in clinic."
- ONT (named as the example) can differentiate by focusing on specific applications and diseases: rare disease, neurology, and biopharma uses in drug discovery, drug validation and biomarker identification, where visibility into biology is more complete.
- Constraints: a quality, robustness or cost penalty. The expert says you cannot spend "$1,000 or $2,000 fully loaded in cost" per case to generate such insights at scale, and there is not yet trust in an ONT platform to implement at scale in workflows today, "perhaps."
- As evidence grows and costs fall, "it would be hard to make a case not to have a long-read-based approach if you're able to get that signal."
- Illumina's counters: multi-omics, better algorithms, and filling gaps. The technology is fundamentally different, so countering requires continuing to build a portfolio, integrating tools, or running multiplex/multi-omic in parallel. It is hard because Illumina has a lot of money in its current business that "won't just migrate overnight," and it must keep serving customers (support contracts and so on).

## Timeline and switching dynamics

- Interviewer summarized: a slow transition. The expert agreed: "I think it'll take a couple of years, at least for the clinical community to really buy into that."
- Possible accelerant: data and AI making larger data sets accessible to smaller companies through licensing, ending a siloed data era and creating a "data economy," which changes how conviction is built from evidence. There could be "an inflection point."
- Even after that, clinicians still need a couple more years to build products, adapt workflows, buy instruments, scale, validate and obtain regulatory approvals. "A whole lot many more steps" are needed to switch systems.

## Key takeaways (as stated by the expert)

- Illumina's moat is breadth of access, scale, brand, clinical evidence base and stickiness, and it remains the safe, lower-cost default for targeted and clinical work.
- Threats are fragmented: cost (Ultima), clinical speed (Roche SBX), depth/long-read/multi-omics (ONT, 10x, PacBio), and nationally protected markets (China, others).
- Illumina's acquisitions and platform commitments (SomaLogic, Fluent, DRAGEN, analytics) are directionally right but create capital lock-in and reduced agility.
- Competitive value is moving to turnkey cloud and compute solutions as chip, memory and storage costs rise.
- Switching in clinical is slow, with timelines of "a couple of years" for adoption plus further years for validation and approval.
