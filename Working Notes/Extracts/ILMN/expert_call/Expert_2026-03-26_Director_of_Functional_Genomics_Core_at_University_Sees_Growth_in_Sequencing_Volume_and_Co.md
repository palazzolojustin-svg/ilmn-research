# Channel check with a university functional genomics core director: NovaSeq X volume growth, shift to outsourcing, and rising interest in Element AVITI24 and Ultima

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-03-26_Director_of_Functional_Genomics_Core_at_University_Sees_Growth_in_Sequencing_Volume_and_Co.md` · Date: 2026-03-26 · Channel check on an expert-network platform (published 30 Mar 2026; interview 26 Mar 2026); expert is a Director of a Functional Genomics Core at a university in South Carolina; interviewer is an AI interviewer · expert call*

**Summary.** A university core-lab director (customer of Illumina, also a heavy Oxford Nanopore user) says their sequencing volume is rising on cheaper NovaSeq X 25B flow cells and outsourcing, even as NIH grant cancellations weigh on demand. They expect 15-20% volume growth in 2026 but falling spending per unit of sequencing, see Illumina holding share while Ultima, Element and possibly a newly independent Complete Genomics gain, and plan to buy an Element AVITI24 because mid-range Illumina reagents are too expensive. Views on Roche AXELIOS, SomaLogic and Fluent BioSciences are explicitly limited or speculative.

## Expert background and how to weigh the views

The expert is an associate professor in a College of Pharmacy and director of a functional genomics core (a shared university lab that runs sequencing projects for other researchers) at a university in South Carolina. The platform bio says they supervise all purchasing decisions for genomics projects. They are also an NIH-funded principal investigator (lead researcher on a federal grant) who uses next-generation sequencing (NGS, high-volume DNA/RNA reading) in their own research, and they teach a university course on NGS technologies and applications. [Expert: university core director, Mar 2026]

Relationship to Illumina: current customer, speaking from direct, current experience (interview dated 26 Mar 2026, covering Q1 2026). They are not a former employee. The expert is classed as "Doctor/Medical Professional", and the interviewer is an AI.

Customer base served: academic labs at their university, across the state and out of state, plus several biochemistry companies. [Expert, Mar 2026]

Instruments in use:
- In the core itself: Illumina NextSeq 500 (a mid-sized sequencer) and Illumina MiSeq (a small benchtop sequencer).
- For outsourced projects (sent to external sequencing providers): heavy use of Illumina NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina's high-throughput sequencer).
- Heavy use of Oxford Nanopore technology, with several systems in-house.
- In the process of purchasing an Element Biosciences AVITI system, and working on a "flow project" with Ultima Genomics.

Where the expert signals limits to their knowledge: they were unsure about equipment price competition, said "I'm not a great expert" on SomaLogic, said they were "not sure about any recent breakthrough" from Illumina, and said they "may be biased" on Roche. Those answers are opinion or speculation rather than direct experience.

## Demand drivers and NIH funding

- The top factor affecting their Q1 2026 sequencing volume and project pipeline was "NIH funding and uncertainty of NIH funding." The effect was a decrease, "because several research grants have been cancelled." The expert confirmed this was the only main factor for the quarter. [Expert, Mar 2026]
- Despite that, the lab's Illumina volume is rising (see next section), so the NIH effect appears to be offset by cheaper sequencing and new access to lower-cost flow cells.

## Sequencing volume and outlook

- Q1 2026 versus Q1 2025: total sequencing volume, "especially in outsourced projects which we are doing with NovaSeq X Plus is increasing due to higher affordability of new Illumina cells." (A flow cell is the consumable slide on which sequencing happens.)
- Versus expectations: Q1 2026 output was "Exceeding." The reason given: they now get access to the new 25B flow cells on NovaSeq X Plus (25B refers to a flow cell of about 25 billion reads; the transcript is garbled, adding "and they're getting access to the eight," which is unclear).
- Full-year 2026 forecast: total volume up approximately 15%-20% versus 2025.
- Outlook compared with 90 days earlier: the expert clarified that sequencing volume will increase but "the cost of the sequencing probably will decrease," meaning budget spent may fall even as data produced rises. On volume, they described themselves as optimistic for two main reasons: (1) sequencing providers started offering both 25B flow cells and access to Ultima, which allow volume to "greatly increase"; and (2) more labs are moving into single-cell and spatial omics (measuring molecules in individual cells or in tissue location), which need large amounts of sequencing.

## Consumables and pricing

- Illumina consumables and reagents spending, Q1 2026 versus Q1 2025: the expert said they are using fewer reagents directly and are not running in-house instruments, "due to very high cost of the reagents for mid-sized platform." Instead they put funds toward paying sequencing service providers, and "we are not paying directly moving forward." So direct Illumina reagent purchases by the core are falling, while outsourced spending (which uses NovaSeq X) is rising.
- Pricing trend observed: Illumina is "mostly focusing on the space of high-end instruments and aggressively decreasing the cost of the consumable for high-end instruments." It is "not as aggressive with mid-size instruments." This gap is the reason the lab plans to buy an Element system, to work cost-efficiently on mid- or low-scale projects.
- Equipment pricing: the expert was "not sure I can answer." They said Illumina offers a lot of discounts for instrument upgrades and some trade-in incentives, but "more or less the same as in the last year."

## Competitive landscape and share views

Platforms gaining or losing favor (recent year):
- Complete Genomics (a sequencer maker formerly owned by BGI, the Chinese genomics group) was "the most problematic" in the last year. The expert said that in the last week it was purchased from BGI by a Swiss company, so "it may rebound."
- On other companies, the expert said they are not sure there is great interest in short-read systems (sequencers reading short DNA fragments), which they called problematic. They believe the leaders will be Illumina and Element Biosciences, "because they have a special model," with Complete Genomics possibly returning.
- Factors driving platform preference: throughput, cost per base (price of each unit of DNA read), computational infrastructure, and support.

Expected share shifts over the next 12 months [expert opinion]:
- Illumina: stays the same.
- Ultima: "may gain share, definitely."
- Element Biosciences: may gain some share, "but not much" / "not a lot."
- PacBio: may lose some share, "especially their short-read sequencing system because it's not great."
- Complete Genomics: "very questionable," but having a robust platform across all ranges and having broken its connection with parent BGI/China, it may get momentum; "it can be huge spike for Complete Genomics."

Competitor innovations the expert watches:
- Ultima: "greatly decrease the cost per base" and provides a much higher raw number of reads per dollar, which the expert estimated as "3X, 4X less than the Illumina" in cost.
- Element Biosciences: its G4 in-cell sequencing technology.

Roche AXELIOS (formerly SBX, Roche's new sequencing platform, which the interviewer said launches "next year"):
- The expert said Roche has been far behind for many years. The platform's advantage is a claimed reliable read length of up to one kilobase (1,000 bases), but "the raw number of reads is going to be less than Illumina," and based on the technology they do not believe the cost will be competitive (the answer is cut off). The expert flagged possible bias. This is opinion, not direct experience.

## Purchasing plans

- Planned purchase in the next 12 months: Element Biosciences AVITI24, for two reasons. First, the cost per base is "the best" and it is in a mid-range instrument; for many projects the outcome "could be even cheaper than a high-end Illumina instrument." Second, it adds integrated spatial in-cell sequencing, making it "technically two instruments in one": a standard NGS system and a spatial transcriptomics system. The expert believes the AVITI24 spatial transcriptomics model will be the main driver of its sales and of their own purchase.
- Change in plans over 90 days: none, but they have submitted an NIH S10 grant application (the NIH program that funds shared instrumentation). It has been reviewed and scored by a board that aligns with funding. If funded, they "will move forward immediately." If not, they will resubmit and try to obtain internal funds.

## Illumina products, strategy and support

- Innovation: the expert is not aware of a recent breakthrough; "Illumina provided steady growth." The most interesting recent development is Illumina's purchase of Fluent BioSciences and its move into single-cell (analysis of individual cells) technology, which the expert confirmed is the most interesting innovation to them.
- SomaLogic (proteomics company, whose acquisition by Illumina the interviewer said is expected to close in H1 2026): the expert is "not a great expert" but is "excited that Illumina coming to high-throughput proteomics space and especially aptamer-based proteomics" (aptamers are short molecules that bind proteins for measurement).
- Customer support: no significant recent changes, "but we never had a problem with Illumina."

## Technology trends on the horizon

The expert expects continuous improvement in long-read sequencing, direct modification sequencing (detecting chemical changes on DNA/RNA directly), and in-cell sequencing. They also said that "the density or the cost per base will continue to go down more aggressively than in last 10 years."

## Key takeaways for Illumina (all from one customer, Mar 2026)

- Positives: volume growth on NovaSeq X 25B flow cells; no support issues; interest in the Fluent single-cell and SomaLogic proteomics moves; expected stable share.
- Negatives: NIH grant cancellations; high mid-sized platform reagent costs pushing the expert away from in-house Illumina instruments and toward outsourcing and Element; expected falling dollar spend per unit of sequencing; Ultima and Element viewed as the share gainers on cost.
