# Former Illumina Associate Director (Product Management, Multi-omics) on Share Erosion, Ecosystem Strategy, Competitors and Talent Loss

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-24_Former_Associate_Director_at_Illumina_Inc._Believes_Market_Share_Erosion_Will_Stabilize_Am.md` · Date: 2026-07-24 · AlphaSense expert call (published 2026-07-28); interviewee is a former Illumina Associate Director of Product Management, Multi-omic and Multi-modal assays (left July 2025); interviewer is a buy-side client (unnamed) · expert call*

**Summary.** A former Illumina strategy and product manager argues that Illumina's sequencing share has fallen from about 80% to the mid-60s percent and will likely level out near 50%, because clinical switching barriers keep entrenched customers in place. Illumina is responding by building an ecosystem of assays (spatial, single-cell, pseudo-long-read) rather than competing on price, but the expert thinks product cycles are too slow, competitors such as 10x, Element, Ultima and Roche are moving faster, and Illumina is losing strategy and R&D talent while leadership relies on long-tenured insiders.

# Expert call notes: Former Illumina Associate Director on Illumina's competitive position

## Expert background and credibility

**Source and format.** This is an AlphaSense expert call. The interview took place on 24 Jul 2026 and was published on 28 Jul 2026. The interviewer is a buy-side (investor) client who says they do competitive-intelligence-style work and have already spoken to other experts on this topic. AlphaSense's AI summary describes the expert's perspective as "former" and the analyst perspective as "investor-led (buy-side)."

**Who the expert is.**
- The expert was Associate Director, Product Management, Multi-omic and Multi-modal assays at Illumina. They led a team developing new products for Illumina's spatial and single-cell platform and worked with the corporate strategy team on next-generation sequencing (NGS) and the competitive landscape. The role was global.
- They reported to the VP of Product Management, two levels below the C-suite (top executives).
- They left Illumina in July 2025, so their direct experience is about one year old at the time of the call. They now do consulting work in the same industry.
- Career path (in their words): a PhD in chemistry. They began in tissue diagnostics at Roche, with some early collaborations with Illumina. They moved to Adaptive Biotechnologies around 2020 as a product manager supporting its immune medicine platform, working closely with Illumina. They then spent four years at Illumina: first in market development (finding new applications, especially in immunity and infectious disease), then in corporate strategy focused on competitive intelligence, and finally as product manager for Illumina's new spatial product, which is "just now being released."
- Their strategy role began around the time Illumina's patents were expiring and new sequencer competitors were arriving, so the team was being expanded and they had broad exposure to the market.
- AlphaSense lists them as able to speak about Roche and other competitors: MGI, BGI, Element, Singular, Ultima and Oxford Nanopore (ONT).

**Direct experience versus opinion.** Much of the account of Illumina's history, strategy, spatial development, and competitive deals is first-hand (up to mid-2025). Statements about the Chinese vendors, Roche's SBX system, Ultima's second-generation instrument and Element's newest features are outside observation or speculation, and the expert often says so ("I don't know," "on paper," "I'm expecting"). The talent and management views are explicitly described as personal opinion. After they left in July 2025, anything about later developments is second-hand.

## Summary of the expert's overall stance (AlphaSense AI summary and call)

- The expert believes Illumina's market share will keep eroding but could stabilize around 50% if current strategies continue.
- They are skeptical of Illumina's competitiveness because of slower innovation cycles, loss of key talent, and a culture resistant to change.
- They doubt Illumina can regain its former leadership without significant strategic and cultural change.

## Why Illumina's leadership position is threatened

**Historical context [Expert: former Illumina associate director, Jul 2026].** When the expert joined corporate strategy, Illumina still had a "monopoly" in the space. Its only real competitor was Thermo Fisher's Ion Torrent, widely considered a subpar technology. MGI and BGI (Chinese sequencing companies) were largely not allowed to sell in the U.S. and were also considered inferior. Once alternative technologies appeared, there was a big appetite for lower prices and, in some cases, higher quality.

**Share erosion.**
- The expert says Illumina's ability to charge a price premium was threatened, and market share "fell from probably around 80% down now into the mid-60s percent and just continuously eroding over time."
- Customers replacing existing Illumina instruments found three things in competitors:
  1. Better prices and promotions on the instruments themselves.
  2. Better consumables pricing (consumables are the reagent kits and flow cells bought for every run), via contracts or even retail prices, which Illumina struggled to match. Example: Element could push Illumina's consumable margins "to below 50% in some cases," which Illumina had no appetite for.
  3. Technology that was competitive and in some cases better. Example: Element AVITI's quality was "simply better" than Illumina's when it launched.

**Technology differences (expert's chemistry reasoning).**
- Illumina's sequencing by synthesis adds "unnatural bases," so as read length grows the polymerases (the enzymes that build the new DNA strand) have a harder time attaching, because the sequence becomes less and less natural.
- Element's AVITI uses sequencing by binding, which does not have that problem, so quality is somewhat better. The expert calls it "simply a better way to do sequencing."
- Ultima's ppmSeq gives a Q70 quality score (Q scores measure accuracy; higher is better), although the expert says Ultima has "some other issues."
- Takeaway from the expert: "It's not just all about price."

**Commoditization.**
- The expert says commoditization is "out of their hands," because competitors' pricing is so low and the technology is now "so ubiquitous and simple" (despite complicated chemistry).
- "There just is no reason to choose Illumina sequencers in most cases anymore, especially if you are a new customer starting out," because alternatives offer comparable data quality, functionality and reliability. The expert calls it "a race to the bottom right now in terms of pricing."

## NovaSeq X disappointment

- Historically, each new Illumina platform produced a stock-price boost and a jump in top-line growth that year, through new instrument purchases and then higher sequencing volume. The higher throughput and lower cost meant volume outpaced the price cut. Example given: a 25% price decrease might be followed by 2X or 3X more sequencing.
- With the NovaSeq X, "that really wasn't the case." Placements (instrument sales and installs) were slower than expected, and sequencing intensity did not get ahead of the price decrease. The expert attributes the "mostly flat growth" since its release to this.
- The expert's view is that Illumina cannot win on cost with new, higher-throughput instruments: becoming the cost winner would fundamentally change its culture and value proposition. "They are a premium company with a premium product. You can't just overnight switch and say, 'Oh, actually, we're just a commodity.'"

## Illumina's strategic response: the ecosystem play

The expert's interpretation is that, starting around when competitors arrived, Illumina made a "complete strategic shift" to an ecosystem play, increasing customer attachment and reducing customers' desire and ability to go elsewhere. Products cited as evidence:
- **StrataMap**, Illumina's spatial biology product, "releasing this quarter," possibly already shipping. It is Illumina-developed from the ground up.
- **The five-day solution** (mentioned without detail).
- **Fluent BioSciences**, acquired for a single-cell solution.
- **Constellation long reads, now TruPath**, which the expert calls a "pseudo-long read technology" addressing genome-mapping concerns. Customers who historically went to true long reads can now "stay here and get a lot of that information."
- The expert says positioning is now about "all of these amazing things that you can do in DNA, RNA, and multi-omics." Other companies are doing the same. Element's play was ecosystem "from day one": AVITI, then AVITI24, which can be modified for single-cell and now spatial analysis.
- General view: sequencing is moving toward commoditization, so differentiation comes from unique assays and sample preps.

## Market share outlook

- The expert says erosion was running at about three percentage points a year, citing DeciBio data that Illumina's strategy team used. That "wasn't going to continue."
- Forecast: "around 50% is going to be a reasonable number to flatline at," because by then "everyone who wants to leave or has a good reason to leave will have left." It would then "just meander around there."
- Condition: this assumes Illumina continues bolstering multi-omics offerings and making iterative product improvements to keep pace.
- AlphaSense summary: share fell from roughly 80% to the mid-60s and is expected to stabilize around 50%.

### Switching barriers in clinical

- The expert says this is "a huge part" of why share should stabilize.
- Example from Adaptive Biotechnologies: its MRD assay (minimal residual disease, a test for leftover cancer cells), clonoSEQ, is run centrally from Seattle. It was developed on an older Illumina instrument, the 550, a mid-throughput machine costing "probably close to 10X as much per read as the current generation of NovaSeq." To the expert's best knowledge, Adaptive still has not updated its fleet, about 10 years of incurring those higher costs, because internal friction is so great.
- Switching platforms for an FDA-approved or cleared product requires revalidation and would be "incredibly hard." Illumina's first-mover advantage in clinical is "going to be a huge advantage that other companies, I honestly do not know how they're going to ever address."

## Low-throughput (MiSeq tier) phase-out

The client had heard the MiSeq tier is being phased out. The expert's answer was "yes and no":
- Low-throughput has always been low revenue for Illumina: instruments and reagents are inexpensive and there are few customers. It is often an entry point; customers buy low-throughput and later graduate to mid- and high-throughput. The expert guesses Illumina has good internal data on this, which is why it kept the line so long.
- Risk if removed: especially in developing countries, it could let competitors (specifically Element, which now offers low, mid and high throughput) build relationships early.
- But the product line generates very little revenue, and removal may be a calculated risk, since low-throughput is "dying because sequencing has gotten so cheap."
- Per sample or per read, low-throughput instruments are expensive compared with mid and high throughput. A new customer might do better buying a mid-throughput instrument and using smaller flow cell options, for flexibility and future-proofing. Illumina may choose to focus on premium customers and leave others behind (speculation).

## Pricing structure and customer tiers

Responding to a small-biotech co-founder's comment that Illumina's pricing is "wild" for smaller labs, the expert gave two causes:
1. Smaller labs often use lower-throughput instruments, so the retail price per base is much higher.
2. Big discounts go to large customers (e.g., the Broad Institute; clinical companies such as Natera, Guardant, Foundation Medicine). They could pay half or even a quarter of what a small CRO (contract research organization) or core lab pays per base on the same instrument. Adding a lower-throughput instrument, the difference "can go up easily five or tenfold."

## Diversification: spatial biology and single cell

**Spatial product timing and competition (direct experience).**
- The expert was part of developing the spatial product, which took four years to reach market. Over that time the market changed: Illumina originally competed against essentially a single product, and the product as originally conceived "would have just blown it out of the water." Now it faces two legitimately competitive instruments.
- 10x Genomics has just released a spatial instrument called the Atera that does much of the same as Illumina's solution. The expert thinks Illumina is likely "scrambling to adjust their business cases right now down" (speculation).
- Many labs are rethinking their commitment to Illumina's spatial solution and want to see how 10x's plays out. The expert sees a recurring pattern: Illumina generates hype, and by launch competitors have improved, so researchers "stick where I am."
- Illumina's brand still helps: researchers want assurance that a product will be supported in a year or two, so Illumina's spatial product will do better than an unknown startup's. But it will be "hard for them to be a leader in any of these spaces individually" if products take two, three or more years to reach market. The expert cited the book The Innovator's Dilemma: larger firms become less nimble.

**Single cell.**
- Fluent is a classic single-cell technology, fundamentally different from the most accepted single-cell technology, 10x Chromium. Its main value proposition: it requires no instrument and costs less per cell, especially at larger scale.
- The expert reads the Fluent purchase as a price play to drive sequencing consumables and attachment of Illumina sequencers: cheaper single-cell means more sequencing.
- Fluent has "really struggled to gain broader acceptance"; researchers resisted it as suboptimal versus what they have.

**Market sizes (expert estimates).**
- Single cell: "probably over $1 billion now. Probably even approaching $1.5 billion."
- True spatial (wide-breadth, many markers): "probably not even a $500 million market today," although projections five years ago were that it would "go off like a rocket ship." It remains small because it is a discovery tool with an unclear path to clinical. Single cell is finding potential "killer apps," led by 10x and collaborations with major research institutions, so its path looks clearer.
- The client recalled earlier experts saying spatial is about 5% of single-cell value; the expert did not contradict the direction.

## Relationship with 10x Genomics

The client asked whether Illumina could use leverage (prices on instruments and reagents) against 10x now that 10x competes in spatial. The expert said the opposite is true:
- 10x deliberately ensured compatibility with each new sequencing competitor and was excited to collaborate, because expensive Illumina sequencing reduced spending on 10x's single-cell and spatial experiments. Illustration: if a $1,000 single-cell experiment had sequencing as 50% of cost and a competitor cut that to 20%, customers ran bigger experiments and more money flowed to 10x.
- 10x was "putting the screws to Illumina" and pushed back on "hardball." Collaborations and co-marketing largely stopped, and the relationship became adversarial.
- 10x's first spatial product was sequencing-based. 10x has moved to non-sequencing-based (imaging) spatial, where it sells a roughly $500,000 instrument plus all consumables, a closed ecosystem. The expert believes 10x will try to phase out the sequencing-based solution "because they want the full wallet."
- The imaging approach uses fancy barcodes and can read out upwards of 18,000 markers on the same tissue, so the breadth sequencing used to provide is no longer necessary, which keeps it competitive.
- Conclusion: "I do not think Illumina has any bargaining or leverage over 10x at all."

## Chinese and other competitors

- **Non-MGI Chinese start-ups (GeneMind, Salus BioMed).** About five years ago their tech looked inferior. The expert said GeneMind's instruments were a deliberate copy of a previous-generation Illumina mid-throughput box, down to the exterior, at "insanely low pricing," but sold mainly in China and Southeast Asia, with no reports of European sales. GeneMind later released somewhat differentiated low-throughput clinical instruments, including one with Chinese regulatory approval for infectious disease testing. The expert does not see most of them as much of a threat.
- **Why cheaper doesn't matter beyond a point.** Once at about a $100 genome or $1 per gigabase, cutting further hardly moves the bottom line, because sequencing may be only 5%-10% of the total workflow cost (assay prep, labor, data processing). Customers are unlikely to move to an unproven company, particularly from China, given privacy concerns and country restrictions.
- **MGI** is an exception with a competitive line of instruments. It could sell in the U.S. through Complete Genomics, which the expert believes has been bought by a Swiss biotech holding company (so China ties should be largely severed; the expert is unsure of any complications). MGI has struggled in the U.S., is gaining some acceptance in Europe, but rarely appears in competitive deals or journals. Customers in China still buy Element and Illumina instruments. The expert gives these companies little thought.
- **Element.** AVITI24 is a mid-throughput system with single-cell and spatial capabilities in one. Element just released a spatial option for imaging tissue directly, and claims (not yet released) direct in situ sequencing, reading base-by-base sequence inside tissue to see point mutations and immune receptor sequences in spatial context. The expert expects quality might be poor ("I'm expecting the quality to be terrible") but notes Element does not release poor products. Element's reach includes low, mid and high throughput.
- **Ultima.** Announced a second-generation instrument, which gets little attention. The first generation was "terrible": expensive, massive, unreliable (stories of labs needing a service engineer on site eight hours a day), needed new cooling because of heat, and required structural reinforcement for weight, so uptake was very low. The second generation reportedly solves nearly all these problems, improves quality and cuts price to a list price below a $100 genome. The expert doesn't know whether Ultima burned its capital on generation one. On paper it looks competitive for high-throughput "factories." The expert wonders whether big clinical firms such as Natera, Guardant or Exact would be interested, given potential operating-cost savings (speculation).

## Roche's SBX system (AXELIOS 1)

The expert said it "could either become a big bunch of nothing, or it could be a serious disruptor."
- **Near-term weaknesses.**
  - First-generation chemistry (named AXELIOS 1), clearly "the beginning."
  - Requires duplex reading to reach Q38. The expert is anxious to see real-world data, suspecting the Q38 is a best case ("up to") rather than typical.
  - Quality context: Ultima at Q70, Element at Q40 with Q50 almost standard, and Illumina announcing a Q60 product.
  - Single-end reads (nanopore-based system), roughly 200-300 base pairs for duplex reads. Many experiments need longer reads or paired-end reads, and some inserts exceed 300 bases.
- **Clinical killer app.** Reasons: flexibility (run samples continuously as they arrive, sample-to-answer in four to six hours, versus 24, 36 hours or more on a NovaSeq where you wait to fill a large flow cell); potential for lower price through a reusable "flow cell"/substrate; and Roche's clinical experience and permanence, making it the most logical choice for another big clinical company to switch to.
- Example scenario: if Foundation Medicine (which is owned by Roche, though the expert does not state this) used AXELIOS 1 for clinical assays, "what a powerful marketing tool" (the expert says this "seems like it could happen").
- Caveat: real-world data is unknown, and it could become "just another sequencing option that never really gains much acceptance." AlphaSense summary: potentially disruptive in clinical if quality improves.

## Long reads versus short reads

- **Market share.** The expert recalls PacBio and ONT combined at below 10%, maybe "hovering just around 10%," upper single digits. The figure has stayed fairly consistent despite projections starting about a decade ago of long reads becoming much bigger, which fed Illumina's appetite to buy PacBio.
- **Expert's view on the PacBio deal.** It was not an anti-competitive move but about a robust long-read technology and Illumina seeing that long reads are important.
- **Why long reads matter.** In clinical and whole-genome work, a long-read genome is the gold standard. Regions with long tandem repeats (maybe 500-1,000 base sections repeating) cannot be mapped with short reads. Typical 100X genomes have gaps and mapping issues. Some diseases need long reads for phasing (assigning variants to the correct parental strand), for example mother-father-child germline studies.
- **But niche.** These applications are fairly niche, and not enough medical value has been found to bring long reads beyond that niche. True long reads run thousands to tens of thousands of bases; ONT reports hundreds of thousands to millions.
- **Merging markets.** ONT can also do short reads, but quality is not there; that is the main gap. A higher-quality nanopore technology doing both would be "very powerful." The limit is the enzymes pulling bases through nanopores too quickly and unevenly, creating noisy signals. Roche's sequencing by expansion adds spacing and a mechanism to stop base movement for more certainty, and something like it might bridge the gap. Currently nothing is available (speculation).

## Talent, leadership and culture

- The expert says Illumina does not have the right people. The reports of talent leakage are "absolutely correct, in my opinion," with many very good people leaving, especially on strategy and R&D.
- Meanwhile "Illumina lifers" who have been there since the early days, who "don't seem to accept how the market is changing," stayed and were often rewarded. The expert fears they have too much influence.
- The new "Danish management team" (the expert names Jakob Wedel and Jacob Thaysen) came in with very little sequencing-market experience, picked favorites among the lifers, and is "continuously getting poor advice."
- The lifers want to return to growing "20%, 30%, 40% year-over-year."
- The expert, who joined late and observed from afar, believes much of Illumina's success was "just luck": it bought Solexa's sequencing by synthesis technology and had patent exclusivity, and it became the winner. The expert rejects the insiders' story that their decision-making was the cause: "technologies come and go, and there's a lot of luck involved."
- The expert's prescription: embrace emerging technologies, take risks, and "consider themselves more of a life science tools company, more like 10x." 10x moved nimbly from single cell into spatial, and Element did too. "It doesn't all have to be about sequencing."
- Caveat from the expert: this is an opinion, "always just going to be the opinion of whoever is answering."

## Key takeaways (from AlphaSense summary, consistent with the call)

- Illumina's price premium and dominance are eroding to competitors offering better pricing and in some cases superior technology (Element AVITI, Ultima).
- Illumina has shifted from competing on price or throughput to an ecosystem of assays and applications to increase attachment.
- Share: about 80% to the mid-60s, expected to stabilize near 50% due to switching barriers, especially clinical.
- 10x, Element and Roche are innovating faster; 10x is moving to non-sequencing spatial; Roche's SBX could disrupt clinical if quality improves.
- Internal issues: loss of strategy and R&D talent, reliance on resistant long-tenured staff, and management lacking deep sequencing experience.
