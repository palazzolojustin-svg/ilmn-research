# Expert call – Sr. Staff ML Scientist (ex-Illumina bioinformatician, now at Exact Sciences) on Illumina's market position, competition, long-read vs short-read, Roche SBX, DRAGEN and support

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-23_Sr._Staff_Machine_Learning_Scientist_at_Genece_Health_Believes_Illumina_Retains_Dominance.md` · Date: 2026-07-23 · AlphaSense expert call (Company Deep-Dive; published 27 Jul 2026, interview 23 Jul 2026); buy-side investor-led; expert is a Sr. Staff Machine Learning Scientist described in the profile as at Genece Health and in the interview as at Exact Sciences; interviewer unnamed · expert call*

**Summary.** A former Illumina bioinformatician (4-5 years, DRAGEN and TSO 500 work), now a customer in cancer diagnostics, says Illumina is losing its grip in emerging and agile markets but stays very sticky in established clinical and diagnostic settings because switching platforms can cost up to about $200 million in new clinical trials. He chose Ultima Genomics (UG) at a prior start-up for 2-3x lower cost and very high base quality (Q60 with ppmSeq), views long-read and Roche's SBX as niche or in-house enablers rather than Illumina replacers, and calls DRAGEN strong but not a decisive differentiator. He rates Illumina's technical support as good but sees UG as more proactive and integrated.

The client's stated topics were: (1) Illumina as the "gold standard" sequencing platform now facing more competition, (2) the long-read versus short-read market, and (3) Illumina's attempts to diversify into single-cell analysis, proteomics and other testing. The interview covered the first two plus support and bioinformatics; diversification was not discussed in the transcript.

The AlphaSense header names Genece Health, but in the call the expert says they currently work at Exact Sciences, "which has been recently purchased by Abbott" [Expert, Jul 2026]. The expert's own account, not that block, is used below.

**Experience.**
- About nine to ten years in the industry since graduating (the bio says 8 years, the summary says over nine), covering next-generation sequencing (NGS, the technology that reads DNA in bulk), oncology, machine learning, and a little enterprise AI and multi-agent workflows.
- First employer was Illumina, for four to five years as a bioinformatician (a scientist who analyzes sequencing data with software). They worked on the DRAGEN team for four years and on the TSO 500 kit. They later joined start-ups and larger companies in diagnostics and pharma.

## Illumina's market position and threats

**Overall view.** The expert says Illumina has been very dominant in NGS, especially short reads (sequencing short DNA fragments, typically accurate), and that the usual concern is how secure that position is given competitors PacBio, Thermo and even Roche with their own platforms, and "they have their patent also expiring." They say "It's definitely looking quite grim for Illumina," then concludes "Illumina is definitely losing grip in this market," but "it still has a lot of stickiness, especially in established clinical settings, oncology and diagnosis" [Expert, Jul 2026].

**Why established customers stay.**
- Having worked in pharma and in small and large diagnostics firms, the expert says that once a product and pipeline are established, "changing a platform is extremely expensive," especially for products with FDA approval or LDTs (laboratory-developed tests, tests run in a single lab without FDA approval).
- Moving such products to a new platform could mean, in a worst case, "another $200 million of clinical trials to justify this change."
- This established clinical and oncology base is "where most of the money is in the market," and major players are "not in a hurry to change their existing product."
- No one is keen on changing "their million-dollar, billion-dollar product line to a new place with a lot of investment to redo."
- The client summarized that large or validated tests would need revalidation and process changes, so dropping everything for another platform is too much; the expert answered "That's very correct."

**Two conditions where Illumina is exposed.**
1. Big pharma developing new products: "it's anybody's game now," and the clear trend is diversification across vendors.
2. Agile settings (universities, small companies, R&D) where FDA approval is not the constraint and teams can pivot fast; there the expert sees "a lot of more fierce competitions."

**Personal example.** At a small start-up, the expert's team evaluated Illumina, but a competitor "very aggressively" pursued the business, knowing the team was a good target and had not yet fixed its LDT product. The team chose that competitor's platform for its LDT sequencer. Conclusion: in emerging areas "Illumina is definitely facing a lot of challenge."

## The Ultima Genomics (UG) decision

**Choice and reasons.** The previous company chose Ultima Genomics (UG). Stated reasons [Expert, Jul 2026]:
- Cost: UG gave new companies "really good discount," and even without discount the team estimated UG was about 2x to 3x cheaper per megabase / per trillion bases.
- Quality: the company needed very high base quality calls, and UG touted Q60 (a quality score meaning roughly one error in a million bases) with its ppmSeq library prep (the chemistry for preparing DNA for sequencing) and downstream analysis system. The expert says this was "quite important" and that unique features matter generally.
- Sales effort: UG's marketing was proactive (see support section).

**Why base quality matters in early cancer detection.**
- Most early-detection companies use liquid biopsy (blood tests), including pan-cancer detection, MCED and MRD. The challenge is a "needle in a haystack": finding ctDNA (tumor DNA circulating in blood) at tumor fractions of one in a million or one in a billion among normal reads.
- Methods include variant calling, CNV (copy-number variation) and fragmentomics (analyzing DNA fragment patterns), all aimed at telling real signal from noise.
- Example: if a sequencer's background noise is one in a million and the real signal is one in 10 million, the noise is 10x the signal and detection is impossible. The expert says base quality "spell[s] out your ceiling for the final detection accuracy."

**Alternatives considered.**
- Element and BGI/MGI: passed "very early on" after a broad study with marketing and technical staff, with the expert doing the informatics and simulation, comparing cost, base quality and target limit of detection.
- At that time (a few years ago) Element Bio had not yet released its platform.
- BGI was "a little bit cheaper" than Illumina but "not as cheap as UG after the discount."
- UG was "a pretty clear winner."

**Later UG 200 consideration.** The company was "seriously considering renting a UG 200" because it wanted ppmSeq, "the distinguishing factor that no other company is going to give us."

## Long-read versus short-read

**Framework.** The expert separates two confounding factors: the use case and the technology, then adds a third pillar, the market feedback loop.

**Technology (base quality).**
- Nanopore (Oxford Nanopore) yields longer reads but "the base quality is really poor… like Q20 something," topping out around Q30-Q35 even after machine-learning improvements. This makes it "very, very unlikely" for applications like MRD.
- If Illumina had long reads, it would not automatically keep short-read perks such as high base quality. Nanopore and Roche's new sequencing by expansion/extension approach "have quite limited base quality… it's just a noise issue."
- Limits are layered: hardware (sequencer design, imaging, primary and secondary analysis) first; assay design second (UMIs, duplex collapsing and ppmSeq can "double the base quality"); clever algorithms third, an "ad hoc afterthought" if the first two are lacking, where results can be "garbage in, garbage out."
- Example of algorithmic work: the Repeat Hunters publication (first author Egor, with Michael Eberle's team at Illumina, now at PacBio) on making short reads better at calling longer repeats using statistical, then AI and machine learning, methods.

**Use cases.**
- Long reads are used to call repeat regions, large inversions and large structural variations. These are not the primary concern in cancer, where tumor heterogeneity, TILs (tumor-infiltrating immune cells) and necrosis matter more. Structural calls "help, but it's not the main signal."
- Oncology is where most diagnostic and CDx (companion diagnostics) money is going. Per colleagues, public information and research, long reads are good for rare diseases and sometimes neurodegenerative diseases with mega-repeats and tandem repeats that short reads cannot resolve.
- These are niche markets, "not like a trillion-dollar market like oncology" (expert's figure for scale, not verified).

**Feedback loop.** A chicken-and-egg dynamic: small markets draw little research and money; but long reads now draw more research, more biomarkers, more applications and more money. The expert says it is a loop "but it's not really at a tipping point"; there will be no overnight discovery that makes it comparable to cancer. "It's not like Apple one day just replaced Nokia." The pace is slow, giving time to adjust.

**Threat assessment.** Long-read players (Oxford Nanopore, PacBio) have niche applications and are "not yet a major threat to Illumina's core oncology market" [Expert summary].

## Open reagents and the economics of consumables

- The client said newer vendors attract users with open reagent design (not being forced to buy the vendor's reagents). The expert was unsure ("Some people should probably fact-check this").
- Example: the TSO 500 kit is RUO (research use only), shipped as a toolbox; buyers can use part or all, third-party tools can replace parts, and there is little regulation forcing use. Downstream analysis is in users' hands. "It's a user-by-user experience and case-by-case."
- Expert insight: for Illumina and other sequencing companies, most revenue comes not from sequencer sales or services but from assay kits and buffers (consumables). Forcing exclusive reagent use would raise revenue per instrument but could limit instrument sales as some customers would choose another product. The expert calls this a "really critical insight." This is general reasoning from financial reports, not figures cited.

## Ultra-high throughput: NovaSeq X and UG 200

- NovaSeq X is "like doubling on the NovaSeq" and "the benchmark" for ultra-high throughput.
- Anecdotally mixed: some feel it lacks the dramatic change seen from iSeq to MiSeq to NovaSeq; "it is just like doubling the flow cells and stuff." The expert does not know Illumina's next step or whether it will revolutionize the space.
- UG's "UG 200" is a very high-throughput competitor; the expert has heard "good and bad things" compared to NovaSeq, and preferences vary.
- Bigger is not always better: smaller companies without a full flow cell must wait for a full batch, lengthening turnaround. Start-up assay teams validating an LDT (for example 100 samples, tumor and normal, with a V1 algorithm) typically use a third-party sequencing service rather than buying a sequencer. The service may offer waiting for a big NovaSeq X or UG 200 run or a smaller flow cell with results "tomorrow." The expert says people "would actually pay double just to get that information" for a key deadline, even though a flow cell might cost "200 versus 100."
- Decisions depend on goals, product size, budget, timeline and unique features such as ppmSeq, not one throughput metric. "You have competitors, the more the better."

## Roche SBX platform

(Client said SBX was released about a month earlier, in June 2026.)

- **Expert's view:** "a genuinely intriguing idea"; on paper a disruptor with potential, but the market's reaction to real performance is yet to be seen. Roche has wanted its own sequencer for years and failed decades ago; the expert has colleagues who led SBX projects, so they know some details.
- **Motivation:** big pharma wants to stop spending "tons of millions of dollars" yearly on Illumina, BGI, MGI or UG. Roche's primary aim is in-house use to cut sequencing COGS (cost of goods sold), not to replace Illumina. If sequencing were free, it would be an enabler, "not a replacer." Disrupting Illumina's position would not be unwelcome, but "it's not their primary goal."
- **Design:** built for in-house use, extensible, supporting longer reads and methylation and other signals, and not needing UG-level Q60. The expert recalls duplex-collapsed quality of about 45, 50 or 52. Under the hood it is "quite like the Oxford Nanopore," using heuristics and machine learning to improve signal-to-noise. The underlying nanopore-type technology means noise is higher and per-base quality won't be super high, though Roche has done well on informatics and assay sides.
- **Workflow (hearsay, "I could be wrong"):** some assay people said the bench workflow is not the best, creating pushback, though not the most important factor; decisions mostly hinge on accuracy, use case and features.
- **Adoption:** the expert "highly doubt[s]" people will abandon current pipelines to migrate; any uptake would likely come from Roche-driven collaborations with diagnostic companies, universities or European consortia, "a good PR stunt." "I'm not seeing that happening organically." Not expected to flock "as the next best thing."

## Bioinformatics: DRAGEN

- The expert worked on DRAGEN for four years and likes it: strong QC and benchmarks, "state-of-the-art for variant calling, for speed," using FPGA hardware (programmable chips).
- But it is "definitely not a decisive differentiator." In other companies' evaluations, "Most of the people just don't use it," even with Illumina sequencers and even when free. DRAGEN has cloud and on-site (onboard) versions.
- Reasons: Illumina's identity is a sequencer; companies don't want to build products on it. A start-up with, for example, "$50-million investment" will build an in-house bioinformatics team rather than depend on Illumina. Start-ups and large diagnostic firms run their own pipelines: BCL to FASTQ conversion from the sequencer, then maybe an aligner, with GATK for alignment and secondary callers, and in-house tertiary analysis.
- Expert suggestion: Illumina would need a "very radical" marketing approach, education or to make DRAGEN a standard "like everybody is running DRAGEN." Verdict: "meaningful, but… not a decisive differentiator."
- The client's premise that DRAGEN is platform-specific and not interoperable was not directly confirmed or refuted by the expert.

## Service and technical support

- **Illumina as supplier (expert's side):** the expert never had to call Illumina for support personally. Working on TSO 500, they supported collaborators, saw "hustle culture" with overtime and escalation emails ("we need to expedite on this") to keep external collaborators happy. This was five to seven years ago, and "I never heard complaints."
- **As customer:** on TSO 500 liquid callers the team asked for help interpreting VAF (variant allele frequency) and requested raw VCF files; turnaround was "pretty good" and "Those people know what they're doing." One sequencer maintenance incident saw an engineer sent in; "sounds like it's pretty good." Assay teams generally don't call Illumina about sequencers.
- **Main complaints heard:** whether reps give more discount, or remove the discount after a year or so.
- **UG comparison:** UG's marketing and technical staff were proactive, asking about other projects and offering technology suited to the use case. "If they hadn't talked about this technology, we probably wouldn't know about it." This was "actually… pretty important in making our decisions." The expert sees proactivity as more impactful than reactive service.
- **Integration:** UG is smaller, with a "closer circle, not as siloed," so staff cross-refer solutions quickly; larger Illumina is "very specialized" and may fix the stated issue without interdisciplinary problem-solving. The client likened this to silo problems seen in the CDMO market. The expert added UG "were pretty desperate at the time," which shapes their behavior.

## Expert sentiment and takeaways (AlphaSense AI summary, not the expert's words)

- Illumina is losing its dominant grip, especially in emerging areas, but stays sticky in established clinical and diagnostic markets due to high switching costs and regulatory hurdles.
- Ultima is a credible threat in specific applications (cost, high base quality, such as early cancer detection) but no rapid wholesale shift is expected in core markets.
- Technology change and market feedback loops are gradual, with no imminent tipping point.

## Risks and caveats noted

- Illumina patent expiry (mentioned by the expert as a concern, no dates given).
- Discounting risk: loss of discounts after a year can annoy customers.
- Expert evidence on Illumina's support is dated (five to seven years) and the UG decision was a few years ago, before Element's platform launch; Roche SBX views are partly second-hand.
- Document inconsistencies: employer named as Genece Health in the header and Exact Sciences in the interview; years of experience given as 8 and 9-10; the bio includes an unrelated former VP profile.
