# Ivy League genomics core director (channel check) on Illumina demand, pricing, share loss to Element/Roche/Ultima, StrataMap spatial and TruPath

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-09_Director_at_Academic_Research_Center_Sees_Competitive_Shifts_in_Sequencing_Platforms_Amid.md` · Date: 2026-07-09 · AI-interviewer channel check on an expert-network platform; interview 9 Jul 2026, published 13 Jul 2026; speaker is an anonymous director of an Ivy League university genomics core facility (consultant perspective) · expert call*

**Summary.** A genomics core facility director who runs Illumina NovaSeq X and a PacBio Revio says Q2 2026 sequencing output rose about 15% year over year from a weak, funding-hit Q2 2025 base, with 10-15% growth expected for full-year 2026, helped by cheaper 10x single-cell and spatial reagents. Illumina consumable spending is flat to down up to 5% because of lower flow-cell pricing, and the expert sees Illumina's price only going down. The expert expects Illumina to lose share to Element (their pick), Roche AXELIOS and Ultima, with new-instrument share falling to roughly 60-70%, and is most hopeful about Illumina's new StrataMap spatial slides while calling TruPath niche.

# Expert call notes: Director of an academic genomics core facility (interview 9 Jul 2026)

## Expert background and how to weigh the views

- **Who they are.** The speaker directs a genomics core facility (a shared service lab that runs sequencing for many separate research groups) at an Ivy League university. They provide next-generation sequencing (NGS, high-volume DNA/RNA reading), including single-cell applications (measuring gene activity cell by cell) and spatial transcriptomics (measuring gene activity at mapped positions in a tissue slice). [Expert: Ivy League core facility director, Jul 2026]
- **Relationship to Illumina.** They are a current, hands-on customer. This is a channel check (a customer interview used to gauge demand), not a former-employee view. The experience is current, covering Q2 2026 (April to June).
- **Customers they serve.** Mostly basic science researchers. Samples include human and mouse, and also insects such as fruit flies and bees, plus wild animals for ecology and evolutionary biology.
- **Equipment.** Short-read sequencing (reading short DNA fragments), mainly Illumina, for now: the NovaSeq X (Illumina's high-throughput instrument) and the MiSeq i100 Plus (a desktop instrument used for library quality control, small genomes and amplicons). For long-read sequencing (reading long DNA stretches) they use the PacBio Revio.
- **Competitor status.** They are actively evaluating other platforms for a possible transition over the next couple of years. They say newer competitors offer advantages Illumina's sequencers cannot match. The most interesting is Roche's sequencing by expansion (SBX) AXELIOS sequencer, which they describe as nanopore-based single-read sequencing.
- **Direct experience versus speculation.** Volumes, spending, shipping, surcharges, support and Illumina's pricing are first-hand. Competitor pricing, launch timing, share-loss percentages, Ultima UG200 cost and 10x Atera pricing are second-hand or forward-looking views. The expert says they "almost know" the UG200 detail, so it is not confirmed. Illumina's StrataMap performance is untested by the expert.

## Demand and volume drivers (Q2 2026)

- **Funding.** The top driver over Q2 2026 was an improved funding situation and anticipation of better funding, with a strongly positive effect on sequencing use. This means more samples and more kinds of application from the same research labs.
- **Comparison with a year earlier.** A year ago, many projects were paused or canceled, with a "wait and see" mentality. Over the past three months many of those projects have started moving. The expert also credits slightly reduced vendor reagent costs for more samples being processed. [Expert, Jul 2026]
- **Single-cell and spatial.** The strongest effect comes from single-cell and spatial transcriptomics. Both are improving technologically and vendors have "generously" cut prices. More single-cell and spatial samples directly create more sequencing demand.
- **10x Genomics single-cell reagent cuts.** The lab mainly uses 10x Genomics single-cell products. There has been a "quite substantial reduction" in the library preparation reagent (the kit that prepares samples for sequencing), formerly a much bigger cost of single-cell work. Cheaper prep means more cells processed and so more sequencing capacity needed.
- **Visium HD 3' assay.** 10x's latest Visium HD (high-resolution spatial product) now supports a 3' spatial assay. It uses poly-A mRNA capture with reverse-transcription amplification, like 10x's single-cell 3' mRNA-seq. Earlier Visium used pre-designed gene probes and worked only for human and mouse. The new 3' assay makes spatial work feasible in all animals and even plants, a much broader market. It also needs more sequencing data per sample to measure spatial expression thoroughly.

## Output (gigabases) trends

- **Q2 2026 versus Q2 2025.** Output on Illumina instruments is "increasing for sure," by about 15%. The expert says Q2 2025 was the slowest quarter of last year because of the unexpected funding change, so part of this is recovery from a low base.
- **Versus expectation.** Q2 2026 output met the expert's expectations.
- **Full-year 2026 versus 2025.** Expected growth is 10% to 15%. Sequencing use also rebounded somewhat near the end of 2025, which tempers the full-year comparison. Drivers: improved funding anticipation and cheaper single-cell and spatial reagents letting researchers run more cells or tissue sections.
- **Change in outlook over 90 days.** Slightly more positive, because of the Q2 trend and because new projects scheduled for the coming quarter or half-year look larger than Q2 volume. The expert expects higher sample volume in the second half, with initial inquiries and consultations already begun.

## Illumina consumables, pricing and supply

- **Spending.** Output rises but spending on Illumina sequencing reagent will probably be flat to slightly down for full-year 2026 versus 2025, "a few percent, maybe up to 5%." The reason is that Illumina lowered the cost of its big 35-billion-read flow cell (the consumable that holds a run). The interviewer's summary, which the expert confirmed ("Correct"), was that cost per flow cell fell so the lab gets more output for less spend.
- **Discounts and surcharge.** Over the past 90 days the expert says discounts were reduced, shipping cost increased, and Illumina still imposes a 5% reagent shipping surcharge attributed to tariffs. The expert called this senseless at present, said they questioned Illumina, and was told it is policy. [Expert, Jul 2026]
- **Inventory.** The expert does not stock up on Illumina reagent ("We do not want to stay over"). The transcript is partly garbled here, but the expert's stated reason is that there is "almost no chance for Illumina to raise their price due to the heated competition from the new sequencing platforms."
- **Price direction.** Quote: "Illumina reagent price has only one direction, that is to go down."
- **Lead times.** They "stay super fast," with shipping almost always the next day after order receipt.

## Sequencing equipment and competitive pricing

- **Per-genome price gap.** The expert says Illumina's current price is about $200 per human genome (whole-genome sequencing run cost). They say all competitors are lower, from $80 to at most $150 per human genome. They add this "puts Illumina in a very hard situation to maintain its market share." This matters if they must phase out their NovaSeq X.
- **Top three alternatives.** Ultima, Element and Roche SBX (AXELIOS). Appeal comes from lower prices and future improvement potential.
- **Ultima: ruled out first.** It is the lowest priced, but its read output is very different from Illumina's, making it hardest to analyze and least likely to be adopted by researchers.
- **Element: expert's pick.** Element's new sequencer, to be rolled out soon at about $100 per human genome, would be the pick if they replaced the NovaSeq X. Reasons: proven reliable read output and quality; read structure and error pattern most similar to Illumina; it can sequence the same physical Illumina library with no user effort using Element's Freestyle reagent and workflow, making transition "effortless." The expert believes Element's scale-up from the mid-throughput AVITI to a higher-throughput 10-billion-read sequencer will stay reliable.
- **Roche AXELIOS: highest potential, highest uncertainty.** It has the least public information and data. The expert will put most watching effort here and wants data disclosed by actual researchers, not the vendor.
- **Share outlook, next 12 months.** All three will gain. Element's new sequencer is the likely biggest winner, AXELIOS has the strongest potential as the newest technology, and "The only loser will be Illumina."
- **Timing and magnitude (expert's speculation).**
  - New sequencers reach market in about six months, near the end of calendar 2026. Until then Illumina will not lose, but more users like the expert will wait and see.
  - Competitor gains really start in early 2027, in the first six months of the 12-month window.
  - When the platforms ship, the expert thinks Illumina loses about 10% of share; counting new share added, "as high as 20%."
  - For new instrument purchases only (from day one of 2027), Illumina would hold "at most 70%, more likely 60%," losing its dominant position. The interviewer summarized this as competitors taking 20-40% of new purchases.

## Purchasing plans

- No new sequencer purchase planned in the next 12 months; replacement of the NovaSeq with one of the three is likely in one to two years.
- Plans were "a little pushed back" in the past 90 days, because of the financial situation and the fast-evolving comparisons among new platforms, which force a longer wait to choose well.

## Illumina products

### StrataMap spatial transcriptomics (most positive view)
- The expert says Illumina released technical specifications and pricing "just a couple of weeks ago" (so roughly late June 2026) for its slide-based, sequencing-based spatial product, now named StrataMap. It uses a large assay area with pre-sequenced address codes at about 1 µm resolution.
- Comparison with 10x Visium HD: 2 µm resolution; assay area 6.5 x 6.5 mm (an 11 x 11 mm area is coming soon). Illumina's slide assay area is 10 x 50 mm.
- Cost: the expert says Illumina's slide is priced "over $2,000," about half the price of Visium HD slides, and with the larger area the price per tissue section area is much lower. This "will unleash a large number of tissue sections."
- Uncertainty: real-world performance. Expected availability is the last quarter of 2026, and the lab is eager to test it.
- Lock-in: spatial position information on the slide cannot be read by any other sequencing platform, which the expert says is intentional so no competitor can take share. The expert calls it "the best hope for Illumina to gain market share in the spatial transcriptomics."

### TruPath Genome (formerly Constellation)
- The interviewer first asked about TruPath, and the expert clarified they had meant StrataMap. On TruPath: usage is "quite limited, only for a niche market."
- Reasons: it only helps with mapping and phasing of reads (assigning variants to the right chromosome copy), not de novo assembly (building a genome without a reference). Its added scientific value is limited. It can help detect structural variants and large insertions/deletions, but needs 300 ng of input DNA, a high hurdle for many research and clinical samples.
- The lab would use it on a small number of samples; not a frequently used assay.

### SomaLogic acquisition (proteomics, measuring proteins)
- No impact on this lab, which does only small-volume proteomics with DNA-barcoded antibodies. The expert's preferred platform is Olink, which is "more open."
- The expert reads the deal as aimed at binding proteomics to Illumina sequencers so competitors cannot read the output: "They want to lock in the protein."

### Support
- Illumina tech support, field engineers and scientists are "superior" and technically very good. The only dislike is pricing strategy. Nothing changed in the past 90 days; "consistently good."

## Competitor products and threats

- **Roche AXELIOS.** The expert says it is already a strong pressure: users needing high capacity are waiting for its commercial release. This is not yet visible, but declines may show in NovaSeq purchases and deliveries in this quarter and the next few. Called "a major threat," as is Element, at similar impact.
- **Element AVITI24.** Can do in situ sequencing (sequencing inside tissue/cells), or at least in situ culture for spatial RNA and protein assays. The expert hopes the new 10-billion-read sequencer has this too, for a much larger assay area.
- **Ultima UG200.** Expert says the new sequencer will cut human genome cost to under $60 (not confirmed). Short-read cost is already "over $75 per human genome" at current levels (as phrased). Cheaper reads likely favor large-scale single-cell, spatial and spatial ATAC-seq (chromatin accessibility) rather than bulk DNA/RNA.
- **10x Genomics Atera and imaging-based spatial.** Spatial can be imaging-based (fluorescence) and needs no sequencing. Atera is claimed to assay the whole human transcriptome over a much larger area. Expected price near $20,000 per run, slightly above Xenium 5,000-gene panel, already $15,000 per pair of slides. "None of the sequencing like or Illumina company can take a share of."
- **Long-read (PacBio, Oxford Nanopore).** Human genome run costs are falling steadily. PacBio offers reliable output and quality, and has rolled out a reusable SMRT Cell (the consumable chip) usable three times so far, with the expert expecting more. PacBio claims a $400 human genome, but only at 20X coverage. Long reads enable de novo assembly, which short reads cannot match. The expert expects PacBio to gain clinical share in whole human genomes.
- **AXELIOS read length (2-3 year view).** Roche claims reads up to 1.5 kb now; the expert expects about 3 kb in two to three years, well below PacBio's 15-20 kb. 3 kb covers most mRNA/cDNA length, enabling full-length mRNA and splicing isoform quantification in tumor and clinical samples, a "huge competitive advantage" if achieved.

## Broader views and recommendations

- Illumina remains the dominant platform and may try to hold it by locking in upstream assays so they cannot use competitors' sequencers.
- The expert says that for almost 10 years Illumina has mostly lowered cost while technical performance did not improve much, and hopes it invests in core sequencing.
- Recommendation: like Element, broaden the sequencer to support in situ applications, and make the system more open so the user community can develop assays, not only Illumina.
