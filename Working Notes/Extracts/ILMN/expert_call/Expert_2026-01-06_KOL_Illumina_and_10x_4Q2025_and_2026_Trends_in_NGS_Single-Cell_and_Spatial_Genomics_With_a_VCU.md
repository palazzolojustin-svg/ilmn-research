# Expert call: VCU Genomics Core co-director on Illumina, 10x and sequencing trends (Jan 6, 2026)

## Background of the expert and the call

The call was arranged by Guidepoint (an expert-network firm), moderated by Andrew Carmen, and ran about 40 minutes. The expert is Gregory A. Buck, a professor of microbiology and immunology at Virginia Commonwealth University (VCU) School of Medicine and co-director of the VCU Genomics Core. He is an academic key opinion leader (KOL, an influential researcher) and a physician-scientist. He is a current customer and user of Illumina and 10x Genomics products, not a former employee. His experience is fully current: he runs the core today and describes purchases made just before the call. The moderator noted he had spoken with the expert before ("once again").

Buck says he has directed the Genomics Core since it began. It started as a nucleic acid synthesis core, switched to genomic sequencing in the 1990s, and moved to next-generation sequencing (NGS, high-volume DNA reading) in 2006-2007 with the Roche 454 and early Illumina Genome Analyzers. Over the years the core has also had Life Technologies, PacBio and some Oxford Nanopore (ONT) technology, "although not very much." Buck also co-directs VCU's Center for Microbiome Engineering and Data Analysis.

Throughout, Buck speaks from direct experience about his own core. He labels his projections as forward-looking and tentative, and flags speculation explicitly (for example on indirect-cost negotiations).

## Instrument fleet and recent purchases

Buck says the core has had "pretty much all" Illumina sequencer generations: Genome Analyzer, HiSeq, and now a mixed fleet. The table below summarizes what he described and its status as of the call.

| Instrument | Status per Buck [Expert: VCU core co-director, Jan 2026] |
|---|---|
| NovaSeq X Plus | Just delivered before the holidays; not yet in production; expected in production "within a month or so" after installation |
| iSeq 100 | Just delivered at the same time; entering production with the NovaSeq X Plus |
| NextSeq 2000 | In production about three years; will continue to be supported |
| MiSeq (a couple) | Being decommissioned/mothballed "as we speak"; replaced by iSeq 100 |
| HiSeq 4000 | Mothballed for quite a while |

Buck expects no more sequencer purchases this year. Toward year-end he might buy other instruments, probably automation and possibly more spatial equipment. The core also owns a 10x Visium HD (spatial) system and a 10x Chromium (single-cell) system, and does the downstream sequencing for both. A funding opportunity for more expensive instruments should open "this summer" (2026).

### How the NovaSeq X Plus will be used

- Single-cell and spatial RNA-Seq work currently runs on the NextSeq 2000 using a P4 flow cell (the consumable slide on which sequencing happens). Buck expects the NovaSeq X Plus to take pressure off the NextSeq.
- He values the NovaSeq's flexibility: three flow cell sizes (1B, 10B, 25B, meaning roughly 1, 10 and 25 billion reads). He expects the 10B and 25B to be used "from time to time" but not be the heaviest use.
- His expected first impact: work that would have run on the NextSeq P4 will move to the NovaSeq 1B flow cell, so "people will get more sequence for less money." He says skeptics who doubted the core had enough volume for a NovaSeq overlooked that the 1B flow cell makes the instrument usable at modest volumes.
- As users realize a cost-effective high-throughput platform exists, he expects occasional 10B and 25B runs.
- Cost logic: the larger flow cells only make sense if filled to capacity. The 1B flow cell costs more per gigabase (unit of sequence data) than the 10B or 25B, and a filled 10B/25B beats both the 1B and the NextSeq on cost per gigabase and per read.
- Utilization guess: "maybe we'll have 25% utilization in 2026," flagged as a forward-looking statement. He does not expect 100% right away, noting the NovaSeq runs a 1B flow cell quickly.

### How the iSeq 100 will be used

It will cover microbial genomes previously run on the MiSeq. Buck also plans to use it as a cheap quality-control check, validating libraries (prepared DNA samples) before committing them to expensive NovaSeq 10B or 25B flow cells.

## Demand, utilization and growth outlook

- Utilization "continues to increase at maybe 10%." Q3 2025 was a flat quarter "for reasons that are not quite clear"; Q4 2025 picked up again. He sees steady rather than dramatic growth.
- Averaged, he sees a 10-20% year-over-year increase in sequencing. He attributes this probably to VCU's success in raising federal and other funding.
- For 2026 he anticipates a "pretty steep increase" with the NovaSeq X Plus, tentatively 10-20%, hoping for more beyond. He says that if volume doesn't rise, "we haven't really justified the purchase of that instrument."
- Reasons for expected growth: (1) in-house users who had outsourced high-throughput sequencing that wasn't cost-effective for the core may bring it back; Buck himself outsourced to the Broad Institute and New York Genome Center; (2) a new collaboration to sequence for other Virginia state institutions; (3) lower NovaSeq cost per unit prompting users to do more high-throughput sequencing.
- Buck's own caveat on Illumina: "it doesn't necessarily bode that well for Illumina" that work moves to his core from other institutions, because that shifts sequencing rather than adding total Illumina purchases. He does expect lower cost to induce more sequencing overall.

## Customer base and funding

- About 95% of the core's work to date has come from in-house VCU demand, with occasional external university or company work. External work has been uncompetitive because of "anachronistic," bureaucratic VCU rules forcing a surcharge on external users, even though in-house pricing is very cost-effective. The core has found a workaround this year and will be more competitive externally, though Buck does not expect a large influx.
- Customers are funded roughly 80-90% by grants, mostly NIH, with few NSF grants (a few in engineering, none currently for Buck).
- VCU says it has moved into the top 50 NIH-funded institutions in the U.S. (previously about 62nd) and has roughly doubled extramural funding in the last few years. Buck calls this "the one thing that's saving us."

### Grant terminations and policy risk

- In 2025, Buck had one grant terminated (a multi-year continuing grant, now "just gone") and another cut by roughly 70%. He says this was common, with behavioral genetics and DEI-related work hit hard. Some grants were reinstated after challenges, and funding seemed partly compensated elsewhere. The shock came early in 2025 and eased over the year.
- A CDC center grant shared across five states was cut 75%. His personal portion in year four was nearly $1 million, cut to $400,000; year five should have been near $1 million and he does not yet know whether it will be funded, cut 50% or 70%. He thinks CDC seems targeted more than other institutions.
- He is less fearful of further clawbacks now; researchers are adapting how they write proposals.

### Indirect cost rates

- Indirect costs are overhead added to grants that fund compliance offices (IRB, human subjects, animal care, grants management) and subsidize cores. VCU's rate is 54%, which Buck calls about average.
- An early-2025 push to cut all rates to 15% would be "devastating." He senses it will not be that dire but has no firm information.
- Existing awards appear to keep their originally awarded rate (he thinks because they are contracts and NIH could face legal challenge). Rates are negotiated on a five-year cycle; schools that negotiated in 2024 are protected, but VCU's will be renegotiated in 2026 (Buck is speculating; he is "not really in the circle").
- Roughly 20% of the core's operating budget comes via the Office of the Vice President for Research and Innovation, funded partly from indirect costs, a rough estimate he never calculated. If indirects are hit the core is likely hit, though how the university would prioritize is unknown.

## Pricing, tariffs and inflation

- Some vendors added tariff surcharges. Buck understands Illumina gave customers a discount covering about 5% of its tariff cost. On the instruments the core bought, Illumina applied a roughly 5% tariff surcharge, then gave a discount that offset it, so the core saw no tariff impact. Illumina is the vendor the core deals with most.
- He cited no other macro cost pressures.

## Outsourcing versus in-house sequencing

Asked whether low-cost outsourced sequencers (the moderator cited Plasmidsaurus) threaten the core, Buck said the core's mission is supporting the institution's research, and in-house technology offers design advice outsourcing cannot. His experience with the Broad and New York Genome Center showed this: the Broad "thought they knew more than we did," and returned data about 75% duplicative because they underestimated the amount of library. Without upfront design help, users can waste $15,000-$20,000 on unhelpful data. Most users, even experienced ones, consult the core before spending, and the core runs many quality controls so it has few failures, even on one-off projects.

## Single-cell: 10x Chromium and alternatives

- About 75-80% of single-cell work runs on 10x Chromium (Chromium X). Many are legacy projects of five years or more, and users resist changing because of protocol inertia, even for savings.
- Year-over-year 10x utilization has risen only "a few percent" and has not fallen. Buck rejects blog claims that single cell is declining, but says it is "probably not going up as fast as it used to be."
- Alternatives: some users are moving to Parse (combinatorial barcoding, labor-intensive), and a few to PIP-Seq (Fluent technology, which he says Illumina now sells). Two or three VCU investigators have used PIP-Seq, some repeatedly, and were happy with the low labor and low cost.
- Buck does not see Parse growing but expects Illumina's Fluent PIP-Seq to grow because it is lower cost and easier and "does seem to work." He expects it to "eat into the Chromium business." The Illumina sales force is pushing it.
- 10x's acquisition of Scale: Buck has not used it, lumps it with Parse as combinatorial and likely labor-heavy, has seen no one claim a serious advantage, and "kind of ignored it."
- Some single-cell ATAC (chromatin accessibility) work is done on 10x kits, but not many.

## Spatial genomics

- Buck is "really disappointed" in Visium HD uptake. The core bought the system over a year before the call; interest was high but only "a couple people" have used it. He feels 10x has "pretty much abandoned" Visium HD to push Xenium.
- Two other VCU cores have NanoString GeoMx platforms (the core itself got two GeoMx units three or four years ago); utilization is very low, with perhaps five or six sequencing readouts ever. He has heard little interest in CosMx (NanoString/Bruker).
- Xenium (10x's higher-resolution imaging-based product, which reads with cameras via iterative FISH rather than sequencing): he would not be surprised if VCU buys one soon, maybe this year via the summer funding opportunity, but it may not sit in his core since the Flow Core and others also claim such instruments. He considers it a genomics instrument.
- Why spatial uptake is slow: one experiment can cost several thousand dollars and typically needs about 20 samples; sample acquisition and handling are hard; planning is complex (a colleague has prepared for at least six months for Visium HD); sequencing and analysis costs follow, and a biostatistician or bioinformatician is needed.
- His own project used Visium HD with the human probe set plus about 100 custom probes for bacterial taxa to see where bacteria bind and how they affect cell gene expression. It worked well, but 10x did not support custom probes; it only advised on design.
- Budgets are not shifting from single cell to spatial at VCU; they do both as complementary methods (single cell shows individual cells; spatial gives location). He has had pushback from people who say to do spatial only.

## Illumina's new products and adjacent applications

- Illumina protein protocol (SomaLogic-based, aptamer-based; the moderator corrected Buck's guess of Olink): nobody has requested it yet, but the core would run it on request. Colleagues in dentistry are considering it and others are looking at Olink, since readout is sequencing. Mass spectrometry proteomics is, in his experience, expensive and slow. Cost is a barrier: about $400,000-$500,000 for 100 samples (a figure he related for SomaLogic).
- 5-base methylation sequencing (Illumina): not yet used at VCU, but he expects interest because of throughput and cost advantages.
- Epigenetics: no more than about 5% of the core's sequencing; a methylation-binding-protein profiling method had some success but was not overwhelming. Small RNA work is a growing interest. ChIP-seq (10-20 runs over the years) and ATAC-seq (no more than 10) are one-offs; he thinks some may migrate to the Illumina protocol.
- Microbiome: roughly 20-25% of sequencing (counting viral sequencing). Buck is unsure it is growing but calls it a significant effort. He expects associations between microbiome and health (reproductive tract, mouth, skin) to matter, with fecal transplants the clear example and little impact seen from probiotics. Clinical use is limited so far; the core is not CLIA-certified (so results cannot go back to patients), and VCU's molecular diagnostics lab does cancer panels but not microbiome.

## Competition and technology risk

- Roche Axelios (sequencing by expansion, "Xpandomers"): Buck finds it very interesting but thinks Roche is "still a bit overstating their accuracy," because it measures accuracy by comparing one strand to its fold-back copy rather than to a known control such as the human genome. Only a few early-access instruments are placed, so he sees no big impact yet and thinks it is too early for a core like his; new sequencers typically need about a year to become turnkey.
- Past bad experience: the core bought a PacBio Sequel that "was nothing but problems" and became obsolete quickly. The core can no longer afford extensive troubleshooting.
- Obsolescence worry: sequencers have a half-life of several years. The NovaSeq X has been out two and a half to three years, and he hopes it is not made obsolete by a Roche or other (including Illumina) sequencer before it proves useful. He describes a trade-off between buying early enough for a useful lifespan and not buying into obsolescence.
- Long reads: the core is all short-read. Buck has an ONT MinION in his own lab (used once or twice a year, not offered by the core), and four or five other VCU investigators have them. The PacBio purchase went unused because reference sequences exist for most taxa, so short reads plus assembly against references covered many needs; "the users didn't come through for us."

## Concluding view

Buck says he may be "unique in the world" but has "always been bullish on sequencing." He expects Illumina to face inevitable challenges, but thinks clinical sequencing will carry the market. He is surprised more whole-genome or continuous diagnostic sequencing is not already done, calls it inevitable, and expects cost per gigabase to keep falling and applications in human health to keep growing.
