# Expert call with PacBio Global Segment Lead for Human Genomics (ex-Illumina): clinical shift, Roche AXELIOS, long-read limits, Illumina stickiness and pricing

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-08-03_Global_Segment_Lead_in_Human_Genomics_at_Pacific_Biosciences_Sees_Growth_in_Clinical_Seque.md` · Date: 2026-08-03 · AlphaSense expert call (company deep-dive); interview 2026-08-03, published 2026-08-05; expert is a current PacBio Global Segment Lead of Human Genomics and former Illumina Senior Health Analytics Manager (left June 2021); investor-led (buy-side) client · expert call*

**Summary.** A current PacBio human-genomics marketing lead who previously worked at Illumina describes a sequencing market shifting from NIH-pressured academic buyers to more reliable clinical buyers helped by reimbursement. The expert views Illumina as the dominant, very "sticky" incumbent because of its end-to-end ecosystem, but says some customers are frustrated with its pace of innovation and are watching Roche's new AXELIOS platform (launched July 2026), Element and Ultima. Long-read sequencing is growing but held back by cost, throughput and missing population databases and clinical software. Advertised per-genome prices ignore informatics and storage costs, which are flat or rising.

## Expert background and relevance

**Who the expert is.** The expert is the Global Segment Lead of Human Genomics at PacBio (Pacific Biosciences, a maker of long-read sequencers, which read long continuous stretches of DNA). They lead strategy and marketing for rare disease, pharmacogenomics (how genes affect drug response), clinical and translational research, population genomics and transcriptomics (measuring RNA, the gene-activity readout). Their work covers market development, evidence generation, product launches, software and analysis partnerships, sales enablement and relationships with key opinion leaders (KOLs). [Expert: PacBio Global Segment Lead, Aug 2026]

**Relationship to Illumina.** The expert is a former Illumina employee and now a competitor. They were Senior Health Analytics Manager at Illumina and left in June 2021, so their inside Illumina knowledge is about five years old. At Illumina they led data analysis for market access research, using real-world data to support insurance coverage and reimbursement of clinical next-generation sequencing (NGS) for children with rare and undiagnosed genetic disease. They also led collaborations on pharmacogenomics and on clinical use of whole genome sequencing. They have spent a little over a decade in sequencing, across R&D, product development, market access, market development and marketing, and earlier did genomics research.

**How to weigh the views.** Comments on the current market, PacBio and long-read, Roche's launch (the expert attended a Roche workshop and saw conference and webinar data) and customer conversations come from recent direct experience. The expert repeatedly labels views on Illumina's reputation, customer frustration, startups and Illumina's strategy as "anecdotal". They say they lack expertise on proteomics and oncology/MRD (minimal residual disease testing, which looks for tiny traces of remaining cancer), because they work only on germline (inherited DNA) testing. Their employer sells a competing technology, so their views on long-read may be favorable to it. The call is classed as a competitor perspective, with an investor-led (buy-side) client.

## Market growth and drivers

**Overall picture.** Sequencing "really picked up" over the past five years, particularly in clinical settings. The expert says the past two years brought challenges that changed how sequencing companies think about their customer base. Overall, "we've seen growth this year" [Expert, Aug 2026].

**Academic weakness.** NGS was historically adopted heavily in academia and research. The expert says this changed "fundamentally" last year (2025) when the NIH (US National Institutes of Health) capped indirect costs, making it much more expensive for university labs to invest in instruments. Academic research is also project-based, so revenue is variable. With NIH funding "drying up", academic funding has fallen.

**Clinical strength.** Clinical work carries heavier regulatory requirements, but revenue is "much more consistent". Reimbursement trends (growing insurance coverage for genetic testing, for example in oncology and rare disease) "open up the doors" for sequencing companies and have "made up for the reduction in academic funding". Once a sequencing assay is in routine use, this gives "a really solid base for steady revenue".

**Accelerators.** The expert names cost and throughput as the biggest accelerators. On short-read, with Illumina and Roche, "it's a race to the bottom in terms of cost", and has been for some time. Higher instrument throughput means that clinically, genetic tests can be run more routinely. Roche launched its AXELIOS platform commercially in July 2026.

**Brakes.**
- **Capital cost.** Despite cheap genomes, machines are expensive. The NovaSeq X (Illumina's high-throughput sequencer, and its biggest install base) costs almost $1 million for the instrument alone. Roche's AXELIOS was announced at $750,000, "so almost $1 million as well" [Expert's recollection].
- **Data.** Growth in data and the cost of storing it, plus interpretation bottlenecks. The expert does not think the bioinformatics community has "quite caught up", and says clinical customers will struggle to interpret and store data in a clinically useful way.

## High-throughput versus low-throughput segments (clinical)

- **High throughput.** Depends on how testing is organized by region. In the US, some health networks centralize testing, and applications such as NICU (neonatal intensive care unit) testing go to large reference labs such as Labcorp, GeneDx and Quest. There it comes down to high-throughput technology and which lab adopts which instrument, and these are the buyers "who have money right now". Roche's announced pre-orders focus on these high-throughput centers.
- **Low throughput.** There is still room for it, especially "from the ground up". Hospitals may lack volume and must wait to batch samples, so they want smaller instruments. Illumina retired the MiSeq, and some buyers are looking to backfill that volume for specific tests such as panels (targeted tests on selected genes) rather than whole genomes. The targeted market, with specific panels, is still the big market for low throughput.

## Long-read versus short-read

**Where long-read stands.** Long-read technologies and other specialized genomic technologies had roots in academia, and the expert says over 50% of their customer base was in academic research. As that funding recedes, both long-read companies (PacBio and Oxford Nanopore) are emphasizing clinical markets.

**Trade-offs.** Long-read has higher cost, lower throughput and longer turnaround than short-read. In return it detects variant types short-read cannot. Its benefits are assay consolidation (information from several assays in one) and, in specific situations, favorable economics. Targeted use cases include repeat expansions, structural variants and copy number information. The expert calls long-read "almost the gold standard" for a complete, comprehensive genome. Focus for both companies is mostly whole-genome applications such as rare disease, plus the value of long-read in targeted settings.

**Adoption path.** Growth is seen first in regions with national genomics programs that "want the best of the best" (biobanking, population-specific reference genomes). It then trickles to clinical research and eventually routine clinical use. Long-read is "a little bit" behind short-read on cost and throughput, though more studies show clinical usefulness, and the expert sees "a lot of growth opportunities as cost goes down."

**Is cost the only barrier?** The expert says it is not accuracy. For current long-read uses, which are mostly germline, "the accuracy is basically there", and people will not pay more for higher accuracy. Accuracy matters most in cancer and MRD, but the DNA quality needed there "don't really apply to long-read". Other long-read uses (methylation/epigenetics, full-length RNA isoforms) are "not clinically useful applications yet".

**What would change utilization.**
- Short-read has large population databases showing whether a variant is common or rare. Long-read lacks these, since it needs vast data across populations, and building them requires international collaboration that is "really not quite there".
- Short-read bioinformatics resources and tools cannot be applied to long-read data. An active community is building long-read tools, strong in research, but clinical relevance of long-read findings is "not quite there yet" and is "still a major challenge".

## Illumina's position and why it is sticky

**Leadership.** Illumina remains market leader "by far" [Expert, Aug 2026]. The expert says Illumina has had a "monopoly" for quite a while and is the first choice in clinical.

**Lock-in.** When the NovaSeq X was announced, buyers hesitated because of the near-$1 million cost, and buying it locks a lab into the Illumina ecosystem, especially in clinical labs. A lab that validates an assay on a NovaSeq X is "probably not going to switch for a while". The expert says Illumina's Q2 earnings announced "a pretty healthy pipeline of NovaSeq X customers" (the expert's characterization of the call), even though anecdotally many people are interested in Element, Ultima and Roche's AXELIOS.

**Sources of stickiness (expert's view).**
- Customers have bioinformatics expertise in the Illumina workflow and have connected it to their EHR (electronic health record) systems.
- Illumina invested not only in instruments but in informatics and library prep reagents (the chemistry that prepares DNA for sequencing), making an end-to-end system with no need for partners to fill gaps. Customers also train staff on it.
- Application breadth is growing beyond germline DNA: a multiomics kit launched last year (2025), SomaLogic purchased this year (2026) for proteomics, and entrenchment across them.
- Competitors "are really going to need to differentiate". Roche claims four hours versus 24 hours for Illumina, which might pull people in time-critical applications. The expert does not see complete switching yet, and says some bigger customers are buying several competitor instruments to find the best.

**Dissatisfaction.** Anecdotally, "quite a few people have a lot of gripes with Illumina" and are looking for something different, but alternatives are "not quite out of the box and push-button as the Illumina systems are".

## Roche's SBX / AXELIOS platform

**Market reception.** The platform has had considerable hype at conferences and in forums over the past year. The expert says the thought is that "Roche has not really successfully launched a good sequencer" yet. People are excited but skeptical and "want to see the data". Capex is as much as a NovaSeq X, so switching after spending $1 million on a NovaSeq X is a big step.

**Data so far.** Data shown at conferences and webinars in the past year has been "pretty good" and "up to the spec that they're claiming". More real customer data is emerging beyond company-only or reference samples, and it "looks decent".

**Technical characteristics (per Roche's disclosures as relayed by the expert).**
- Reads up to about 1,000 base pairs, significantly longer than Illumina's, though not classed as long-read.
- Accuracy seems higher than Illumina's.
- Cost metrics are not directly comparable to the usual cost-per-gigabase; they seem on par with short-read competitors, "maybe a little cheaper than Illumina". The expert says "it's still TBD."
- Two modes, simplex and duplex. A duplex 30X genome (30X is a standard depth of coverage) is about $150, as the expert recalls. Simplex could be cheaper but less accurate, and no simplex genome data has been published.
- A formal RUO (research use only) version launched in July 2026.

**Demand.** Excitement is especially high in Europe, where many want access or to have samples sequenced by someone who has the platform. For those seeking Illumina alternatives it is "very exciting", but they need more data.

**What customers want to see.** A variety of samples and variant types, performance in homopolymer regions (repetitive single-letter DNA stretches that are hard to read) and structural variant regions, and simplex data. Buyers usually want to run their own positive control samples against their current instrument before investing. Currently: "a lot of hype without a lot of data."

**Early customers and targets.** Data came from the Broad Institute and a large Spanish genomics hub. Early messaging stressed time-critical use, sequencing a whole genome in four hours, similar to a world record previously shown on Illumina. The expert infers a focus on acute testing, NICU and rapid whole genome sequencing in rare disease, and also oncology/MRD (inferred from accuracy and read length; the expert is not familiar with that area). At a Roche workshop, Roche said it is "really interested in the clinical market", and the expert thinks that, like Illumina, Roche wants "hardened routine clinical applications" rather than research.

## Illumina's response and competitive concerns

**Response.** The expert has seen no Roche-specific messaging from Illumina. Illumina is expanding applications: heavy multiomics investment in the past year (proteomics, methylation, RNA), and calling itself a "five-base sequencer" (native methylation sequencing, terminology long-read companies have used). Other short-read companies focus on lowering cost per genome and germline applications. The expert reads Illumina's approach as increasing overall genomics penetration rather than a defensive stance against Roche.

**Relative concern.** The expert says competitive intensity is "hyper-regional" (Americas, Europe, Asia) and cost often decides. The expert would expect Illumina to be concerned about Roche for clinical applications, because Illumina has shifted so much of its install base and strategy to clinical customers and Roche says it will focus there "right out the door". Other startups (including Element and Ultima by implication) are mentioned less than three years ago: "a lot of hype, and now we're not seeing as much adoption", reason unknown.

## Pricing

- Marketing language is hard to separate from customer reality. Illumina's TruPath (a multiomic solution using a separate chemistry) and the "$150, $200 genome" (the expert believes Illumina's claim is now $150) raise unanswered questions: does the price cover only sequencing reagents, or also Illumina Connected Analytics and storage? That is "not well-captured in any of the marketing language for any sequencing company."
- In actual pricing discussions, sequencing reagent and consumable costs are falling, and higher throughput lowers per-unit economics. But "all of the ancillary costs are either staying the same or going up across the board." People will keep pushing consumable and cost-per-gigabase metrics, but the other costs and their link are unclear, and it depends on customer and application.

## Switching costs

- **Short-read to long-read** is the biggest change for an Illumina shop: fundamentally different technology and workflows, not all automated or clinically optimized, shorter expiration dates, and a supply chain "not as hardened as Illumina's". Training, automation and data interpretation are major bottlenecks. Illumina's software is "very push-button" and not very customizable, but easy for clinical labs to start, and they know how to interpret and report the output. Long-read solutions can be end-to-end but are more piecemeal for niche applications. Clinical validation may lack clear SOPs (standard operating procedures) and clinical reference materials. It is "a new frontier" and "a much bigger lift", though some do it.
- **Illumina to another short-read vendor:** workflows and data are very similar, short-read interpretation software works on any platform, so it is "much more interchangeable" and a "lighter lift".

## Informatics

- Informatics is a major bottleneck in clinical and research settings, since most tools were built and benchmarked on short-read data, and grad students are trained on them.
- For long-read, new variant classes can be detected with high confidence, but interpretation is hard because there are no reference databases showing frequency. Some labs are building in-house databases from thousands of samples and compare new samples to them.
- Beyond DNA it is harder: short-read RNA counting algorithms and short-read/array methylation algorithms do not translate to long-read because the range of signal differs mathematically.
- Many long-read tools are research-only. For short-read, Illumina has "tried and tested and clinically validated" tools, well integrated with the sequencer. Switching to other systems would probably require bioinformatics expertise.

## Illumina's growth opportunities and reputation

**Clinical growth.** The expert sees Illumina's clinical market picking up and says "that's why they raised their guidance this year" (the expert's inference; no figures given). Illumina has a large team in medical affairs, market access and government affairs, championing the technology and generating evidence payers need to reimburse. The expert says Illumina "paved the way for all the sequencing companies" because everything was proven on its technology. They expect more routine, reimbursed genomic assays. If customers see it is easy to build an LDT (laboratory developed test) on an Illumina machine, that creates stickiness and steady consumables volume, "filling their boxes".

**Reputation as innovator (anecdotal).** It has "slowed down quite a bit", and people are "a little bit tired of them". New launches appear to be iterations of what others are already doing, fitting new applications to technology not designed for them. The expert is unsure expansion is "the right thing". Illumina is trying to build a complete ecosystem: the example is 10x Genomics, which previously made library prep and an instrument feeding Illumina sequencers and now does spatial; the expert asks whether Illumina entering that space is innovation "or is it them just trying to eat all their competitors?" The expert senses a "maybe negative feeling", and some customers want to differentiate with other technologies.

## Proteomics and SomaLogic

- The expert says they lack expertise (their technologies went no further than DNA and RNA). They view proteomics as "both near and far away" for clinical use. It could serve as biomarkers if studies show strong clinical signals (such as disease progression), especially paired with genomic data, but Illumina "would have to do the work to show that".
- Even RNA sequencing is not used clinically as much as expected, and proteomics is a step beyond. Research interest is high, but a few more years of translational evidence are needed. The acquisition gives Illumina a complete multiomics picture; market strength is "TBD".
- On integration, "we're still waiting to see". The acquisition was earlier in 2026, and the expert has seen mostly press releases and a TruPath/multiomics emphasis, little on proteomics. They are interested in what Illumina shows at ASHG (American Society of Human Genetics meeting) this year.

## Portfolio gaps: IVD

Pharma companies (especially those running clinical trials) often request IVD (in vitro diagnostic, regulator-cleared) boxes and applications. Illumina had the MiSeqDx and NextSeq Dx, which the expert thinks were less successful than expected. Roche has hinted at IVD, and requirements for clinical use are stricter in APAC and EMEA. If Roche moves in, the expert anticipates Illumina would too, and other companies have hinted. It could be a new Dx box or a specific assay (possibly oncology), but "nobody has actually put anything out yet."
