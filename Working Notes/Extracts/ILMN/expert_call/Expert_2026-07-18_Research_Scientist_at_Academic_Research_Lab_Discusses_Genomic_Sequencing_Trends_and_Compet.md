## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

- **Relationship:** Current customer and purchasing decision-maker (not a former Illumina employee, not a competitor). [Expert: academic research scientist, Jul 2026]
- **Role:** Oversees the sequencing operation of a laboratory of roughly 40 people, runs "a great number of sequencing projects", and is "involved in the purchasing decisions, making major choices". Also does RNA extraction, qPCR and digital PCR (two lab techniques for measuring nucleic acids).
- **Experience recency:** Current, day-to-day user. Mentions a paper recently published (an EMBO Journal paper, see below) and recent grant news.
- **Instruments in the lab:** Illumina NextSeq 500, NextSeq 550 and NovaSeq 6000 (Illumina's mid-scale and high-throughput short-read sequencers). The expert also says they send some work to a core facility for the NovaSeq 6000, and that the lab maintains the NextSeq 500 under a service contract.
- **Competitor platforms:** Not used day to day. The expert says "mainly PacBio and NanoString" in one passage, but the AI interviewer summarised this as no long-read platforms (PacBio, Oxford Nanopore) in day-to-day work, and the expert says "we work mainly on the NGS". The expert wrote a book chapter titled "The Power of Whole Genome Sequencing in Biomedical Research and Clinical Applications" and believes third-generation (long-read) sequencing is important for finding variants in non-coding regions.
- **Customers served:** Projects from pharmaceutical companies collaborating with academia and the hospital. The expert calls the hospital "probably... the best one" and says it serves a large population of cancer patients. Pharma partners supply drugs to test and to find "setbacks" in drug resistance. The lab also does resistance testing and studies the immune system's response to therapy.
- **Scientific focus:** Mostly whole exome sequencing (reading only the protein-coding parts of the genome), with some whole genome sequencing. The reasoning: exons produce proteins, proteins are what targeted therapies and immunotherapies act on, so deep exome sequencing on NextSeq/NovaSeq suits their work. They search for predictive biomarkers, response to therapy and pathway biology.
- **Generations of sequencing (expert's framing):** First generation is Sanger; second generation (NGS) is Illumina, Ion Torrent and SOLiD (short reads); third generation is PacBio and Nanopore (long reads, useful for long non-coding regions).

## Drivers of the lab's sequencing volume (Q2 2026)

The expert names three things that set volume: ideas, funding and staff. Funding comes first because "sequencing... is very expensive."

- **Funding sources:** (1) internal seed funding from the institution, which the expert says is plentiful ("MD Anderson has a lot of money... it's in a good spot"); (2) external government grants, which are "a little bit more tricky because the politics is going towards less investment"; (3) pharmaceutical collaborations, which fund drug-related work because pharma is "driven by profit"; plus (4) philanthropy and donors.
- **Current position:** "We have an increase in funding", with new grants won for projects such as exomes. Staff availability and collaborations also support volume.
- **Cost of single-cell RNA sequencing (expert's example):**
  - Sample preparation with 10x Genomics (library formation, RNA/DNA/cDNA preparation): under $3,000 per sample.
  - Sequencing on Illumina: another under $3,000 per sample.
  - Total: under about $6,000 per sample. A project of four or five samples would be under $15,000 on the 10x Genomics side plus under $15,000 on the NovaSeq 6000, or under $30,000 in all. Bulk work costs "a little bit less".
- **Discovery then validation:** Sequencing is used to explore and discover; findings are then validated with proteomics (large-scale protein measurement) and other methods.
- **Funding mix:** Government funding is typically about one-third of a lab's funding, per the expert.

### Political and government funding uncertainty

- The expert says political uncertainty influenced "roughly, I would say, 20% of our sequencing volume", but it could be reduced "to 20% or even less" by shifting to other sources, since the funding system works like "an organic machine": when one source shrinks, others (seed money, philanthropy, pharma) expand. The interviewer summarised this as an initial 20% impact offset by seed funding, philanthropy and pharma.
- Timing: the shock "started kicking in" last year (2025). From this quarter (Q2 2026) the lab feels "more secure" and relies more on seed funding; the uncertainty "has been decreasing."
- Quote: "I would say the political uncertainty influences definitely quite a bit because even those 20% are still having a major blow." Researchers become "a little more careful, especially at the beginning."
- Risk framing: the expert says it is wise to diversify, since the uncertainty could shift to pharma or philanthropy; a lab relying on one or two sources faces high risk and needs "plan B, C".
- Opinion on the wider market: because of the uncertainty, academic researchers are "very, very careful" about spending, and the expert says Illumina products are "not essential... more of a privilege", used when money is available to expand and experiment.

## Sequencing output (gigabases of data) – Q2 2026 and full year

- **Q2 2026 vs Q2 2025:** Output on the lab's Illumina instruments is up about 10%. The driver is a recently received $2 million seed grant from CPRIT (the Cancer Prevention and Research Institute of Texas, named by the expert as "seed funding... a big funding"). It funds hiring and more sequencing reagents.
- **Versus expectations:** The expert first said output "is following the expectations", then "slightly exceeding" them, and later "exceeding our expectations by 10%" (an inconsistent statement; the interviewer recorded "slightly exceeding"). Expectations going in were "high and... positive", about the same 10% increase because of more grants. The expert stresses data quality over quantity.
- **Full-year 2026 vs 2025:** The expert forecast "10%-12%", then "10%-13%", and finally "10%-15%" ("20% is too much"). Reasons: more funding, and grant renewals that depend on publications. The expert says "There are a lot of Illumina projects at the moment" and "It's a very good moment for the lab."
- **Versus 90 days ago:** More optimistic. A recent publication reignited hope after the expert worried it would never be published and that grant renewals would be lost; before that, the expert thought "there wouldn't be no increase."
- **Publication cited:** An EMBO Journal paper (impact factor 8) on how CDK8 remodels the tumor microenvironment and promotes resistance to KRAS G12D inhibitors (a class of targeted cancer drugs). Another article is hoped for this year or next, which would feed grant renewals.
- **Risk to research direction:** The expert says the Mirati KRAS G12D drug (named "MRTX") "came out from the clinical trials" and is not helping pancreatic cancer patients, which "is actually not very positive for the type of research" they want to base on that project. If one project fails, the lab can shift to other topics (exomes, aging, smoking, diet). The expert also notes that field direction (targeted therapy, bispecific antibodies, immunotherapy, degraders versus inhibitors) shifts funding and therefore sequencing. This is the expert's reading, from what they "read on the internet", not direct knowledge of the trial.

## Consumables and reagents (kits needed for every run)

- **Spending trend, Q2 2026 vs Q2 2025:** Up 10%-15%, in proportion to rising sequencing volume. The expert compares consumables to "the oil and the gas of a car."
- **Cost per kit:** Illumina reagents "cost under $3,000, at least for the high throughputs" the lab uses (the 300-cycle and 150-cycle kits).
- **Wider spend that rises with volume:** RNA/DNA extraction, protein purification, cell culture media and fetal bovine serum (FBS, "very expensive"), cell counting supplies, electricity, 10x Genomics reagents and beads, cDNA and library preparation, plus sequencer service contracts and maintenance.
- **Volume, not price or stocking:** The expert says the increase is driven by volume. Prices are only mildly higher, and stocking is not an issue.
- **Stocking behavior:** The lab does not stockpile. Reagents have a shelf life under six months (sometimes up to a year), so the lab buys only what it will use. Expired reagents also void the Illumina warranty ("the warranty... covers only in case you're using something that is new, it has not been expired").
- **Delivery:** Illumina "has everything in stock", delivers "within a couple of days", and the expert believes it produces "somewhere in Dallas" and ships by FedEx (expert's belief). Current NextSeq 500/550 reagents need three temperature conditions (room temperature, cold, dry ice); the expert says newer Illumina kits no longer need dry ice.
- **Expert's view of Illumina pricing strategy (opinion):** Illumina "is not going to make a dramatic increase". It could even cut reagent prices because of new technology and Roche competition; even "a slight increase in price could cause a major setback". If it raises prices, perhaps 2%-3%.

## Pricing

- **Consumables price change in the past 90 days:** Mild. Reagents rise yearly with inflation; the expert says the increase is "very mild", "5% or something like that", and held down by the lab's relationship and collaborations with Illumina.
- **Discounts:** Illumina reagent specialists can give 8%-10% extra discount on request. MD Anderson has partnerships with most vendors and gets institutional discounts; the lab's own collaboration cuts prices further.
- **Price-lock pattern:** Representatives approve pricing, sometimes equal to the prior year, with incentives holding prices for about three months, after which prices rise "like 5%, 10%" (the expert's rough range), moderated by collaboration deals.
- **Equipment pricing environment (past 90 days):** "Pretty much the same." Illumina and competitors are "not very aggressive in concessions" and the expert is happy with pricing.

## Inventory and lead times

- **Inventory:** "Sufficient", the "sweet spot"; too much risks waste because of short shelf life. New quotes are requested when new experiments are planned.
- **Lead times (past 90 days):** Unchanged. The expert describes Illumina as "efficient, quick, accurate, precise, friendly, and honest" and says they never had a complaint.

## Customer support

Illumina staff visit, bring extra reagents, fix problems, sometimes send free reagent if a machine problem occurs, and give scientific consultation. The expert describes them as "educators as well", reasoning that Illumina's success depends on customers' publications.
- The expert calls Illumina "a company that is really positive" with an "educational purpose" and a "noble purpose".

## Competition and platform share

### Roche AXELIOS (a new sequencer from Roche)

- **Direct knowledge?** Mostly speculation from public marketing. The expert says "there is not enough data online to prove" the claims: "They just say what they want to say."
- **Described attributes:** More reliable, precise and clearer data; uses Xpandomer chemistry (the expert's description: encoding information into a high signal-to-noise reporter, with "clear signal and minimal background as they advertise"). The expert also says it "combines together the Nanopore technology with the Illumina sequencing by ligation technology" and has "the beauty of both worlds, the third generation, second generation" (the expert's characterization, offered as a view and not verified).
- **Threat level:** "Major to a medium." If data proves better than Illumina's, "people will prefer buying that." The expert thinks Roche "can take 20%, 30% of the market, which is already big", and that Illumina "can struggle if they don't have a backup plan." 20-30% of jobs "could be lost as well".
- **Expert's view on Illumina's response:** Illumina has bought a proteomics company and is expanding its proteomics portfolio as a "backup plan", so "they don't go bankrupt in case that happens, which is mildly possible. It's slightly possible" (opinion).
- **Competitive pressure now:** "It's creating a lot of pressure because people are very worried..." about Illumina's position. If data appear in big journals showing superiority, "Illumina is going to struggle big time"; Illumina "could go down in terms of shares, stocks, personnel, and funding."
- **Share outlook, next 12 months:** AXELIOS "will take a lot of market space", alongside Illumina's MiSeq i100.
- **Purchase intent:** The expert would buy AXELIOS "if it proves out to be good", as an additional instrument.

### Illumina MiSeq i100 Series

- Described as "very good", short-read, benchtop, low reagent use, no need for ice or dry ice shipping, suited to exome and large panel sequencing and small projects. The expert says it lacks depth and "cannot do 10x Genomics sequencing", so the NextSeq 500 and NovaSeq 6000 remain necessary for deep, high-quality sequencing. The expert expects it and AXELIOS to gain share while larger platforms "may face some pressure" (the interviewer's summary).

## Equipment purchasing plans

The expert cited specs ("accuracy of 30%-90%", "dark genome regions of 20,000 fewer errors") in a garbled way that cannot be reliably interpreted.

### Competitors

- Watching Roche AXELIOS and 10x Genomics' spatial products ("I like that a lot"). The expert says 10x Genomics is "pushing hard" in spatial to overcome competition.

## SomaLogic acquisition (Illumina's proteomics purchase)

- Effect on Illumina's value proposition: "no effect", possibly a slight decrease. The expert says SomaLogic is "not very famous in terms of publications" and sees the deal as a sign of weakness, a "plan B" because Illumina fears losing its place in sequencing if AXELIOS proves strong. Net effect "medium to depreciating the value of Illumina" (opinion).
- Effect on the expert's own Illumina usage: none.

## Future of genomic sequencing (expert's outlook)

- Sequencing will become "more precise, more refined, and accurate, quicker, and cheaper." The expert expects a combination of AI-driven analysis (to fix regions hard to see), better long reads and TruPath-type analyses, and AI to analyze the resulting data more thoroughly.
