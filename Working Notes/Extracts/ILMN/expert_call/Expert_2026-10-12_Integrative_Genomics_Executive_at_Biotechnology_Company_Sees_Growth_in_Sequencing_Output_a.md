# Channel check with a California biotech Director of Integrative Genomics (Illumina customer): sequencing volume up 20-25%, Ultima cost pressure, Roche AXELIOS one seen as niche

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-12_Integrative_Genomics_Executive_at_Biotechnology_Company_Sees_Growth_in_Sequencing_Output_a.md` · Date: 2026-10-12 · Channel check on an expert-network platform (AI interviewer; "Channel Check Grid"), interview 12 Oct 2026, published 14 Oct 2026; expert is a customer, Director of Integrative Genomics at a biotechnology company in California (unnamed) · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer who runs internal R&D sequencing (research-use-only) at a California biotech says their Q3 2026 output beat expectations and grew about 20-25% year over year, driven by more samples from single-cell and other high-throughput projects. Consumable spend rose about 20%, entirely from volume, with Illumina pricing flat to slightly lower thanks to discounts. The expert sees Ultima's roughly one-third list-price cost as the main share threat to Illumina, views Roche's AXELIOS one, TruPath and SomaLogic as interesting but niche, and has no instrument purchases planned.

# Expert background and how to weigh the evidence

**Who the expert is.** The expert is a Director of Integrative Genomics at a biotechnology company in California, with visibility into genomics R&D and sequencing operations. The platform labels the perspective "Customer" (a buyer of Illumina instruments and consumables, the reagent kits needed for each run). The interviewer was an AI, not a human analyst. No years of experience or tenure are given. The experience is current: the expert speaks about Q3 2026 in the interview of 12 Oct 2026. [Expert: Director, Integrative Genomics, California biotech, Oct 2026]

**Lab setting.** The lab serves only internal R&D groups and scientists in preclinical research and development. It operates under research-use-only conditions (not a CLIA-certified clinical lab; CLIA is the US clinical laboratory certification). Funding is 100% from the company's own budget, with no federal grants, institutional or philanthropic money, or external biopharma customers.

**Scale.** The expert first said output is "probably at the terabyte level" per year. When pressed for gigabases (Gb, units of sequence data), they said about 500 Gb per run and about 200-300 runs. The AI interviewer converted this to roughly 20,000-150,000 Gb per year; this conversion is the interviewer's, not the expert's, and the arithmetic of 500 Gb × 200-300 runs would be about 100,000-150,000 Gb.

**Direct experience versus speculation.** Most statements are first-hand: the lab's own volumes, spend, discounts, support experience and fleet. Views on Roche's AXELIOS one, TruPath and SomaLogic are opinions from a non-user. The expert has not adopted any of them. The expert explicitly did no break-even calculation for AXELIOS one and did not ask about instrument discounts in Q3. One caveat: the expert described some of their own statements loosely and asked the interviewer to clarify several questions, so some figures are approximate.

# Sequencing fleet and workload mix

**Instruments in use.**
- **Illumina short-read, in-house:** NextSeq 2000 and MiSeq (the expert operates other sites with a NextSeq 2000 and MiSeq).
- **Illumina NovaSeq X, via CRO:** very large-scale short-read work goes to a contract research organization (CRO, an outside lab paid per project) running NovaSeq X, rather than an owned instrument.
- **Oxford Nanopore (ONT) long-read:** a P2 Solo at the expert's site, with other sites having a P2 and a P24. The expert also mentioned a PromethION. Nanopore runs can yield thousands of Gb per run, per the expert.
- The expert said that outside of these, they have no instrument to support other needs.

**Short-read versus long-read split.** About 90% of output is short-read and 10% is ONT. The expert noted that ONT is not used only for long reads, but also for epigenetic (chemical modifications on DNA) work, and the company does not think of Nanopore as only a long-read platform.

**Outlook for the split.** The expert expects it to stay about the same over the next 12 months. Reasons given:
- Allocation is driven by project purpose.
- Nanopore library prep (sample preparation before sequencing) is less streamlined for high-throughput work than Illumina's.
- Much accumulated historical data is short-read, which is useful for comparison with databases and past data, and integrating old and new data is not easy.
- Analysis pipelines for Nanopore are streamlined to some degree, but the history is short-read.
- A change would depend on cost, ease of use, bandwidth, and project demands that require one platform over the other.

# Demand, drivers and constraints

**Tailwinds (positive).** More projects, many of them single-cell (analyzing individual cells), and a faster pace of research within the company.

**Headwinds (negative).**
- Budget constraints on upfront library-prep costs.
- Staffing: higher-throughput projects need much more library prep, and the full-time employees (FTEs) doing the work lack bandwidth to prepare samples ahead of time.
- Upfront budget can negatively affect sequencing volume.
- The expert said the main factors were covered by these.

## Q3 2026 and growth figures

The expert gave the following volume figures for the lab's sequencing output. They say the growth comes from more samples being sequenced, not from deeper sequencing per sample.

| Period | Comparison | Expert's figure |
|---|---|---|
| Q3 2026 | versus expectations | Exceeded expectations |
| Q3 2026 | versus Q3 2025 | Up about 20-25% |
| Full-year 2026 | versus 2025 | Up about 20-25% (already increased; based on current projects and pipeline) |
| 2027 | versus 2026 | Up about 10-15% (later stated as "additional 10%") |

**Why Q3 beat expectations.** More high-throughput sequencing, including single-cell RNA-seq (measuring gene activity in single cells). Efforts to make single-cell assays more cost-effective mean people use them more. Although reagent cost per cell may have fallen, it leads to many more sequencing demands. The expert called it project demand and project-based assessment.

**Confidence for 2026.** Q3 made the expert more optimistic, because "data generated in Q3 is the basis of the data that will generate in Q4"; work is project-based but builds on prior data, so Q4 should look like Q3.

**2027 view.** 2026 came in higher than the expert expected (they had expected it to stay flat from 2025). From the higher 2026 base the expert expects 2027 to be at least flat and probably up an additional 10-15%. The AI interviewer interpreted this as deceleration; the expert corrected that this is continued growth on a higher base, not a slowdown. Driver: assay tools are getting more cost-effective, so as sequencing and upfront reagent costs (single-cell and other modalities) fall, researchers plan more experiments within the same budget. The expert's logic: "Even though the sequencing cost might come down, you still see the growth in overall about 10%."

**Mix shift toward high-throughput instruments.** The amount of sequencing run on high-throughput platforms has increased, and the mix may continue to shift toward high-throughput sequencers, even within short-read, driven by high-throughput assays and single-cell analysis.

# Competitive landscape

## Short-read share (the expert's R&D support work)

The expert's estimate of the split is loose and changed during the answer: first "Illumina probably 70%, Ultima 30%," then "80/20 split between Illumina and Ultima Genomics, if everything goes well," then "Ultima, maybe 15%-20%, and then maybe 80%-85% Illumina." The expert was "not quite sure about Roche element" (the interviewer listed Roche and Element Biosciences), and said it would be a very small fraction of the field. [Expert, Oct 2026]

The driver is the cost of very large sequencing projects: Ultima's per-run cost to generate the same amount of data is "about 1/3 of the Illumina sequencing cost in list price, at least." This is a claim from the expert, who is not described as an Ultima user in the fleet description (the fleet answer lists no Ultima instrument), so the share figures appear to be forward-looking or via outside providers; the expert did not clarify.

**Next 12 months.** If the library types the lab wants to sequence work well on Ultima, there could be a shift toward Ultima because of competitive pricing. They will not shift immediately before due diligence and their own benchmarking: "No matter who does it, everyone needs their own additional benchmarking." Low-cost sequencing at the same quality will become "more competitively attractive." Vendor share did not change in Q3 2026 and is about the same as in the same period of 2025.

## Long-read share

About 98% ONT and 2% other long-read platforms. Reasons: ONT has capabilities beyond long reads (assays not available on other platforms), ease of library prep, instruments already on site, and acceptable cost although it "could be pricey, depending on the project." The expert has not explored other platforms intensively.

## Roche AXELIOS one

- **Adoption:** not adopted.
- **View:** "a very interesting sequencer technologically" and scientifically appealing. The instrument cost was "lower than expected." But the upfront capital expenditure (CapEx) is "not a minuscule thing," and for their R&D support work it "does not warrant the upfront investment."
- **Reusable sensor model:** very interesting and economically useful, but to justify it the investment has to break even, and the expert has not run that calculation for their organization. It could be a significant change depending on an organization's capacity, but currently it is not an easy investment to justify.
- **Threat to Illumina:** AXELIOS one offers versatility, very fast independent runs and flexible configurations. The expert said Illumina's sequencers are "locked in a very specific way that only can do very specific configuration," whereas Roche has more built-in flexibility.
- **Magnitude:** "a little bit of niche challenge at this stage." Reasons: upfront cost in the current economy; first-generation instruments are hard to adopt; and historical data must be re-evaluated for any new sequencer, which is a large hurdle.

# Illumina products and roadmap

## NovaSeq X

- **Adoption:** not owned; used through a CRO. The expert's work benefits from high-throughput capacity, but they described NovaSeq X as "limiting to short-read sequencing at the moment."
- **Why not buy:** CRO access is relatively cost-effective, and a capital outlay of over $1 million has to be complementary rather than simply additive throughput. "Simply adding the throughput might not be enough for us to do the investment of that scale."
- **Roadmap risk:** Illumina historically updates instruments on a cycle, and NovaSeq X "has been around." Despite announced roadmaps, "it's really hard to imagine what's next, and there is a little risk that the field is always feeling."
- **Announced upgrades (interviewer's premise):** the interviewer said Illumina committed to raising NovaSeq X flow cell output by up to 40% and speeding whole-genome sequencing by about 30%. The expert replied that higher throughput is not the decision point unless a company's priority is producing data quickly. Lower per-base cost is appealing, but a bigger flow cell can be hard to fill and can slow turnaround or be wasteful on smaller projects. Higher data volume also requires changes to infrastructure for data ingestion and analysis, which are "really not discussed" when talking about throughput expansion.

## TruPath Genome

The interviewer said Illumina launched it in February 2026, removing library prep from the whole-genome workflow at a list price of about $395 per sample. The expert asked whether that includes sequencing cost; the interviewer did not know.

- **Expert's view:** a "very cool technology" and a "very good technology"; removing library prep is appealing. But it is a niche for their organization, which would not need it regularly at scale. It could be appealing for human genetics or fast whole-genome work in hospital clinical settings. It does not shift anything the lab does in the foreseeable future.
- **Effect on long-read work:** the expert did not see a strong pull from long-read to TruPath. They said long-read whole-genome sequencing is "always better than short-read, so there is no doubt on that," including for accuracy. Long-read adoption has grown and costs have fallen, and many people want multi-omic approaches (several data types in one run). Which long-read type shifts the field would need deeper data.
- **Added point:** TruPath is, as the expert understood it, limited to the NovaSeq X/NovaSeq platform, which restricts access, and TruPath alone would not warrant a $1 million capital purchase.

## SomaLogic

The interviewer said Illumina closed the SomaLogic acquisition in January 2026. The expert said it has not shifted work to Illumina "at the moment." SomaLogic's aptamer-based proteomics platform (measuring proteins) "sounds very interesting and we definitely would like to try that." But it is "not as clear or advertised," and the expert is unsure how successful adoption has been. It adds value because the field is moving to layering proteomics on sequencing data, but for everyday projects it is also a niche. "The accessibility is not as widely known as what people might think, but it could be definitely a useful and added value to Illumina."

# Instrument purchases and capital spending

- **Plans for next 12 months:** no purchases planned, and "a little early" to say given company timelines. Increasing in-house throughput is "not a good justification enough" to buy another instrument, with CRO access reducing urgency.
- **Q3 2026:** plans are on track (not accelerated, delayed or cancelled). Instead of adding sequencing capacity, the lab is adding capacity to keep up with upfront library prep and sample prep.
- **2026 capital spending on sequencing instruments versus 2025:** flat.
- **Instrument discounts/incentives in Q3:** no answer, because the expert did not inquire.

# Consumables, pricing and supply

**Spend.** The lab's consumables and reagent spending in Q3 2026 rose about 20% year over year. The expert attributes it to volume, not price: they obtained some extra discount on Illumina consumables yet spent more, "which only means its volume has increased." It was purely organic demand growth, with no stocking or destocking effect.

**Unit prices, 2026 versus 2025.** Hard to generalize. Frequently used Illumina consumables had deeper discounts, so they are slightly lower or the same as in 2025. Other consumables are about the same or slightly higher.

**Discounts in Q3.** Unchanged versus the prior quarter, because the discount was negotiated before Q3.

**Inventory.** "Sufficient," not excessive; the lab avoids stocking too much because project demands change and it does not want wasted resources.

**Lead times.** Unchanged in Q3.

# Vendor support

- Support from all vendors has been "great," and other vendors provide help as needed "without any reservations."
- Illumina has been "steadily a good partner" on sales and technical support.
- The lab generally does not get bioinformatics help except when onboarding or evaluating a new platform. On Illumina's DRAGEN (its software for analyzing sequencing data), the efficiency or knowledge on the few occasions they used that help "was not really up to our expectations."
- Support levels were unchanged in Q3 versus the prior quarter.

# Emerging technologies and closing views

- Sequencing technology has been "stable"; the expert values layered, multi-omic information and sequencing flexibility, and said many vendors have exciting roadmaps.
- The expert flagged spatial and in situ sequencing (reading sequences within tissue, preserving location) as "coming stronger." It may not look like a competitor to conventional sequencing, but the expert can see applications that are comparable, and said in situ technology from other companies could be important to the field.
- The expert called the sequencing market "very competitive" and an exciting time for the last few years and likely the next few. There are many niche demands, and first-time buyers have a lot to weigh.
- Switching cost: many facilities already own well-established sequencers, and "It's not as easy as what people might think to change, unless you have a budget to add." The expert expects new platforms to be complementary rather than complete replacements "until the other technology matures." (The interviewer summarized this as switching costs and installed-base inertia.)

# Key takeaways for Illumina (as stated by the expert)

1. Demand is strong at this customer: Q3 2026 volume and consumables spend both up roughly 20% year over year, and 2027 expected up a further 10-15%, with cost declines per experiment expected to expand rather than shrink volume.
2. Pricing is stable to slightly down for Illumina consumables through discounts; no price increases cited.
3. Ultima's list-price cost advantage (about one-third, per the expert) is the clearest share risk, but the expert would require benchmarking first and had seen no share change as of Q3 2026.
4. Roche's AXELIOS one, TruPath and SomaLogic are seen as appealing but niche for this lab, with historical data compatibility, capital cost and workflow inertia limiting switching.
5. No capital purchases are planned, so this customer's spending is on consumables and outsourced (CRO) NovaSeq X capacity rather than new instruments.
