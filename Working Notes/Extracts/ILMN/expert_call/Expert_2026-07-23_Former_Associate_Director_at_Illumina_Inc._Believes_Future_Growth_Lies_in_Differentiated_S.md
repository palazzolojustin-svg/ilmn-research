# Expert call with former Illumina Associate Director of Product Management on sequencing commoditization, pricing pressure, competition (Roche AXELIOS, Element, Ultima, MGI/BGI), reagent rental, and the pivot to sample prep and spatial products

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-23_Former_Associate_Director_at_Illumina_Inc._Believes_Future_Growth_Lies_in_Differentiated_S.md` · Date: 2026-07-23 · AlphaSense Expert Insights (Company Deep-Dive); former Illumina Associate Director, Product Management, Multi-omic and Multi-modal Assays (left July 2025); interviewed by a buy-side client; published 2026-08-06 · expert call*

**Summary.** A former Illumina product and strategy manager explains why Illumina's core sequencing consumables (the reagent kits needed for every run) have been flat since about 2022: price per unit of output now falls as fast as customer volume rises, because competitors are eroding pricing power and sequencing is becoming a commodity. They say flat sequencing consumables would be "a huge win" and that growth must come from differentiated sample prep, spatial and single-cell assays, and an "ecosystem" lock-in strategy. They also give views on Roche's AXELIOS (slow burn, roughly 10-15% of 2027 new instrument purchases at best), 10x's Atera (cautiously pessimistic), long-read sequencing (too niche) and reagent rental (now a necessity).

# Expert call notes: Former Illumina Associate Director on sequencing commoditization and the pivot to sample prep and spatial

- **Who:** A former Associate Director of Product Management for Multi-omic and Multi-modal Assays at Illumina. They left in July 2025, so their direct experience is about a year old at the interview (23 Jul 2026; published 6 Aug 2026). They now do consulting work in the same industry. [Expert: former Illumina associate director, Jul 2026]
- **Experience:** Over 15 years in biotech, 6 in genomics and 4 at Illumina. Their roles covered R&D, market development, corporate strategy and product management. Before Illumina they worked at Roche Tissue Diagnostics.
- **Three Illumina roles:**
  1. Market development, joining around COVID times. They focused on infectious disease assays and on growing Illumina's penetration in the infectious disease market globally, including Africa and other underserved countries.
  2. Corporate strategy, focused on competitive intelligence. This was when new sequencing competitors such as Element and Singular were coming online and the team was being expanded. The work covered the genomics competitive landscape, including all 10x products.
  3. Product management on Illumina's spatial product, called "Illumina Spatial" for a long time and, per the expert, "just launched last month" (June 2026) as **StrataMap**. They led a team developing new products for the spatial and single-cell platform and worked with corporate strategy on next-generation sequencing (NGS) and the competitive picture.
- **Seniority:** Reported to the VP of Product Management, two levels below the C-suite.
- **Competitors they can speak to:** Roche, MGI, BGI, Element, Singular, Ultima and ONT (Oxford Nanopore).
- **Direct experience versus speculation:** Pricing negotiations, customer behavior, the history of product cycles and Illumina's strategy are direct experience, up to mid-2025. ## Core thesis: why sequencing consumables are flat

### The consumables attach rate and the "push-pull"
The client modeled the **consumables attach rate** (consumable dollars generated per installed instrument). They noted it declines almost every year, a modest decline each time. They asked how to think about volume growth versus effective price decreases.

The expert's explanation:
- **The hypergrowth era:** New instruments cut the cost of sequencing and raised output, and appetite was so large that customers sequenced far more. Their illustrative example: an instrument released in 2010 halves the price, and customers sequence 4X more. The attach rate rose.
- **The change with NovaSeq:** With the introduction of the NovaSeq platforms, customers still sequenced more, but price drops were so aggressive that volume did not fully make up for them. Illustrative example: price drops by half, but sequencing intensity rises only about 25%.
- **Cause (expert's view):** Competition. BGI and MGI globally (especially in Asia, but also Europe), then Singular, Element and Ultima, which "started to come online in 2021, 2022." Illumina "just didn't have the ability to keep the price premium high." Margins came down.
- **Result:** This was "one of the big factors" behind Illumina's "real flat growth starting in about 2022 and to the present day." The price decrease no longer causes the acceleration in adoption and sequencing intensity seen in the past.

### Worked example of past step changes (NextSeq to NovaSeq 6000)
- The NovaSeq 6000 (the expert thinks released about 2018) raised output per instrument from roughly 400-500 million reads on the NextSeq 500 to about 5 billion and ultimately about 10 billion. That is a 10- to 20-fold increase in reads per run, with the same upfront workflow and less cost per read.
- Price per read fell from about $20 per gigabase (Gb, a unit of sequence output) to about $5, a roughly four-fold decrease. The expert apologized for mixing metrics and said these are order-of-magnitude figures.
- Customers responded by sequencing 5X, 6X, 7X or even 10X more, from pent-up demand. The expert compared this to the semiconductor industry, where memory density keeps rising and you "fit more reads into the same size space."

### Cadence of product generations
- Historically a major high-throughput launch came every five to six years, later corrected to five to seven. Each launch brought a step change, and "Illumina was practically defining the market and what was possible."
- **NovaSeq X** (current high-throughput series, released 2022 per the expert) was about to launch when the expert joined corporate strategy. It "seemed like there just wasn't quite the same pent-up demand." The expert recalled investment firms at the time commenting that "maybe the world just wasn't quite ready for this instrument" and that Illumina should perhaps have waited a few more years. Future cadence is uncertain ("who knows how that's going to change?"). The client wondered whether it might lengthen.

### New lever: bigger flow cells instead of new instruments
- Sometimes a new instrument is needed to lower price and raise output (NextSeq to NovaSeq, NovaSeq 6000 to X). The expert notes Illumina now has other levers, in particular increasing output through new consumables.
- NovaSeq X launched with 1.5B, 10B and 25B flow cells (billions of reads). Illumina is expanding the line with a **5B** and a **35B**. The expert believes the 35B (up from 25 billion reads) was officially announced in June 2026 and had been teased at conferences since early 2025. They don't know of a special product name.
- **Expert's inference on strategy:** Perhaps the world is not ready to buy another $1 million-$1.5 million instrument, but may accept another consumables option.
- **Price effect:** Cost of about $2.50 per Gb may drop to about $2 flat with the 35B, roughly a 20% price drop. This is much smaller than the roughly 4X price change of the NextSeq-to-NovaSeq era. "TBD" on whether it spurs demand. The expert thinks a 20% drop will probably not spur huge demand, but may help Illumina keep customers from switching to competitors by giving flow-cell flexibility, which is "more of an incremental thing versus a real game-changing step change."

### Why further price cuts matter less: the "full workflow" argument
- The workflow has three cost components: **sample prep** (preparing the material for sequencing), **sequencing**, and **data processing** (analysis, storage). The cost split is called the "customer wallet," and each company's slice is its "share of wallet."
- Historically, 20-25 years ago, sequencing was upwards of 90% of the wallet. Sample prep and data processing have stayed roughly flat in cost, and data processing can even rise as datasets and storage grow. Only sequencing keeps getting cheaper.
- Illustration: cutting sequencing from $1,000 to $100 per sample is a huge cut in total sample cost. Cutting from $100 to $80 is a 20% sequencing cut but perhaps only a single-digit percent change in total sample cost. Dropping sequencing cost has "a lower and lower impact on customer decision-making."
- The client calculated that for a clinical lab the new flow cell lowers effective total cost by only about 5%, and the expert agreed ("Right").
- Historically sequencing cost for a genome fell from about $1,000-$1,200 to $200-$300, which "is going to spur massive demand."

### Cost per genome benchmarks (expert's figures)
| Component | Expert's estimate |
|---|---|
| Whole genome at 30X depth | About 100 Gb of data. Companies compete on "the $100 genome" or "the $80 genome." |
| Sequencing on NovaSeq X, highest-output flow cell (not the new higher-output one), list price, fully loaded | About $250 per genome |
| Genome sample prep at scale | Under $100 per sample, possibly lower for very high-output labs. It is standardized and cheap. |
| Data processing, clinical (e.g., pairing with SOPHiA GENETICS or PierianDx, with pre-existing libraries and sophisticated or AI models) | About $500-$1,000 per sample, with some at $500-$700 or more |
| Data processing, research (e.g., bioinformatics facilities at the Broad or Allen Institute) | About $50-$200 per sample depending on scale, tens of dollars plus minimal storage at the low end |

Note: the expert at one point described the sequencing figures as per-genome costs and said the "$2.50 to $2" figures relate to a genome cost in the same framing; the per-Gb and per-genome numbers are loosely used in the call.

### Net effect today: price decline offsets volume growth
- The expert says: "Unfortunately for Illumina, the price decrease is keeping up with demand."
- Their rough math: NovaSeq X was released in 2022, and many NovaSeq 6000s remain in the market, with high-throughput customers slowly switching. The blended consumables cost has fallen from about $5 per Gb (NovaSeq 6000) toward about $2.50. From 2022 to today, they estimate roughly a **30%-40% decrease in blended consumables price** for high-throughput, which offsets demand growth. The expert said they had not thought about it this way before and was computing on the spot.

### Outlook for sequencing consumables
- Expert's view of what would be needed for a step change: because we are already at a $100-$200 genome, price would need to fall to **$10-$50** per genome. In their opinion Illumina "does not have the appetite to do that."
- Reasoning: "Sequencing companies shouldn't really want the cost of sequencing to go down too fast, because then you're just speedrunning sequencing's path to commoditization." The expert says we are close to commodity but "maybe we're not there quite yet."
- "For me, things are starting to feel like the sequencer is almost like a commodity. There's no more step change, there's no more explosive growth coming from that end." Value is in "what you surround it with," such as data analysis and unique sample prep.
- Asked where the sequencing-consumables revenue stream (the client said roughly 60%-70% of revenue) grows over the next few years: "Honestly, I would be surprised if it grew. I think flat would be a huge win."
- The client then clarified that this concerned the core sequencing consumables ("the 80%"). The expert said their comment applies to that core, but the pre-sequencing consumables (library and sample prep) are where growth lies (below).

## Growth opportunity: differentiated sample prep, single cell and spatial

### Where it appears in reported segments
The client listed the reporting lines (sequencing consumables, instruments, service; microarray consumables, instruments, service). The expert said library prep and sample prep products fall under **sequencing consumables**.

### History and rationale
- Until about two years ago, Illumina's sample/library prep offering was basic: DNA prep, RNA prep, and a basic research-use-only (RUO, not for diagnostic use) cancer screening test. The market was hyper-competitive and the products were not differentiated.
- Customers bought them mainly because staying in one ecosystem was easier (validated, one service provider). Many customers instead preferred premium products from other brands. "Illumina really never had much differentiation on the sample prep side."
- **Strategic decision around 2021:** With "the great patent cliffs" coming for NGS technologies and private companies raising funds to attack Illumina on sequencing, Illumina made a conscious decision to invest much more in sample prep.
- **New products named by the expert:**
  - **Five-base:** a DNA methylation assay, "highly differentiated from what else is on the market."
  - **A spatial assay that just launched** (StrataMap, launched June 2026).
  - **Fluent BioSciences:** a single-cell company that Illumina bought, with the deal closing in early 2025 ("I think").
  - A "pseudo-long-read technology" (not discussed further).
- **Share-of-wallet logic:** In a single-cell workflow where a researcher uses a 10x Genomics assay, sequences on Illumina and uses 10x's data analysis platform, the share of that assay's spend going to Illumina was about 25%. Illumina decided to "get in on that game." These assay markets are growing at "a fairly high cadence."
- **Growth potential:** "Potentially explosive growth opportunity" if the products are highly differentiated. The expert says Illumina has communicated to investors for the last couple of years potentially **hundreds of millions of dollars added to the top line over about five years** (the expert's recollection of company messaging, not a figure they verified). The products are only now being released.

## Spatial biology: how it works and the competitive picture

### Two approaches to spatial (measuring gene activity while preserving where in the tissue it occurs)
1. **Imaging-based:** Big instruments, including 10x's Xenium and Atera, NanoString CosMx (NanoString now owned by Bruker), Vizgen MERFISH, and others. You buy an instrument, put slides on it, it images them and you read the result. No sequencing is involved. It is complicated and expensive, and the assay is enclosed, so there is no mixing of companies.
2. **Sequencing-based:** A slide (like a microscope slide) with a pre-populated grid of barcoded oligonucleotides (short DNA strands) whose positions are known, like a map. Tissue is laid on top, RNA hybridizes (binds) to the barcodes and is pulled off. Sequencing the barcodes maps each RNA back to its location. You pay for the barcoded consumable, the sequencing and the data analysis.

- **10x Genomics has both:** Visium and Visium HD are sequencing-based (customers sequence on Illumina, and only a small share of wallet goes to Illumina). Xenium and Atera are imaging-based, where 10x keeps all the revenue, which 10x prefers.
- **Illumina's approach:** Always develops products that drive more sequencing, so it takes the sequencing-based path. In the client's description, Illumina spatial would be "Illumina all the way," with 10x "completely disintegrated from this workflow." The expert clarified it is not an instrument but a consumable, and the customer buys every product along the way from Illumina, so all revenue goes to Illumina.
- Sequencing-based alternatives historically: 10x Visium was effectively the main option for high-plex (many genes at once), unbiased discovery of the whole coding transcriptome. Curio Bioscience had a product that "never really caught on." MGI has one but struggles to enter developed markets because of its China ties.

### Illumina spatial revenue exposure (ballpark)
- The expert cites analyst reports estimating 10x Visium consumables revenue at maybe **eight figures annually** (tens of millions of dollars). The sequencing portion is less than half of the experiment's cost, so Illumina's current revenue from sequencing-based spatial assays is "not going to be more than maybe low eight figures." This excludes the new Illumina product, which has not yet hit the market. [Secondhand estimate based on analyst reports]

### 10x Atera
- Atera is an imaging-based instrument that can read the same roughly **18,000 coding RNAs** (the whole coding transcriptome) with imaging, which is more familiar to spatial researchers. Sequencing-based outputs look like grids ("an eight-bit video game rendering"). Atera/Xenium images are "absolutely beautiful," like city lights, with each dot a biomarker.
- **Expert's skepticism:**
  - The whole-transcriptome capability is not new: NanoString (now Bruker) CosMx has always been able to do the whole transcriptome even though its flagship is 5,000-plex. The expert has heard it "is not great," with many issues from jamming so many biomarkers into a small space, which may explain limited adoption.
  - 10x has not released chemistry details. The expert is "cautiously pessimistic" that there will be trade-offs and that Atera will not be "the magic bullet," and that sequencing-based approaches may give better results for researchers wanting that many biomarkers.
  - **Instrument fatigue:** There are "so many damn instruments" across spatial, sequencing, single-cell, live-cell imaging and proteomics. Atera costs about **$500,000**, and many customers already bought Xenium, CosMx, Vizgen or Akoya. Budgets are "super tight." It is faster and cheaper per sample, but the upfront cost is hard to justify.
  - **Illumina's advantage:** The Illumina spatial product is sequencing-based with trade-offs, but customers can just buy the substrate, run as few as four samples and sequence on an existing Illumina instrument, with no new CapEx (capital expenditure).

## Pricing and contracts

### Large research accounts (e.g., the Broad Institute)
- The Broad has "10+ high-throughput Illumina sequencers" and runs massive volumes. The expert says the discounts Broad negotiates are "insane" and declined to share prices. Contracts are annual or biannual and "heavily guided by competition."
- Ultima Genomics, with its factory-based high-throughput capability and fast growth, is aggressively pursuing such accounts and gives the Broad "crazy pricing." The Broad then goes back to Illumina to negotiate.
- Many such customers exist worldwide; the Broad is one of the biggest.

### Core labs and small labs
- Core labs at academic universities have similar competition-driven conversations with less power. They also hold contracts.
- Expert: those contracts "can very quickly become null and void if another company comes in and says, 'Hey, we can undercut Illumina by 25%, 50%.'" The expert saw this happen a number of times. Illumina scrambled and either price matched or not, depending on whether it would set a bad precedent.
- The risk: matching every competitor is "a pure race to the bottom." Even a small lab talks to peers at conferences, and word spreads. The expert's phrasing: "all of a sudden, Illumina's at a 20% margin and the wheels are falling off."

### Clinical customers
- The client said clinical is about 50% of the business. The expert's examples of clinical customers: Natera, historically Roche, Foundation Medicine, and (per the client's example) Guardant, Exact. They run large sample volumes for diagnostics, such as MRD (minimal residual disease, a test for leftover cancer) or early cancer screening tests, often charging several thousand dollars per test. "Very lucrative business for Illumina" because it is "growing like crazy" with potential to keep growing at a high rate.
- Almost all have their own sample prep and run it on a sequencer, today Illumina. Most of their enterprise value is tied up in IP around their assays.
- **Pricing pressure:** Because of Element, Ultima and now Roche, it is harder to command a price premium, and "they're seeing quite a bit of price erosion in there as well." The expert says that what applied to the Broad applies to clinical customers. The sequencer is not that important to the assay output anymore.
- **Switching:** All competitors have high-quality products, and there is "a lot of friction to switching," but switching could make sense for a company with high enough volumes and enough cash.
- **Roche as a customer:** One of the biggest within clinical ("Top 10, for sure"). Overall the expert is unsure it was top 10 but says it was large. Illumina's customer base has a very long tail, with the top 10 or 20 customers making up a very large proportion of the business.

## Reagent rental
- A reagent rental model: the customer signs a multi-year contract committing to purchase a set quantity of reagents and consumables, and gets the instrument placed without paying instrument revenue up front.
- **Expert's knowledge:** Illumina had never done reagent rental while they were there. Their previous employer, Roche Tissue Diagnostics, did it for tissue stainers. The expert is not sure whether Illumina has started this year ("It very well could be starting now").
- **Why it was resisted:** Instrument sales would suffer, creating a push-pull inside the business.
- **Rationale:** Guaranteed revenue (e.g., a three-year rental guarantees reagent pull-through). Instrument margin is lower anyway. Instruments are so expensive that placing them can be a hard conversation.
- **Expert's opinion:** For Illumina it has "become a necessity." Competitors are private, not yet in "forced profitability mode," burning cash and "giving instruments away" with heavy discounts. Illumina would never discount a NovaSeq to 90%, but "Element and Ultima, they will absolutely do that." Reagent rental would let Illumina place more instruments while guaranteeing consumable pull-through. "I'm really glad to hear that they're starting to do it."

## Competition

### Roche AXELIOS
- The expert is unsure of its impact. Many expect Roche, a clinical company, to go into the clinic with it. The expert's view: it is "not ready." By modern standards it "isn't a very good sequencer." It is not much cheaper per base: perhaps cheaper than Illumina, but "not all that much," or not even cheaper than many competitors.
- Because quality is lower, it suits niche applications that do not need very high-quality sequencing, such as single-cell, spatial and RNA sequencing. It will take a while to reach the clinic and adoption will be low. It "feels like the first generation of a product that maybe would've been competitive 10 years ago." The expert sees it as "a slow burn."
- "Illumina's definitely quite afraid of it, but I would be absolutely shocked if they were losing accounts to it." Some larger customers may buy it anyway, but a new sequencing lab would be unlikely to adopt AXELIOS as its platform in its current generation.
- **Technology:** Per the client, first entry in 2027 is academic and research. The expert describes a different type of sequencing, "sequencing by expansion," with very complex chemistry (the expert has an organic chemistry background), and says it is nanopore-based. There is room to improve: chemistry, enzymes, semiconductor-style tighter tolerances, more pores per consumable. They suspect Roche is working on a revamped assay, potentially keeping the same instrument (though they also said it may be "potentially a new instrument"), to raise quality and output and cut cost for high-volume clinical customers.
- **The scary scenario:** A clinically viable Roche instrument. Roche is "so well-respected in the clinical space" that labs may say "Roche knows what they're doing," especially in an upgrade cycle. The quality must be there.
- **Share estimate:** Asked, if the industry buys 100 instruments in 2027 (50 clinical, 50 academic), how many would be AXELIOS: "Maybe 10, 15 tops," preferring 10, because there are many proven platforms competing, not just Illumina. The client summarized this as 10%-15% share of new purchases in year one, and the expert agreed.

### Other competitors
- BGI and MGI (strong in Asia, also Europe, but with difficulty in developed markets because of China ties), Element, Singular and Ultima are named repeatedly as eroding pricing power. Ultima is described as aggressive on large accounts such as the Broad. Element and Ultima are described as willing to discount instruments heavily.

### Long-read sequencing (PacBio, Oxford Nanopore)
- **Why there was excitement:** Long reads can read through long repeat regions (hundreds or thousands of bases of repeated sequence) where short reads are "completely blind," allowing nearly continuous complete genomes, which the expert says short reads could never do. PacBio and (they think) ONT have published complete genomes. This was pitched as the next level of information for certain diseases. ONT also offers speed, small size (plugs into a laptop via USB), easy sample prep, low purchase cost and reads out to about one million bases, which excited people during the COVID era for in-field screening.
- **Why it did not pan out:** Promised discoveries didn't happen, and the clinical market stayed with tried-and-true short reads, which are "cheaper, faster, easier." Long reads remain "a fairly niche area," "probably a little too niche to support an independent company." The expert says they are "not failed by any means, but just the magic that they've promised just hasn't come to fruition."

## Illumina's strategic response: the ecosystem

- Asked how Illumina will respond to Roche, the expert says "you're already seeing it." Two parts:
  1. **Price/output:** The move from the 25B to the 35B NovaSeq X flow cell "is going to be a pricing decrease. They're definitely going to do that."
  2. **Ecosystem:** Historically Illumina was "incredibly tight-lipped" on product launches, "probably to a fault," often revealing an assay only a month before release, which frustrated R&D scientists. A couple of years ago, **Steve Barnard, the CTO,** gave "an insane talk" at a major conference disclosing a two-to-three year roadmap of new assays.
- Illumina is positioning itself as a "broad multi-omic ecosystem" with spatial, single-cell, DNA methylation and a pseudo-long-read technology. These products must be sequenced on Illumina instruments, so "if you go with another sequencing technology, you can't get any of this." The goal is to "make people lock in," while also beefing up the current sequencer.
- The expert recalled an Apple executive or board member on the Illumina board, and said ecosystem talk featured even in earnings calls, aiming to get customers engaged with the full ecosystem rather than a "piecemeal approach." "I think that's the response to Roche."

## Key risks and caveats highlighted
- Continued price erosion from Element, Ultima, MGI/BGI and now Roche, hitting both research and clinical accounts.
- Contract fragility: competitor undercuts of 25%-50% can break contracts, and price matching risks a race to the bottom and margin collapse.
- Core sequencing consumables likely flat at best; new flow cells give only about 20% price reductions that may not spur demand.
- Reliance on new sample prep, spatial and single-cell products succeeding, which depends on high differentiation. The new spatial product is just launching, and current sequencing-based spatial revenue is estimated at low eight figures.
- Reagent rental could reduce instrument revenue while protecting consumables pull-through.
- Customer budget tightness and instrument fatigue, which also cut against competitor instrument launches (Atera) but could limit demand for any new capital purchase.
- Staleness: the expert left in July 2025, so post-departure observations (35B timing, StrataMap launch, reagent rental, AXELIOS) are not first-hand.
