**Summary.** A director of a small CLIA-certified patient-testing lab (1,000-3,000 gigabases per year, 100% Illumina for short-read, 100% PacBio for long-read) describes steady volume-driven growth, expecting at least 20% sequencing growth in both 2026 and 2027. Reagent spend rose about 15% year over year in Q3 2026, with 5-10% annual list price increases roughly offset by volume discounts. The lab sees Roche's Axelios as a real competitor and expects Illumina's short-read share to erode slowly, but it will switch platforms only if economics clearly justify revalidation. It has no plans for NovaSeq X and finds TruPath Genome ($395 per sample) too expensive versus its current under-$200 cost.

## Growth drivers and constraints on output

- **Main limits on output [Expert]:**
  1. Sequencing time (turnaround time) is the first factor.
  2. Annotation and assembly of large data sets takes time. The lab outsources bioinformatics (data analysis) rather than doing it in-house. Report generation is routine.
  3. Batch size, meaning how many samples fit on one chip. This depends on the number of genes being sequenced and is a limiting factor.
- **Application mix of clinical output [Expert, approximate]:** About 50% cancer diagnostics (oncology and tumor profiling); under 20% hereditary genetic testing; the remaining ~30% covers infectious disease, rare disease and similar. The expert said it is "difficult to follow all those things" in precise categories.
- **Slowest and fastest growing [Expert]:** Infectious disease is expected to grow slowest because it can be done with PCR-based methods (a cheaper, older technique that copies targeted DNA). Panels for genetic risk factors (hereditary) are expected to grow fastest, and that is where the lab sees volume.
- **Why volume is rising [Expert]:** Volume is increasing across cancer diagnostics, including somatic (tumor-acquired) mutations as well as hereditary. The expert said "It's not about physicians ordering, but the volume is increasing across the cancer diagnostics," and speculated about more cancer samples, possibly environmental exposure. This is a general impression, not data.

## Fleet and short-read versus long-read mix

- **Illumina short-read:** The lab uses three MiniSeq and two MiSeq instruments (Illumina's smaller benchtop sequencers, suited to small gene panels), and also NextSeq (a higher-throughput Illumina benchtop). Expert described short reads as 150-200 bases and "long-read" as 5-10 kb, but also called NextSeq the long-read machine; the interviewer clarified NextSeq is short-read, and the expert did not dispute that the fleet is Illumina short-read plus PacBio long-read.
- **PacBio long-read:** All long-read work runs 100% on PacBio. The lab does not currently use Oxford Nanopore, though it used it at previous labs. Long reads are used for repeat regions and other areas where short reads give poor information.
- **Illumina long-read alternative:** The expert noted Illumina has a platform equivalent to PacBio, but the lab did not buy another Illumina platform for long-read because it already has PacBio.
- **Current split:** About 80-85% short-read on Illumina and 15-20% long-read on PacBio.
- **Expected shift [Expert, opinion]:** Short reads are currently sufficient, but the lab may shift slowly toward long-read, perhaps 5-10 percentage points over the next 12 months, for example from 80/20 to 70/30. Drivers, in order: technology improvement; need for complex-genome sequencing; possibly cost-effectiveness; and easier bioinformatics, because long reads are easier to assemble than short reads.
- **Instrument choice within short-read:** NextSeq runs more samples per batch than MiniSeq, which the expert called "the basic platform." The lab keeps using the smaller instruments because of batch sizes, "so that we don't waste the reagents of the high throughput ones."

## Utilization and sequencing volumes

- **Q3 2026 versus expectations:** Output met expectations. The lab runs at about 60-70% of its capacity, so it has headroom.
- **Q3 2026 growth:** Output rose about 10-15% versus Q3 2025, "mainly due to our salespeople sourcing, getting more samples out."
- **Full-year 2026 outlook:** At least 20% growth in sequencing-based methods versus 2025, driven mainly by higher sample input from the sales force. Q3 trends made the expert more optimistic: "The trend is increasing at least by 15%-20% year after year."
- **2027 outlook:** At least 20% growth again, "It might be more, but minimum at least 20%." Upside to 30-40% is possible but "all depends on our sales." The expert noted that for CLIA labs, revenue depends on the sales force; more test volume raises sequencing volume.
- **Takeaway on interpretation:** The growth reflects this lab's own commercial success rather than a market-wide measurement.

## Competitive landscape

- **Share today [Expert, direct]:** 100% of short-read output is on Illumina; 100% of long-read on PacBio. No vendor share change occurred in Q3 2026. The expert is familiar with Element, Roche and others but does not use them: "It is very expensive to have different kinds of platforms because of workflow."
- **Expected share loss for Illumina [Expert, opinion]:** "Illumina slowly will lose because there are multiple companies that can provide short-read sequences," naming Roche, Thermo Fisher and Element Biosciences. Thermo Fisher's platform is different technology but still short-read. The expert expects Illumina's share to reduce slowly as others take it.
- **What would trigger a switch [Expert]:** Price (sequencing cost) first; then throughput (samples per run); then run time, for example 24 hours versus an eight-hour run.
- **Roche Axelios (Axelios 1):** The lab has not adopted it. Expert's views, which are not based on use:
  - Roche is "an excellent provider" and a leader in sequencing and clinical diagnostics; the platform has high throughput and shorter turnaround than Illumina.
  - The reusable sensor model is "more attractive," but the expert wants to be sure there is no carryover of sequences between runs, a concern they have not tested.
  - "I think Roche AXELIOS is really a competitor to Illumina, definitely, because of their very strength in clinical diagnostic side."
  - RUO (research use only) status is not a barrier: "Even though it is a RUO platform, we can validate. Most of the CLIA laboratory tests are LDT tests."
- **Validation cost and time [Expert, direct experience]:** Validating a new platform for a panel takes at most about two to three weeks, from planning to the written report, and costs roughly $10,000-$20,000, depending on panel size and number of genes.

## Illumina products and deals

- **NovaSeq X (Illumina's high-throughput production sequencer):** "An excellent high throughput platform," but no current plan to buy; it might be considered in future if the lab stays all-Illumina. The interviewer cited Illumina's commitments to raise NovaSeq X flow cell output by up to 40% and speed whole-genome sequencing by about 30%. The expert said this matters only for whole exome or whole genome work; the lab uses targeted gene panels and sees no use for WES/WGS in its clinical diagnostics, so its NovaSeq plans are "very limited."
- **TruPath Genome (launched February 2026 per the interviewer; removes library preparation from the whole-genome workflow; list price about $395 per sample):** The expert said "$395 is a little more," versus their current spend of under $200 per sample. Skipping library prep might speed turnaround, but the price is higher. It gives no strong incentive to upgrade.
- **SomaLogic (Illumina closed the acquisition in January 2026 per the interviewer):** Expert sees it as a different, protein-based biomarker platform and "a good partnership" that could help expand Illumina's targeted panels, but it does not make the lab buy more: "Partnership with SomaLogic does not increase our usage of Illumina currently."

## Emerging technology and adoption approach
