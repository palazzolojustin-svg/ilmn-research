Transcript published 13 Jul 2026, interview held 8 Jul 2026. · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer of Illumina inside a large pharma preclinical research group says their Q2 2026 sequencing output rose almost 50% year over year and full-year 2026 may roughly double, driven by large-scale Perturb-seq (CRISPR gene-editing screens read out in single cells) using 10x Genomics Flex kits on NovaSeq X Plus 25B flow cells. The expert is price-tolerant, sees Roche's Axelios as only a minor threat because of switching costs and depreciation rules, and sees a modest future risk from imaging-based spatial platforms such as Atera. They are satisfied with Illumina service and expect Illumina to remain relevant.

# Expert background and how to weigh this call

**Who the expert is.** The expert is a Senior Principal Scientist and Head of Genomics at a global biopharmaceutical company, with visibility into sequencing operations and volumes and direct oversight of purchasing decisions (platform bio). They lead a cellular biology team inside a targeted diseases and systems biology department of a large pharma company. Their interview is categorized as "Doctor/Medical Professional" and was conducted by an AI interviewer. [Expert: Head of Genomics at a global pharma, Jul 2026]

**Relationship to Illumina.** The expert is a current customer, not a former employee or competitor. Their experience is current: the call covers Q2 2026 and the following months. Most statements come from direct experience of their own lab. They are explicit that they are less close to "global RNA-seq or genome sequencing rates" because they work on the disease-profiling side. Market-wide comments (for example about Ultima, CROs and Axelios) are partly second-hand or opinion, and are flagged below.

**What the team does.** The team generates high-dimensional data (measurements of thousands of genes or features per sample) for preclinical drug development. Activities include disease profiling, assay profiling, disease modeling, mechanism-of-action studies and reverse translation (using clinical-sample data to inform earlier research). They also evaluate new sequencers, vendors, service providers and contract research organizations (CROs, outside labs hired to run studies), connect internal research stakeholders with technologies, and generate data for functional genomics assays and for larger AI data lakes and predictive models.

**Customers served.** The team is internal to a large pharma. Its customers are exclusively internal bio leads across therapeutic areas, mostly in discovery or early preclinical work, with limited contact with the clinical side.

# Installed base and workloads

- The team primarily uses the NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina's current high-throughput sequencer), which is its "primary workhorse."
- Historically it also had two NovaSeq 6000s (the previous-generation high-throughput machine) and smaller benchtop sequencers (NextSeq 500, iSeq, MiSeq). It is moving toward discontinuing the smaller machines as most projects have shifted to the NovaSeq X Plus. The expert believes the X Plus was bought in "2023 or 2024."
- Workloads include bulk RNA-seq (reading gene activity averaged over many cells), single-cell RNA-seq, 10x Genomics Flex (a single-cell kit), GeoMx spatial readouts, and sequencing support for Perturb-seq.

# Competitor and alternative platform evaluation

- **Element Biosciences AVITI24:** The team is in a technology access program (TAP) with Element to assess the AVITI24 for optical pooled screens. Planned uses are CRISPR guide sequencing with direct in situ sequencing (reading RNA inside intact cells), the full 3' transcriptome, cell painting (imaging-based cell profiling) and the proteome. The project will test whether the platform suffices for their program, as justification for purchase and potential internalization.
- **Long-read (PacBio, Oxford Nanopore):** There is no internal long-read platform. With another internal customer they are evaluating external providers for an isoform profiling project (reading full-length RNA variants). Options: Oxford Nanopore's pipeline within Fulgent; a start-up called "Clareo Biosciences" (name as transcribed) for PacBio work; or a separate internal genomic-manufacturing team that owns a PacBio instrument for quality assurance and offered access. The expert anticipates they "may end up internalizing a long-read sequencer," especially if isoform profiling becomes a major strategic direction.

# Q2 2026 business conditions

## Main driver: Perturb-seq acceleration (direct experience)

- The expert's biggest volume factor is "the rapid acceleration of Perturb-seq." The team is working with functional genomics labs to expand single-cell readouts of CRISPR perturbations.
- This has "drastically accelerated" adoption of the 10x Genomics Flex kit. Sequencing demand rises as 10x pushes toward eight million cells in its largest kit formulations.
- The newer 10x Flex v2 (the expert thinks it is also called "Apex") allows many more guides for targeted Perturb-seq (not yet whole-genome Perturb-seq), and the team is expanding aggressively. It is also moving into combinatorial Perturb-seq (two perturbations per cell), "not quite VIP-seq."
- The expert is aware 10x is developing new barcoding systems that could make VIP-seq a standardized solution. The switch from v1 to v2 has allowed very aggressive sequencing, which "accelerated the demand for the downstream 25B sequencing flow cells on the NovaSeq X Plus."
- Cell counts: historically about 100,000 cells at most per single-cell experiment; now pushing toward four to eight million cells.
- Broader volume otherwise depends on ambient, variable project flow (for example bursts of bulk RNA-seq projects).

## Potential future negative: spatial (partly speculative)

- The team is moving away from ex situ Visium (10x's spatial method that uses sequencing). It has two Xenium (10x's imaging-based spatial) platforms and will pursue buying the Atera platform.
- Current protocol: run same-FFPE-block (preserved tissue) single-cell sequencing with Flex to validate cell identities seen on Xenium.
- If Atera performs as advertised, the paired single-cell step may become unnecessary, which "could cause a little bit of a negative consumption of Illumina kits." The expert says this is offset by, and "negligible compared to," the Perturb-seq acceleration.

Approval came "just a couple weeks ago" and the project was green-lit the day before the interview (about 7 Jul 2026). Ninety days earlier the expert did not know it could happen this year.

# Consumables, pricing and inventory

- **Consumables (reagent kits and flow cells needed for each run) spending:** Increasing year over year in Q2 2026, on the basis of flow cell consumption; they are "consuming more 25B flow cells" than before.
- **Price sensitivity:** "We are not sensitive at all to pricing changes." Volume is "entirely driven by the science and the projects" green-lit internally. The one pricing effect was acquiring the X Plus, which has much cheaper sequencing costs than the NovaSeq 6000.
- **Price level:** The expert is unaware of notable list price changes in the past 90 days (orders are sporadic and bulk). They cite spending of about $11,000 to $12,000 per "35B" flow cell (as transcribed; elsewhere the expert refers to 25B flow cells) and budget by project chunk. The move from NovaSeq 6000 to X Plus saved "considerably more" on cost to reach comparable output.
- **Inventory:** The lab orders per project once approved. Past attempts at bulk stocking failed because internal strategy changes made demand hard to predict.
- **Lead times:** More or less the same over 90 days, with no complaints.
- **Budget behavior:** Reagent budgets are rarely exceeded and less strictly policed than capital or labor budgets, so "we are very price tolerant on the reagent side." Because they spend heavily on 10x gel beads, sequencing cost is a smaller concern.

# Competitive landscape and share views

## Ultima (mixture of hearsay and opinion)

- The expert sees Ultima as the most notable pricing consideration, because it can hit more aggressive price-per-read numbers for cost-sensitive programs.
- Their own lab is more time- and strategy-sensitive than cost-sensitive, unlike CROs or some academic groups.
- Anecdotes: a friend in a reptile genome sequencing business is seriously exploring Ultima; the expert has also heard 10x Genomics float Ultima as a cost-saving option when aggressive cell counts make sequencing needs "alarming."
- Outlook: "Ultima may be good for the price-conscious consumer." For established users, Illumina's known quantity is "not going to be overturned simply by a slightly cheaper cost per read."

## Share outlook over the next 12 months (opinion)

- **Atera:** Expected to gain share because "everyone I know wants to preorder one." It was announced around April 2026 and suddenly three teams in the company wanted to preorder it.
- **Element:** "50/50"; the expert will judge from their own TAP.
- **Singular (G4X):** One academic KOL used it and the data seemed decent, but it is "still a bit niche." The expert has not compared it aggressively.
- **Illumina:** The team will stay with Illumina "unless there's some game-changing alteration," for example Atera proving sufficient so that the team moves away from single-cell for solid tissues, which "might start seriously cutting into Illumina."
- **Illumina's position:** Illumina has taken a more holistic approach and part of its readouts has long been bolstered by 10x single-cell. As spatial adoption grows and overlaps with generic high-dimensional readouts, there may be "some loss of just brute sequencing market share." The expert cannot see a competitor becoming the new bread-and-butter short-read player; they think newer modalities open niches rather than crowd Illumina out.
- **Spatial risk:** Atera could cut Illumina demand if users move from sequencing-based readouts such as Bruker NanoString GeoMx to imaging-based readouts that bypass Illumina.

## Drivers of platform preference

- CROs are cost-sensitive because they chase volume. Internal research is driven by strategic direction.
- Pushes toward AI model training (virtual cell, lab-in-the-loop, Parse GigaLab, consortium-style training) could drive large perturbation experiments and adoption.
- New technologies with a sequencing readout (as Visium and GeoMx had) increase sequencing demand if adopted.

## Roche Axelios

- The expert has not been sensitive to its performance. They know it is meant to compete with the NovaSeq X. Possible openings: CROs, academic core facilities that can afford it, and new pharma central labs building from scratch.
- "It's a harder sell" to replace a working NovaSeq X Plus on metrics alone.
- Rating: "a minor threat." The lab hits very aggressive single-cell numbers well within budget, and even a sequencer with orders of magnitude more capacity would give them pause.
- **Depreciation mechanics (direct experience):** Finance treats instruments as on-book assets, depreciating 1/8 of purchase value each year even if unused. A NovaSeq X Plus bought in about 2023 or 2024 would not fully depreciate until 2032. Replacing it means taking the remaining book value out of the CapEx (capital expenditure) budget on top of the cost of a new sequencer, which the expert puts at about $750,000.
- Most Axelios selling points (turnaround time, cost per read, read length) appeal more to CROs than to a lab "basically just counting genes."

# Equipment purchase plans

- Plans for the next 12 months: buy the Atera; money set aside to conditionally buy the Element AVITI24; possibly a PacBio later (probably beyond 12 months); also exploring confocal microscopy.
- Plans change constantly: "it turns into a constant political battle" and the 2026 CapEx budget has been "extremely difficult to predict." The team keeps a running wish list.
- Change in the past 90 days: the Atera announcement (about April, Q1-to-Q2 timeframe) added an item; the team also pivoted toward microscopy; and some items were removed after finding that teams on the same floor already had or were buying the instrument.

# Illumina products and ecosystem

- **NovaSeq X Plus:** "I have mostly been pretty happy with the X Plus."
- **Fluent single-cell (Illumina acquired it):** Tried; hoped for labor savings but "it really was not as labor-saving as we thought."
- **PIPseq:** Tested; "not game-changing."
- **Partek Flow (analysis software Illumina bought):** Tried; would need a heavy lift to integrate into the digital ecosystem, beyond current IT bandwidth.
- **Illumina spatial:** The team is "fairly saturated" on spatial, especially via 10x, and will not upend commitments just because Illumina enters the space. Nothing on the horizon they are "chomping at the bit about."
- **TruPath Genome:** Not interested; the team is not doing whole-genome work and cares about counting genes, large cell and gene numbers and accurate barcode matrices, not sequencing fidelity or genome volume. If it did genomic work it might lean on long-read. It does no vector profiling or LNP payload profiling.
- **SomaLogic acquisition:** Sensible for Illumina to keep access to a proteomics platform, but it does not change the expert's consumption. The team primarily uses Nomic Bio (and also Olink) instead.

# Competitor products watched

- AVITI24 (dense multiomics) and spatial proteomics. The team has Akoya and watches Lunaphore. Akoya has "had some issues"; panel validation is the biggest weakness, and the team looks to the community for better validation.
- Atera: seen as fundamentally new because of larger imaging areas, higher-volume and whole-transcriptome spatial on top of a bespoke library. The expert wants to see how it competes with CosMx.
- The team only adopts a box "if it does something fundamentally new"; leadership questions redundant readouts from different vendors and prefers simplicity.

# Support and service

- "Very satisfied." Illumina does routine preventative maintenance and responds quickly. Service contracts "can get a bit pricey, but you get what you pay for"; the team juggles older instruments on and off contract to manage cost. Field application technicians are praised.
- No change in engagement in the past 90 days. Contacts are the same, and service contracts are managed through a Thermo Fisher rep.

# Longer-term view

- The expert once expected long-read to displace short-read and in situ sequencing (Singular G4X, Element AVITI24) to displace Xenium-type approaches; instead long-read became its own niche and demand for molecular biology remains high with "sticky use cases."
- "I don't see Illumina going anywhere." Threats are matched by bolstering trends; the expert does not foresee Illumina obsolete by 2030. Newer technologies are "increasingly niche."

## Clinical adoption as a growth driver (opinion with direct observations)

- The expert sees growing willingness to add sequencing and high-dimensional profiling of clinical samples, both single-cell and bulk, and possibly spatial, for reverse translation. Previously it was "like pulling teeth."
- 10x Flex's lower single-cell cost has begun to overtake flow cytometry (the historically preferred cell-measurement method) as a cheaper readout. A clinician asked about a cheaper alternative to a flow panel on about 350 patient samples; the expert replied single-cell sequencing would cost considerably less.
- The clinical side is "the big sleeping giant that all the vendors are trying to unlock," and it is "largely a cultural fight" over belief in the data's value and lowering the infrastructure barrier.
- Clinicians favor mature technologies. Bulk and single-cell sequencing are maturing; spatial may take longer. Raw outputs (a barcode matrix, 27,000 genes) are not actionable without cell ontologies, pathways and patient-stratification endotypes. Single-cell DNA sequencing is struggling because it lacks a clear ontology, even though it enables tumor lineage tracing.

# Risks and caveats

- Results come from one lab with an unusually low 2025 base, so the near-50% and possible doubling figures reflect lab-specific project timing, not market growth.
- Future negative to Illumina consumption from Atera (removal of paired single-cell on FFPE blocks) and from imaging-based replacement of sequencing-based spatial readouts; the expert judges this negligible next to Perturb-seq for now.
- Equipment plans are volatile; the expert admits budgets are hard to predict.
- Statements on Ultima, CROs and Axelios outside the expert's own lab are second-hand or speculative.
