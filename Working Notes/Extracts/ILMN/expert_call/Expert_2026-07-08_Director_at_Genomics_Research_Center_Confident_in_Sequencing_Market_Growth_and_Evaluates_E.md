# Channel check with an assistant director of a Canadian genome center on Illumina volumes, pricing, Roche AXELIOS and competitors (Q2 2026)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-08_Director_at_Genomics_Research_Center_Confident_in_Sequencing_Market_Growth_and_Evaluates_E.md` · Date: 2026-07-08 · Channel check grid (AI Interviewer; interview 08 Jul 2026, published 13 Jul 2026); expert is an Assistant Director at a Genomics Research Center in Canada · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer of Illumina (assistant director of a large hospital-based genome center in Canada) says their Illumina sequencing output rose about 15% in Q2 2026 versus Q2 2025 and is forecast to rise about 15% for full-year 2026, driven by high single-digit organic growth plus ramping federally funded projects. Prices, inventory and lead times are stable. The expert sees Roche's upcoming AXELIOS as a potential major threat to Illumina, but probably not for 6-12 months, and is not interested in Illumina's proteomics or TruPath offerings.

# Channel check: Canadian genome center assistant director on Illumina (interview 8 Jul 2026)

## 1. Expert background and how to weigh the evidence

- **Who:** Assistant Director at a large genome center in Canada, with visibility into high-throughput sequencing and infrastructure grant writing. Classified as "Doctor/Medical Professional." The interviewer was an AI Interviewer. The interview was on 8 Jul 2026 and published 13 Jul 2026. [Expert: assistant director, Canadian genome center, Jul 2026]
- **Relationship to Illumina:** A **customer**, not a former employee or competitor. The center has run next-generation (high-throughput) sequencing since roughly 2008 and has had "a very long and abiding relationship" with Illumina since then. The expert's experience spans many generations of Illumina instruments, and the center now also runs other technologies.
- **Recency:** Current, first-hand experience. The questions cover Q2 2026 (April to June 2026).
- **Facility:**
  - It is hospital-based, affiliated with a university (a teaching hospital), and is one of **three large genome centers in Canada federated together** as a national genome sequencing and analysis architecture.
  - It serves **over 800 individual principal investigator (lead scientist) labs per year**: about **90% academic**, about **5% private sector** (almost entirely small to medium biotechs, "very little for large pharma"), and about **5% government or non-profit**.
  - It does a great deal of whole genome sequencing for research projects for hundreds of researchers in Canada and abroad.
- **Instruments (Illumina):** two **NovaSeq X Plus** (Illumina's newest high-throughput sequencer), one older **NovaSeq 6000** still in production, and one **MiSeq** (small benchtop machine) that is little used.
- **Instruments (competitors):** two **PacBio Revio** long-read sequencers and one **Oxford Nanopore PromethION 24** long-read sequencer.
- **Direct experience versus speculation:** Volumes, pricing, inventory, lead times, purchasing plans and support are first-hand. Views on Roche AXELIOS, Ultima, Complete Genomics and the market outlook are secondhand or speculative; the expert says no one they know has an AXELIOS yet.

## 2. Sequencing volume and demand in Q2 2026

- **Main driver:** A large number of **federally funded projects** that fund genome sequencing. These are run across the three national genome centers; the expert's center does roughly **half** of the sequencing. **Five projects** are being supported and are **ramping up now**. [Expert, Jul 2026]
- **Quarter-on-quarter:** "A bit of an increase in Q2 of this year as compared with Q1," mainly because of those projects. There are "no real negatives" at the moment. Utilization of the long-read sequencers is also quite high.
- **Funding durability (follow-up on government research spending):** The expert is not concerned. These large-scale projects run on long funding cycles of two, three, four years or more. Averaged over the facility's lifetime, demand has not changed much, and short-term funding fluctuations are not a worry. The expert notes the center is **not in the US** and so is less sensitive to concerns held by US peer institutions.
- **Long-term price-volume relationship (not specific to Q1/Q2 2026):** As sequencing cost per genome or per base has fallen, the center has seen "modest increases in volume" over a long period. In the expert's words, "if things become cheaper, they just do more."

### Year-over-year output (gigabases, a measure of sequencing data produced)

- **Q2 2026 vs. Q2 2025:** Output on Illumina instruments is estimated up **more than 10% but less than 20%, "probably around 15%"**, "certainly double digits."
- **What drives it:** The center normally sees **high single-digit organic growth** year over year across the facility. The remainder of the 15% comes from the ramp-up of the federally funded projects.
- **Versus expectations:** "About where we would expect it to be," i.e., meeting expectations.
- **Full-year 2026 vs. 2025:** Expected up **10% to 20%, about 15%**, again a blend of organic growth and project-driven volume.
- **Change in optimism over the prior 90 days:** None; "pretty much where we would expect to be."

## 3. Consumables, pricing, inventory and lead times

Consumables are the reagent kits and flow cells that must be bought for each sequencing run.

- **Spend:** Consumable spend is "completely correlated" with data throughput. The center runs a **cost-recovery model**, billing clients for services, and most of the bill is Illumina consumables and reagents. With throughput up about 15%, consumable spend is also up about **15%** versus Q2 2025.
- **Price:** No change in the past 90 days. Because of purchasing power across the national network of genome centers, the center has "very long-term locked-in pricing" with Illumina and does not expect changes even if Illumina changes list prices or fluctuates. "We are pretty stable."
- **Inventory:** "Sufficient." The center does not stockpile reagents and keeps a consistent flow so it is not caught short.
- **Lead times:** No change in the past 90 days; they have not decreased and have not notably increased. "Nothing is getting delayed because of shipments."

## 4. Equipment buying criteria and plans

- **Competitive pricing for instruments:** The expert has not noticed any change in competitor price concessions or incentives, but has not been looking to buy in the last 90 days. Capital cost "is probably one of the least important concerns."
- **Buying criteria, in order:**
  1. **Data quality** ("the single most important factor").
  2. **Fit with existing facilities and workflows**, especially how hard data are to analyze and how much retooling of analytical pipelines a platform change would need.
  - Cost matters little because instruments are bought with **federal infrastructure grants** and are known to be expensive.
- **Purchase plans for the next 12 months:**
  - Yes, there are federal infrastructure funds. As a publicly funded hospital, the center must use competitive or sole-source procurement, so the outcome is not 100% certain.
  - The center needs more Illumina capacity; **another NovaSeq X Plus is "probably in the cards"** over the next year or so. It "could indeed be a Roche sequencer instead," depending on how good Roche's turns out to be.
  - It is also looking at adding **long-read capacity**, possibly another long-read sequencer in that time.
  - **No change in plans in the last 90 days**: grant timing and cash flow are known long ahead, so there are no short-term fluctuations in capital purchasing.

## 5. Competitive landscape (expert views)

### Platforms gaining or losing favor

- **Oxford Nanopore:** A subset of people are "very excited," "but that has always been true." Reasons: instruments are much cheaper (appealing to cost-sensitive buyers); long-read advantages; in clinical diagnostics, speed and the ability to pull data off the instrument in real time while it runs. The expert's center is a research facility, not clinical.
- **Ultima Genomics:** "Getting a little bit of traction"; some colleagues use it now. It is "more like an Illumina NovaSeq X Plus, a very high-throughput instrument," with the promise (the expert says "supposedly") of very cheap genome sequencing, which attracts high-throughput facilities like the expert's.
- **PacBio:** "Well-established, but I don't see it gaining ground at the moment."
- **Roche AXELIOS:** Not really on the market yet but generating a lot of interest.
- **General driver:** Buyers are always sensitive to **cost per experiment**. Cheaper genome, RNA or other sequencing is attractive, and that is "the big promise of the Roche platform as well."

### Expected share shifts over the next 12 months (opinion)

- **Roche AXELIOS:** "If it does everything that it promises to do... will gain a lot of market share." People will want to buy and try it. The expert does not see this eroding Illumina greatly in that window, because Illumina has a very large, familiar installed base.
- **PacBio:** "Might slip a little bit." From the people the expert talks to, there is more excitement about Oxford Nanopore than PacBio for long reads. PacBio needs "to do something interesting or exciting or release a new instrument, perhaps with higher throughput" to hold or grow share.

### Roche AXELIOS in detail

- **Technology:** Very different chemistry, which Roche calls "Expandamer sequencing." The expert says: "I don't think any of us really know how good that technology is yet," despite many demonstrations and road shows.
- **Expert's stance:** "A little cautious": instruments are expensive, Roche is a new sequencing vendor for them, and data quality is unknown.
- **Threat level:** "If it works well, it's a major threat." Roche talks about sequencing genomes more cheaper than Illumina. The expert believes instrument cost will be "in the same ballpark as a NovaSeq X or X Plus." A tipping factor is that Roche is an enormous company with, the expert guesses, a very large marketing budget that could push adoption; if people try it and it works as claimed, "that could be a big problem for Illumina."
- **Timeline (speculation):** Not within six months, since the expert knows no one who has one. Beta testers likely exist; the expert thinks the Broad Institute (Boston) is perhaps running them. Probably not within a year either; if it gains traction over its first year of production, then **after about 12 months** it could become a big problem for Illumina.

### Other companies mentioned

- **Element:** Makes mid-throughput sequencers that "don't really fulfill a useful need for us," though other people use them.
- **Complete Genomics:** Formerly part of the BGI/MGI Chinese group, recently divested and now owned by a Swiss company the expert names as "Swiss Rockets." It seems to be focusing on diagnostics. The expert says its technology "works very well" from long history, and that new ownership gives it a much easier ability to sell instruments and reagents in the United States than when it was Chinese-owned. "Probably worth looking at, but not a player in this very high-throughput genome sequencing space."

## 6. Illumina products and strategy

- **General:** "Illumina has made a very good business out of providing sequencing that works."
- **Proteomics / SomaLogic acquisition:** Illumina acquired a proteomics company (SomaLogic, named by the interviewer). The expert's center does no proteomics, so the acquisition has **no effect** on Illumina's value proposition to them. A separate proteomics core facility in their building might be interested, but the expert sees no demand and no interest from their own research.
- **TruPath (formerly Constellation):** A "pseudo long-read application" from Illumina. The expert is not excited, knows no one using it yet, and rates their likelihood of using it as **"not at all likely."** Two reasons: (1) they already have two long-read technologies (PacBio and Oxford Nanopore); (2) running a TruPath flow cell would use time and space on NovaSeq instruments that are needed for regular short-read genome sequencing, for which "we have a lot of demand."
- **Customer support:** Satisfied. No known changes in the support network. As a big customer in a big city, the center gets good attention.

## 7. Expert's outlook on the future of sequencing

- **Costs:** Whole genome sequencing costs "are going to keep coming down."
- **Clinical adoption:** Expect many more hospital clinical diagnostic labs to adopt whole genome sequencing as a **first-line genetic test** instead of gene-specific tests.
- **Spatial and single-cell genomics:** Only a limited number of the center's clients use them now, but they are becoming more popular as prices fall and will be increasing areas.
- **Epigenetics (chemical marks on DNA such as methylation):** "Really ripe for expansion." Illumina has what it calls the "five-base genome." Oxford Nanopore and PacBio instruments can read methylation natively alongside primary sequence. The expert sees growing interest and room for more work.

## 8. Key takeaways and caveats

- **Facts (customer's own data, Jul 2026):** about 15% year-over-year growth in Illumina output and consumable spend in Q2 2026; 15% expected for full-year 2026; stable locked-in pricing, inventory and lead times; a likely additional NovaSeq X Plus purchase.
- **Opinions and speculation:** AXELIOS as a major threat only if it performs as promised and probably not before roughly mid-2027 (12+ months); PacBio possibly slipping; Ultima and Oxford Nanopore gaining some attention.
- **Possible bias or limits:** One large, Canadian, publicly funded, research-focused customer with long-term locked-in pricing and federal grant funding; the expert says they are less exposed to US funding concerns. Little exposure to large pharma, clinical, proteomics or single-cell work.
