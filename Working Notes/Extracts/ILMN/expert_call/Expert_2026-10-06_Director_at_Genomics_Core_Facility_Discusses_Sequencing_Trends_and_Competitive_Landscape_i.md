# Genomics core facility director (South Carolina, customer) on sequencing demand, shift from Illumina to Element AVITI24, and competitive landscape, Oct 2026

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-06_Director_at_Genomics_Core_Facility_Discusses_Sequencing_Trends_and_Competitive_Landscape_i.md` · Date: 2026-10-06 · Channel Check expert call (AI Interviewer); expert is a Director at a Genomics Core Facility in California per the bio, but describes serving University of South Carolina and other South Carolina universities; published 2026-10-08, interview 2026-10-06 · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer-side director of a university genomics core facility (Research Use Only, about 6,000 gigabases a year) says demand grew about 40% in Q3 2026 versus Q3 2025, driven by single-cell and spatial work, and expects 20% growth in 2026 and 15-20% in 2027. The lab bought an Element Biosciences AVITI24 in September 2026 because reagents are cheaper than Illumina's (about $1,100 versus $1,600-1,700 per comparable unit), so its short-read work moves from 100% Illumina (via external providers) to roughly 70-80% Element, and the expert expects Illumina to lose share. The expert still sees NovaSeq X as unmatched for very large projects, regards Roche's AXELIOS one as lacking a clear niche, and names Ultima, Complete Genomics and Element as Illumina's real competitors.

## Lab profile and scale

- **Output:** About 200 projects a year, each about 1.2 billion reads of about 300 base pairs. The expert calculates roughly 6,000 gigabases a year (a gigabase is a billion DNA letters read).
- **Customers:** University of South Carolina and other South Carolina universities, with some commercial and academic users elsewhere.
- **Funding mix of sequencing work:** About 75% federal grants, about 15% institutional support (from the University of South Carolina or Prisma Health, a major local research hospital), and about 10% commercial or private funds.
- **Short read versus long read:** About 85-90% short-read and 10-15% long-read output. The expert says the split is set by application and output requirements.

## Demand drivers and headwinds

### NIH funding (mixed effect)
- The expert names NIH funding as the main factor. When funding is down, the effect is mixed. New grants build the pipeline of future projects. But projects reaching completion raise demand, because groups must spend remaining money or return it to the federal government.
- In Q3 2026 grant funding available to the researchers served was unchanged versus the prior quarter. Some groups rushed to spend their money, while several groups, including the expert's own, received NSF and NIH awards with substantial sequencing components.

### Single-cell and spatial
- The expert says demand for single-cell and, now, spatial applications is "driving the sequencing demand significantly." Single-cell RNA sequencing was the first driver of Q3 output beating expectations.
- At the close, the expert says research-market growth will depend on technology development that makes single-cell more accessible, naming BD Rhapsody (the expert attributes it to "Norvatis"; the AI interviewer later says BD Rhapsody), Parse, and Illumina's PIPseq. The expert believes this "would be a main driver of the sequencing outcome in the research space."

### Biopharma and commercial work
- Pharma and biotech-funded work in Q3 2026 was stronger than expected. The reason is a new collaboration between the University of South Carolina, a foundation focused on pediatric cancer research and cure, and the local hospital's pediatric oncology group. It brings many projects analyzing primary tumors. The expert calls it a new development.

### Low-coverage genome sequencing
- Second driver of Q3 beating expectations. The expert describes it as a relatively modern technique that analyzes many genomes (for example plant, mouse or environmental organism variants) at low cost per genome, with the variation recovered across the whole dataset through permutation analysis.
- It lets the lab process large sample numbers cheaply for QTL (quantitative trait loci) and eQTL (expression QTL) analysis, which link genetic variants to traits and gene expression.
- Many labs in genome analysis, including those working on experimental model organisms, are asking for low-coverage short-read sequencing.

### Environmental microbiomics
- More projects in environmental microbiomics, which need analysis of large sample numbers, also lifted demand.

## Volume outlook

The lab's own output compared with expectations and prior periods:

| Period | Expert's figure | Stated drivers |
|---|---|---|
| Q3 2026 versus own expectations | Exceeded | Single-cell RNA-seq; low-coverage genome sequencing |
| Q3 2026 versus Q3 2025 | About +40% | Established and optimized single-cell pipelines (scRNA-seq and scATAC-seq); more environmental microbiomics projects |
| Full year 2026 versus 2025 | About +20% ("to be on the safe side") | Spatial omics and single cell |
| 2027 versus 2026 | About +15-20% | Single cell and spatial |

- Q3 trends made the expert "still more optimistic" about the 2026 forecast.
- **Caveat on linearity:** Growth may not track linearly because the AVITI24 gives more flexibility. The lab may order more reads, but with shorter reads per read than it obtained from external providers.
- **2027 assumption:** The 15-20% is a cautious figure assuming no growth in the user base. If the lab wins contracts with local hospitals and institutions and enters clinical research, the expert says a significant increase in sequencing could follow. The expert sees little growth from existing institutions. The interviewer's summary of this was confirmed by the expert.
- **Short versus long read split:** The expert expects short reads to prevail "at least in the next several years" and long-read share to stay near 10-15% for at least the next 12 months, though demand will grow in parallel. Reasons given:
  - Cost per genome is still not comparable between short and long reads.
  - Short-read quality is still much higher than long-read quality.
  - Long reads, specifically Nanopore, are complementary: whole-genome assembly, large genomic variation, full-length transcriptome and RNA variation analysis.

## Installed base and platform choices

### Short read
- **Before:** The lab used external providers (Psomagen, Metagenom) running Illumina NovaSeq X, and it owned an old NextSeq 500 (Illumina's older mid-range sequencer) that was used about once a year because its consumables were extremely expensive.
- **September 2026 purchase:** Through an NIH STTR award (a small-business technology transfer grant), the lab bought an Element Biosciences AVITI24 at the beginning of September 2026.
- **New split of work:** All medium- and small-scale projects (about 10 million reads per project, per the expert) move to the AVITI24. Only large-scale projects stay on NovaSeq X at external providers.
- **Share of short-read output:** 100% Illumina until October 2026. The expert now expects about 80% Element and 20% Illumina, or perhaps 70% Element and 30% Illumina.
- **Why (stated cause):** Reagent cost. "Reagent cost for mid-size instruments is much higher in Illumina relative to Element Biosciences."
- **Throughput preference:** The lab prefers a medium-throughput instrument, which covers most projects. A high-throughput instrument is cheaper per unit but "not significantly less expensive per run," and the NovaSeq X's service contracts and reagent investment are much higher, so it "doesn't give us enough flexibility." Balancing an in-house instrument with external providers is easy.

### Long read
- **Fleet:** Oxford Nanopore MinION and PromethION P2. 100% of long-read output is Oxford Nanopore.
- **Reasons over PacBio:**
  - Lower investment in instrument purchase and maintenance.
  - Much higher flexibility, including direct RNA-seq, which the expert says is not available on PacBio.
  - Longer available read length.
  - "For now, we didn't see the project where PacBio would be significantly better than Oxford Nanopore."

## Share outlook (next 12 months)

- **Illumina:** "Illumina will lose share because of the purchase," since the AVITI24 shifts a significant amount of sequencing to Element.
- **Oxford Nanopore:** Continues in use, but Nanopore is discontinuing the Flongle (a small, low-cost flow cell) from February 2027 (the expert says "February next year"). This "created a significant hassle for our workflow" because the Flongle was "extremely valuable" for small-scale and test projects. The expert calls it "extremely unfortunate." The plan is to bundle projects onto PromethION or MinION flow cells, "but it would be difficult."
- **Long-read share:** Expected to stay near the current 10-15%.

## Competitive landscape views

### Roche AXELIOS one
- Not adopted. "I don't see a niche for the Roche system."
- The expert's understanding (from Roche announcements and websites, not hands-on) is that maximum read length is about 1 kilobase. "It's not a long read. Definitely not long read."
- As a short-read system, it is unclear that it would be significantly cheaper than Illumina, AVITI24 or Complete Genomics, which offer up to 600 bases (300 paired-end). The expert adds "I can make a mistake."
- The expert did not comment on the reusable-sensor model, instrument price or cost per run beyond this.

### Who threatens Illumina
- The expert ranks the competitors: first Ultima, then Complete Genomics, then Element Biosciences. The expert says Element is releasing a new platform (named "VITARI" in the transcript, presumably the AVITI line) to compete at scale with NovaSeq X.
- "I think the Illumina competitor is not Roche, but rather Ultima."

### NovaSeq X strengths
- For very large projects (the expert cites 10 billion or 25 billion reads), "there's no viable competitor" currently.
- The expert notes Illumina's pseudo-long-read sequencing for human genomes on NovaSeq X, which could matter for clinical applications by standardizing long and short reads on one platform.
- The expert expects NovaSeq X to keep significant share for at least the next 12-24 months.

## Illumina product and corporate questions

- **NovaSeq X roadmap (interviewer cited Illumina's commitment to raise flow cell output by up to 40% and speed up whole-genome sequencing by about 30%):** Does not change the lab's use of NovaSeq X through providers. "For research application, the cost per project is more important than overall cost per genome."
- **TruPath Genome (interviewer: launched February 2026, removes library preparation from the whole-genome workflow, list price about $395 per sample):** Not a strong incentive. The lab would use NovaSeq X for a large human genome project, but that needs much higher clinical demand. Most of its work is non-human: mouse, rat, Drosophila, Arabidopsis, plant samples such as soybean. For mixed-origin samples the lab will keep using medium-size instruments in-house.
- **SomaLogic (interviewer: Illumina closed the acquisition in January 2026):** The expert is "not sure" of the effect. SomaLogic "is a great platform," but the expert does not know how well Illumina integrates it, and "this platform sits outside of the core business." It has not changed how the lab works with Illumina.
- **BaseSpace (Illumina's cloud informatics software):** The lab may use more of it if it gets more clinical projects, "but it's also questionable."

## Capital spending and instrument plans

- **2025:** No spending on sequencing instruments.
- **2026:** Low-to-mid six figures (dollars) for the AVITI24, already spent in September 2026, plus a planned low six figures for an Oxford Nanopore upgrade, for roughly mid six figures in total. (The interviewer first misread this as 2024 spending; the expert corrected it.)
- **Nanopore upgrade:** Nanopore is discontinuing support for the PromethION P2, so the lab seeks a PromethION P2 Solo, PromethION P2 or full PromethION, urgently, hopefully within three months and certainly within 12. The main reason is support discontinuation. A secondary reason is that a system with an integrated GPU is easier to use than one connected to an external GPU.
- **Other purchases:** None planned. "We're fully covered in a sequencing space." Focus turns to lab automation upgrades.
- **Timing:** The AVITI24 purchase "happened a bit late due to overall government shutdown and significant delay in NIH funding decisions."
- **Vendor incentives:** Instrument discounts, incentives and placement terms were unchanged in Q3 2026 versus the prior quarter ("I think").

## Consumables, pricing and inventory

- **Spending change:** Direct consumable spending in Q3 2026 rose about 90% versus Q3 2025 ("quite a lot"). The expert attributes this to the change in operating model, not price: previously the lab paid per lane or flow cell through external providers, and the NextSeq 500 was used about once a year at a couple of thousand dollars of consumables annually. After one month with the AVITI24, the lab has already spent about five figures on consumables. The expert says this is fairly representative of the run rate going forward.
- **Ordering:** Not stocking reagents. Orders on demand through the distributor (the transcript says "WR," presumably VWR) or directly from Element using flexible purchase order numbers. Inventory is "sufficient," described as project-based, just-in-time, without large volume discounts.
- **Price comparison for the main operating unit (RNA-seq):**

| Platform | Unit | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| Illumina NovaSeq X (external provider) | One lane on a 10-billion-read flow cell | About $1,600-1,700 |
| Element AVITI24 (in-house) | One flow cell | About $1,100 |

  The expert states the unit cost shifted from about $1,700 to about $1,100. The interviewer calculated this as a roughly 30-35% reduction. This is a platform switch, not a like-for-like price change on the same platform.
- **Terms and lead times:** The reagent commitment model is unchanged, and consumable lead times were unchanged versus the prior quarter.

## Vendor support

- Very satisfied with support from both Illumina and Element. The expert says Oxford Nanopore "has significantly improved in recent years" and has drawn no complaints in the past year.
- Support levels in Q3 2026 were stable versus the prior quarter: efficient, not noticeably better or worse.

## Emerging technologies to watch

- With the AVITI24 and, as the expert says, "G4" (not otherwise identified), the expert is watching in-cell sequencing technologies. There are few applications yet, but improvements for frozen and FFPE (formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded, the standard preserved tissue format) samples "may drive sequencing demand even higher."
- See also the single-cell democratization point under demand drivers.

## Takeaways for Illumina (as stated by the expert, not editorial)

- A mid-sized customer is shifting most of its short-read volume to Element because of lower reagent cost, while keeping Illumina for the largest projects.
- Volume growth is strong in this lab (+40% in Q3 2026 versus Q3 2025), driven by single-cell, spatial and low-coverage genomics, with NIH funding as a mixed factor and a government-shutdown-related delay in NIH funding decisions noted.
- The expert sees clinical research as the main upside to the outlook, and is skeptical of Roche's AXELIOS one relative to Ultima, Complete Genomics and Element.
