# Former Illumina Field Application Scientist on Illumina's Competitive Position, Rival NGS Platforms and Market Share Risk (Expert Call, Jul 2026)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-24_Former_Field_Application_Scientist_at_Illumina_Inc._Believes_Illumina_Faces_Significant_Ma.md` · Date: 2026-07-24 · AlphaSense expert call (interview 24 Jul 2026, published 28 Jul 2026); expert is a former Illumina field application scientist, now at SOPHiA GENETICS; interviewer is an investor-led (buy-side) client, unnamed · expert call*

**Summary.** A former Illumina field application scientist (left Oct 2024) ranks Illumina's challengers as Element, then Ultima, Roche, GeneMind and MGI, and explains why customers do or do not switch (validation burden, flow-cell capacity, end-to-end workflows, panels, software). The expert says Illumina has fallen from about 80% to about 70% of the market and could lose roughly 30% in total over four to five years (to 2029-2030). Illumina is described as responding with free instruments, bundled software and reagent-rental deals, a break from its past premium-only stance, while lagging in proteomics, software, automation and long reads.

# Expert call: Former Illumina field application scientist on Illumina's competitive position (24 Jul 2026)

## 1. Expert background and how to weigh the views

**Who the expert is.** The expert is a former Illumina field application scientist (FAS, a technical specialist who supports customers on instruments and assays). They left Illumina in October 2024, so their Illumina experience is roughly 21 months old at the time of the call. Since then they have worked at the following places.
- Medical biologist at the NGS cancer center at UofL Health (Brown Cancer Center), until January 2025. There they implemented TSO Comprehensive (Illumina's TruSight Oncology Comprehensive panel, a comprehensive genomic profiling test), which supports insurance claims in the US.
- Technical support specialist and lab supervisor at Altru Health System (part of the Mayo Clinic network), until August 2025. They implemented an Abbott Alinity ci laboratory automation system there.
- Currently regional implementation scientist and subject matter expert (biopharma and genomics, MENA) at SOPHiA GENETICS, a clinical genomics software and panel company. They oversee the GCC (Gulf states), with scope extending to EMEA and the US. They implemented a hereditary cancer panel at ACL Chicago and are supporting NYU Langone with an end-to-end integration of the PACT genome with Epic and SOPHiA DDM.
- Earlier, a molecular medical scientist at Mediclinic Middle East, until November 2021.
- They also say they work as an independent technical consultant for large organizations in the US, Europe and the Middle East, designing NGS labs end to end.

**Direct experience claimed at Illumina.** The expert was responsible for clinical and population-genome NGS, including the Emirati Genome project and the Bahrain Genome project. They also collaborated with All of Us and the UK Biobank. Their main account had 21 NovaSeq 6000 and 15 NovaSeq X Plus instruments plus 23 DRAGEN servers (Illumina's genomic analysis hardware and software), and sequenced about 4,000 whole genomes a week. They covered the oncology portfolio, including the launch of Pillar (a third-party oncology panel company whose deal is discussed below). They trained Pillar's field application scientists to use DRAGEN Amplicon. They were the subject matter expert for Illumina Connected Insights (ICI, Illumina's interpretation software).

**Certifications.** ASCP (American Society for Clinical Pathology) certification in molecular biology, Illumina platform certifications, and AHA Basic Life Support.

**Vantage point and bias.** The expert says that at SOPHiA they sequence their panels on every platform and therefore know the installed base of Illumina, Element, Roche and Ultima, because customers disclose what they run. The expert works for a company that partners with Element, and says SOPHiA is "leading" but "not just because I work in it." They also say that as a consultant they try to be unbiased, asking only about budget and services before recommending a sequencer. The listener should weigh this alongside the commercial interest of a software vendor. Most claims below are first-hand field observation. Predictions and market share figures are the expert's own estimates and no source is given.

**Call context.** The client opened by noting that Illumina leads the NGS market but faces cheaper options such as Ultima Genomics and Element. The client said they want a buyer-level view (accuracy, price per base, support, vendor-agnostic reagents) rather than deep technical detail, and asked how competitors are winning share and what share of Illumina's base is at risk.

## 2. Headline view and market share estimate

- **Share today.** The expert says Illumina "already has or had 80% of the market" and has "already lost 10%". They put Illumina at about 70% now. They say "their earnings will speak of it" and that earnings are being supported by layoffs and other measures, not by growth in share.
- **Share at risk.** The expert's forecast is that Illumina will lose "at least 30%, if not even more sometimes" over the next four to five years (they refer to 2029 or 2030). They say the lost share "will be divided by others". It is unclear from the call whether the 30% is in addition to the 10% already lost or includes it. Elsewhere in the call they say "for the next four to five years, it's around 30%."
- **Current ranking of challengers.** The expert gives this order: Illumina first, then Element, Ultima, Roche, GeneMind, and MGI last. Earlier in the call they gave a slightly different order (Element second, Ultima third, Roche fourth, GeneMind fifth, MGI sixth), which is consistent with this. They also said that on installed base, Element is larger than Roche, Ultima, GeneMind and MGI.
- **Three-to-four-year scenarios.** If Roche's acquisitions work out and Element and Ultima do not do well on other panels and diagnostics, Roche could move up to number two. If MGI fixes its technical problems it could become number three. If MGI does not fix them it stays at the back.
- **Overall tone.** The expert sees Illumina as reactive rather than proactive. They compare its slowness to Nokia, which was a leader in phones and almost went bankrupt because it did not renew itself when touch screens arrived.

## 3. Illumina's own product transitions

**Legacy instruments being retired (expert's account, partly speculative on timing).**
- The old MiSeq ("MiSeq legacy") will be discontinued. The NextSeq 550 will also be discontinued, but it will take longer because of its Dx (diagnostic) certification. The iSeq, the smallest instrument, used mostly in research labs for microbial or viral work, will also be discontinued soon. The expert says the iSeq and MiSeq were effectively merged into the MiSeq i100.
- Even though the MiSeq has Dx certification, customers move away easily because the Dx volume on it is small. The expert says most Dx was taken by "a sister company" and the MiSeq Dx is used for forensics or Dx-related work, and "that would be discontinued."
- Customers are also moving from NovaSeq 6000 to NovaSeq X Plus and from legacy MiSeq to MiSeq i100.

**Why the legacy instruments are being dropped.**
- The expert says the older instruments use LED-based optics that read bases less accurately, because bases are read by fluorescence at certain excitation wavelengths. From personal troubleshooting in cancer work they recall false base calls that later became false-positive variants. This is a risk when reporting diagnostic or prognostic results.
- Customers lived with this in earlier years because there was no alternative. The expert says the legacy instruments caused Illumina "a lot of headache, replacement issues."
- Illumina's strategy is to move away from them. The expert adds that customers now know there are many players and use this to their benefit.

**MiSeq i100 (expert's claims).**
- Two-colour chemistry (the legacy MiSeq uses four colours), with higher-level LEDs and different wavelengths. The expert describes it as more accurate than the legacy MiSeq and NextSeq 550. It uses XLEAP chemistry, one of Illumina's two chemistries alongside standard SBS (sequencing by synthesis). XLEAP has advantages but also disadvantages "if you don't do it right," and the chemistry also helps the hardware read bases accurately. The expert says it was designed to fix accuracy problems seen in oncology, metagenomics, lymphoma panels and other third-party panels.
- It was designed so an engineer is not needed to install it. The expert links this to Illumina cost cutting. They say Illumina's stock fell to about $40 around 2024, causing financial problems and layoffs. There were layoff waves in 2023 and 2024, and based on ex-colleagues at Illumina the expert says "there is one coming soon."
- The expert notes the NextSeq 2000 is different from the NextSeq 550 (not LED-based, as implied).

**NovaSeq X Plus economics.**
- The expert says NovaSeq X consumables are cheaper than those of the NextSeq 550 and the NovaSeq 6000, which surprised them given it is a newer instrument. Cost per sample falls as a result.
- The X Plus supports flow cells up to 25B (billion reads). The commonly used 10B flow cell can do up to 24 whole genomes and give up to 13 billion reads.
- The expert says the X Plus has much cheaper consumables than the 6000 and fits more samples per flow cell, so a customer already on an X Plus has little reason to switch.

**Other Illumina weaknesses called out.**
- Two big mistakes, in the expert's view: the investment in GRAIL (the expert says they "was living those days"), and the Pillar deal. In the expert's own validation work switching customers off Pillar panels to other panels, customers' comparisons found many false positives. The expert says Pillar panels are "really weak in the market" and not comparable to the earlier myeloid amplicon panels. They illustrate the risk: telling a patient she is BRCA positive, leading to breast removal, when the variant was a false positive.
- Software: Illumina Connected Insights (ICI) is "still not perfect" and Illumina cannot yet balance software pricing. The expert says ICI has a monthly cost and is "really, really expensive." DRAGEN licensing worked, but big customers are unhappy with licensing-based contracts. Illumina has not worked out pricing per sample or monthly storage for ICI and ICA (Illumina Connected Analytics).
- Services: Illumina started professional services but "they are too late, honestly."
- Automation: Illumina has end-to-end workflows only for some products. For whole genome it has the IGA workflow with Hamilton (liquid handler maker). The same holds for exome with Hamilton. For TSO (RUO, research use only) there is automation. For TSO Comprehensive there is no automation, and automating it would be out of label. NIPT is a fixed system on the NextSeq 550.
- Illumina Complete Long Reads: the expert says it "failed drastically" and "nobody wanted" it. They say this is why Illumina turned to PacBio for long reads, since it cannot compete with Oxford Nanopore. Long reads matter for whole genome and epigenetics (methylation). Oxford Nanopore brings large cost savings versus Illumina long reads.

## 4. Why customers switch or stay: the buyer's decision

The expert lists what customers weigh when asked "why should I switch?":
1. **Validation.** This is the "number one headache." Labs in routine use do not want to revalidate from scratch because it disrupts workflow. A competitor often has to offer free sequencing reagents to win.
2. **Samples per flow cell.** How many samples fit and what the sequencing-only consumable cost per sample is (excluding kit prep, automation and manpower).
3. **End-to-end solution.** Is there automation or a liquid handler script for the customer's panel? Will the vendor sponsor script design? Element and others lack many panels (the expert says Element has no oncology and no infectious disease panels and is "still weak in that area"), which Illumina has.

The expert also says the client's focus on cost per sample matches how they themselves calculate.

**Worked example: Element AVITI versus NovaSeq.**
- An AVITI system costs about half a million dollars including shipping. An AVITI flow cell is a maximum of one billion reads, versus up to 13 billion reads for a 10B X Plus flow cell, so the AVITI cannot match the X Plus's sample count.
- Against a site with a NovaSeq 6000, the AVITI wins on price even though it fits fewer samples.
- Against a site with an X Plus, "Element will lose," because X Plus consumables are much cheaper than the 6000's, more samples fit, and no revalidation is needed.

**Illumina's pressure tactics (expert's firsthand claims).**
- When a customer considers Element for TSO oncology, Illumina says the TSO pipeline is not validated on Element and it will not support the validation. The customer "thinks four times" before bringing Element in, and Illumina "start putting pressure on the customer. They have done it."
- Illumina has increased prices "out of nowhere" on some customers, particularly those using third-party panels, and during troubleshooting has blamed the third-party panel when "it's never the panel, sometimes it's the instrument itself, and I have enough proof of that." Some of these customers have then quietly placed Roche instruments.

## 5. Competitor-by-competitor assessment

### Element Biosciences (rank 2)
- The expert says Illumina "hates" Element and Element is "really playing this game." They view Element as the strongest challenger because it "really understand[s] the market."
- **Products.** AVITI replaced legacy MiSeq and NextSeq 550 benchtop customers but could not replace an X Plus. AVITI24 and VITARI (the expert's spelling; they refer to a larger new system) are larger. VITARI is "much bigger than the AVITI24," costs about half the NovaSeq X, and can fit the same number of samples as the X Plus. The expert says this makes a real contest with the X Plus, and Element is now moving customers from the X Plus to VITARI.
- **Strategy: end-to-end bundles.** Element partners with SOPHiA GENETICS (and the expert mentions SeqOne as another tertiary analysis provider), with Hamilton and other automation, to offer a CGP (comprehensive genomic profiling) panel, the sequencer, automation scripts for existing Hamilton or Tecan liquid handlers (so customers need not buy new automation), a professional services team, customized clinical-grade reports with the customer's logo, validation help, and a dedicated bioinformatician and field application scientist. The expert says customers "feel the burden is out."
- **Cloud storage.** Element offers five years of free storage on the Microsoft cloud.
- **Sendouts.** The expert contrasts Natera and Guardant, whose tests are sendouts (samples shipped to the vendor), with in-house kits. Sendouts are risky in wartime or when sea and air routes are interrupted.
- **Limits.** Element is not number one because it lacks the full portfolio, notably NIPT (non-invasive prenatal testing, a blood test for fetal chromosomal conditions). Sites with a NextSeq 550 running NIPT will not shift, as the expert says that is hard to move.

### Ultima Genomics (rank 3)
- Technology the expert calls "very innovative," especially the rotating disc used for sequencing, which "reduces the cost drastically." Cost is "super cheap" relative to Element and Illumina for the accuracy it provides.
- Ultima is willing to invest with the customer, and the expert says some population genome projects are secretly validating Ultima without telling Illumina, and the expert is working with one of them.
- The only bottleneck is the pipeline: there is no steady pipeline for routine workflows. Data quality is "spot on," instrument maintenance "really good," and the instrument "very stable."
- Ultima has more instruments in the market than Roche at present.

### Roche (rank 4)
- The expert says Illumina does not currently see Roche as a threat and KOLs (key opinion leaders) in the region think it is "still too early for Roche." They say there is hype but "nobody has seen real-life data." They mention work with the Broad Institute on rapid whole genome (needed for children's hospitals or certain cases but "not everyone wants it"), and say the clinical market such as MRD (minimal residual disease testing, where patients return every three to six months) is the real prize.
- Roche has placed a few instruments "secretly" under agreements, especially with customers whose prices Illumina raised. Roche will do reagent deals (for example 500 samples a year, with discounts and free-of-charge items), but is less willing than Ultima to invest with customers. Customers use these deals to push Illumina's prices down.
- Roche's AVENIO panel: a good CGP validation, and TMB (tumour mutational burden) score "spot on," but few genes and no RNA, and lacking the DNA plus RNA plus low-pass or HRD (homologous recombination deficiency, genomic instability) combination. Roche is also weak in infectious disease and NIPT.
- Roche's automation is marketed and priced in a way customers find "annoying"; customers say they will take the sequencer but already have Hamilton, Tecan or Opentrons liquid handlers.
- Roche lacked tertiary analysis software and used Franklin, which the expert says is good in germline but weak in somatic (oncology). Roche has acquired companies, especially in MRD and oncology testing.

### GeneMind (rank 5)
- A Chinese sequencing brand that "overwhelmed the market" and is in the US and the Middle East. The expert says it is "the exact mimic of Illumina," needing no conversion kit and being much cheaper. Illumina finds it very competitive in the Middle East and Europe.
- It is not mature enough for large panels such as CGP (500+ genes, TMB, MSI). It can do exomes and small panels (30-40 genes, myeloid). It is looking for a partner. It has NIPT, but the expert, from consulting complaints, says the NIPT from MGI and GeneMind is "not as perfect as Illumina," though they add Illumina is not perfect either.

### MGI Tech (rank 6, last)
- The expert says MGI "lost its touch." It is cheap but complicated: more manpower, library-type conversion costs, and it cannot do all applications. Exomes and genomes are fine, but not oncology CGP or MRD at high accuracy.
- Example: M42 (a UAE group) had the biggest MGI site in the world and, in the expert's account, "they were kicked out in less than 48 hours" because of technical mistakes. The T7 and T10 were cheaper but not at clinical-level accuracy. The expert offers to share an article. The expert says MGI is losing sites for technical reasons, not price.
- Even if Illumina is more expensive, the expert says they would still choose Illumina over MGI because MGI costs more in labor.

### Others
- **Thermo Fisher Oncomine:** "losing the market so bad right now" compared with Illumina in CGP assays and biopsies.
- **Abbott:** now in the game after (per the expert) acquiring Exact Sciences, stepping into oncology. The expert says Abbott has mastered automation (Alinity), and Illumina cannot match it at the automation level.
- **Natera and Guardant:** referenced as MRD leaders offering sendout tests.

## 6. How Illumina is defending its base

- **Bundling.** Facing Element-plus-partner bundles, Illumina has started its own: ICI, ICA, a sequencer and kits.
- **Reagent rental and free placements.** The expert says Illumina historically never offered reagent rental deals except rarely, because it considered them bad for its premium brand (the expert's analogy: Louis Vuitton does not give free goods). Now it does, "too late." Example given: at a site such as Memorial Sloan Kettering, which has its own MSK-IMPACT and MSK-ACCESS assays, with an X Plus already installed and Element offering assay, tertiary analysis and automation, Illumina offers a free Hamilton, a free NextSeq 2000, full training and a free year of ICI. The customer will take it, then "next year, we can revalidate or look into it."
- **Targeting.** Illumina goes to sites where it fears competition.
- **Distributors and KOLs.** Illumina is partnering with distributors and offering conferences, hotels and other perks to attract KOLs. The expert says this may bias decisions and "should not be allowed" under HCP rules, the medtech treaty and the Sunshine Act, and is increasing around new panels (Pillar, TSO 500 V2).
- **Customer perspective.** The main concern is operational cost, scarce expertise, and difficulty shifting among oncology, infectious disease, somatic and germline work.

## 7. Multi-omics, proteomics and single cell

- **Proteomics.** The expert says Illumina is far behind and "woke up a bit late." It acquired SomaLogic, but comparing SomaLogic and Olink data quality is "a difference between a mountain and a sea level." Olink is number one and is now part of Thermo, which the expert calls a setback for Illumina. Illumina cannot yet win in proteomics.
- **Multi-omics.** Illumina is trying to keep customers and stop other applications (proteomics, multi-omics) from pulling them away. Not all customers want multi-omics; the expert calls it an add-on, with customers starting with germline, exome, rare disease, somatic, MRD, myeloid and lymphoma before moving to proteomics and single cell.
- **Single cell.** Illumina was the lead once but the expert does not think so anymore. 10x Genomics is losing the market somewhat to cheaper rivals such as Singleron, which is almost 5x cheaper than 10x and can still be sequenced on Illumina.
