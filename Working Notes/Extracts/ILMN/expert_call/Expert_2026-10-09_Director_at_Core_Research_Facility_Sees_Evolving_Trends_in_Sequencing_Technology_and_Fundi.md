# Channel check with academic core-lab director (research-use-only, ~20,000 Gb/yr): funding squeeze, Illumina-centric fleet, TruPath and AXELIOS views, discount war

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-09_Director_at_Core_Research_Facility_Sees_Evolving_Trends_in_Sequencing_Technology_and_Fundi.md` · Date: 2026-10-09 · AI Channel Checks (AI interviewer) expert call; expert is a Director at a Core Research Facility (customer); interview 2026-10-09, published 2026-10-14 · expert call*

**Summary.** A director of an academic medical center core lab (research use only, over 20,000 gigabases per year, almost entirely Illumina short-read) says Q3 2026 sequencing output fell about 10% year over year because of erratic and delayed NIH funding, but expects about 5% growth in 2026 and mid-double-digit growth in 2027 as delayed grant awards arrive. The lab received larger Illumina trade-in discounts on NovaSeq X, describes a "discount war" among vendors, has not adopted Roche AXELIOS or Illumina TruPath, and expects 5-10% of demand to shift to Oxford Nanopore long-read if it can fund a GridION. Consumable spend fell 5-10% mainly by switching to cheaper third-party library prep kits, and the expert flags the US DoD 1260H list (Complete Genomics, Novogene) as a market-structure risk.

## Expert background and how to read this call

- **Who:** A director at a core research facility (a shared laboratory that runs sequencing for many research groups) inside an academic medical center. The expert has visibility into operations, financials and strategic investment. [Expert: core-lab director, customer, Oct 2026]
- **Relationship to Illumina:** Customer. The lab owns Illumina instruments and buys Illumina consumables (the reagent kits needed for every sequencing run). It is not a former employee or competitor.
- **Recency:** Interview held 9 Oct 2026, published 14 Oct 2026, so Q3 2026 comments are first-hand and current.
- **Setting:** Research use only (not a CLIA-certified clinical diagnostic lab). Serves mostly academic research institutions.
- **Format:** AI-interviewer channel check. Much of the content is direct experience (own lab's output, spending, discounts, support). Views on the wider market (AXELIOS threat, DoD list, Atera) are opinion or speculation and are labelled as such below.

## Lab profile and funding mix

- **Scale:** Average annual output is "over 20,000 Gb, give or take" (gigabases, a unit of sequencing data volume). [Expert, Oct 2026]
- **Funding sources:** About 80% of research projects are federally funded, roughly 5% come from pharma-related sponsors, and the rest is foundation or philanthropic money (internal to the institution or from national foundations). Federal grants are the majority of the lab's revenue.

## Funding climate (the main headwind)

- **Top factors affecting output:** The expert named reagent price points, data storage and AI, and the competitive market. Federally funded grants were called a "minor impact" in the first answer, but the funding climate was described as "quite erratic" and was then discussed at length.
- **Behavior changes (direct experience):** Researchers are less willing to buy and pre-order reagents at scale for a whole study, are reducing sample sizes to save money, and in some cases have stopped studies altogether to rethink whether the grant money has a better use. The interviewer asked whether it was pullback or uncertainty; the expert said "It's both."
- **Q3 2026 grant funding vs Q2 2026:** Decreased. Most of the decrease was delays by the NIH (National Institutes of Health) and other federal funders in releasing funds and/or notices of award (NOAs, the formal notice that a grant has been approved).
- **Outlook:** Notices of award have started to go out, late. The expert expects work to pick back up next quarter and "an increase in grant activity over the next couple of quarters."
- **Pharma:** Q3 2026 pharma/biotech-funded work was "as expected" (not stronger or weaker).

## Sequencing fleet and short-read vs long-read mix

- **Illumina short-read fleet:** MiSeq, NextSeq 2000, NovaSeq 6000 and NovaSeq X Plus, plus Illumina's array platform (a non-sequencing DNA chip technology).
- **Long-read fleet:** One Oxford Nanopore MinION (a small, low-cost portable long-read sequencer). The lab has been exploring buying a GridION (a higher-throughput Nanopore instrument).
- **Current split:** 95% short-read, 5% long-read. Most investigators focus on gene expression, with very little interest in long-read uses such as non-model-organism genome references or translocations (large structural DNA rearrangements).
- **Expected shift:** The expert expects long-read to reach about 15%-20% of output over the next 12 months. Drivers: more interest since Roche released AXELIOS for potentially more long-read applications; newly recruited investigators from other institutions whose questions require long-read; and grants going out.
- **Long-read vendor split:** 100% Oxford Nanopore, because the lab has only a MinION and "the big driver of that is cost."

## Q3 2026 output and outlook

| Metric | Expert's answer |
|---|---|
| Q3 2026 output vs expectations | Below expectations |
| Q3 2026 vs Q3 2025 | Down about 10%, from funding shifts and investigators saving money on projects to reinvest elsewhere |
| Full-year 2026 vs 2025 | Up slightly, about 5%, as NOAs let the lab "make up a pretty big gap" by year-end |
| 2027 vs 2026 | Up, "about mid-double-digits" (the interviewer contrasted this with the ~low-single-digit 2026 growth) |

- **Sentiment:** More optimistic after Q3, because the expert can see incoming NOAs and is working with investigators on plans for them.
- **2027 drivers:** Conditional on no major federal or other funding problems. (1) Multiyear grants and inter-institution collaborations the lab knows it is funded for. (2) Pending large grant applications related to ARPA-H (a US federal health research agency), with optimism that a percentage will be funded. (3) New technologies that use sequencers already in the fleet and let investigators work faster or cheaper. (4) Newer sequencers the lab hopes to acquire in the next 12 months. (5) Spatial technologies (which map gene activity within tissue) need much more sequencing output, and their falling costs make them more attractive to investigators, feeding the sequencers.

## Competitive landscape

### Short-read
- **Share:** "Almost 100%" Illumina; no Roche, Element, Ultima or others. Reason: heavy investment in Illumina, and "the infrastructure and administrative costs to switch to different platforms really hasn't had a strong value yet." The lab has started exploring alternatives and may shift capability in future depending on investment size and instrument utility.
- **Throughput mix:** Not shifting. The lab strongly favors high-throughput production sequencers because they give the best per-base price and suit diverse projects. The few low-throughput instruments are walk-up, user-run machines for the community.
- **Share change in Q3 2026:** None within the lab.
- **Next 12 months:** The expert expects Illumina to lose some share of the lab's work to Oxford long-read, about 5%-10% of sequencing demand moving from Illumina short-read to Oxford, partly for spatial work needing long-read. This is predicated on securing funds for the GridION.

### Roche AXELIOS
- **Adoption:** Not adopted. Price is "attractive," but hesitation stems from not yet understanding the diversity of projects it can run, and quality metrics have not been in the field long enough (launched, in the expert's recollection, late 2025 or 2026). The lab has not been able to vet it.
- **Threat to Illumina (opinion):** "It depends." In clinical labs it "could be a significant threat" because many clinical labs like Roche technology and its simplicity. The biggest headwind for Roche is labs' existing investment in LIMS (lab information management systems) and SOPs (standard operating procedures).
- **Research labs:** For the expert's lab, running two workflows (Roche and Illumina) would be a big administrative, supply-chain and cost burden. A switch makes sense mainly for a complete switch, or in clinical settings where specific tests run uniquely on Roche.

## Illumina products and roadmap

- **NovaSeq X:** Adopted (NovaSeq X Plus). The lab uses it for most things. Illumina's committed roadmap of up to 40% higher flow cell output and ~30% faster whole genome sequencing does not change usage: "No, because we predominantly use it now for most things." (Roadmap figures were stated by the interviewer.)
- **TruPath Genome:** Launched Feb 2026 per the interviewer; removes library prep from the whole genome workflow; list price about $395 per sample (interviewer's figures). The lab has not adopted it, but just began testing and will do due diligence over the next few months.
  - Concept on NovaSeq is "interesting." The problem is scale: the quoted price "is indicative of max efficiency of scale," so initial costs for the lab will be much higher than other platforms until volume grows.
  - Strategic priority: see how well TruPath supports clinical applications and whether the lab can partner with clinical colleagues to raise volume enough to reach the quoted price.
  - **Long-read implication:** TruPath raises the question of moving future long-read projects to Illumina. One instrument doing multiple things beats multiple instruments with separate workflows and supply chains. The expert is "optimistic" that the NovaSeq X investment may keep long-read work on Illumina, but must evaluate it against PacBio and Oxford first. Unless sample volumes are exorbitant, it becomes a platform decision point.
  - It has delayed the lab's long-read purchase decision.
- **SomaLogic (acquisition closed Jan 2026 per interviewer; a protein measurement platform):** Has not caused the lab to shift any extra work to Illumina. The expert sees the concept but doubts it helped Illumina's value proposition: the platform is "burdensome" and the starting equipment is "very, very expensive." Competitors such as Olink and sequencing-based proteomic readouts exist; Olink Reveal covers roughly 90% of the institution's needs without buying automation and equipment, so SomaLogic may not be worth the investment for most uses.

## Instrument purchases and discounts

- **Plans, next 12 months:** The one potential sequencer purchase is Oxford long-read (GridION), depending on the TruPath evaluation and long-read needs. The lab is also exploring replacing its older MiSeq fleet with the new MiSeq i Series (more flow cell options, output and chemistries); the current MiSeq is "starting to become pretty outdated." Other investments are mostly single-cell and spatial tools to feed the sequencers.
- **Q3 2026 placement plans:** Delayed. Causes: TruPath's release delayed decisions, and the funding climate and revenue decreases led the lab to put off expensive instrumentation "for an extended period of time."
- **2026 capital spending on sequencers vs 2025:** Higher by about 5%, on a likely long-read purchase (contingent on TruPath not fitting needs). The expert does not see an absolute need to upgrade the MiSeq in 6-12 months, but trade-in discounts could change that.
- **Instrument discounts, Q3 2026 vs Q2:** Increased, specifically from Illumina. Large trade-in discounts were offered on new NovaSeq X for customers trading in a NovaSeq 6000, "substantial" versus prior quarters, which made the lab consider a second sequencer (adding redundancy and enabling clinical TruPath use if the clinical partnership works out).
- **"Discount war":** The expert believes vendors discounted aggressively to win placements, and that rumors and data around Roche's AXELIOS pushed providers to be more aggressive. Illumina lock-in remark: once labs, especially smaller ones, invest in a large capital instrument like NovaSeq X, "it's very hard" to invest in another technology. (Opinion, partly speculation.)

## Consumables and pricing

- **Spend, Q3 2026:** Decreased from Q2 "for sure," by about 5%-10%. (The question asked about Q3 2025; the answer was framed versus Q2.) Cause: pivoting away from premium consumables because of financial constraints.
- **Sequencing reagents:** No competing consumables exist for the Illumina platforms, so the lab could not substitute there.
- **Library prep (the step that prepares DNA for sequencing):** The lab cut spending by switching to cheaper third-party library prep kit vendors.
- **Price of comparable consumables, 2026 vs 2025:** About 5%-10% lower, mostly from alternative library prep vendors. Illumina also gave special discounts on some NovaSeq X consumables used for larger projects, but "the majority of the change was related to the library preparation component."
- **Reagent/commitment terms in Q3:** Remained the same versus Q2.
- **Lead times in Q3:** Stayed the same.

## Inventory

- **Internal stock:** Described as "insufficient"; clarified as lower than usual on-hand stock. Expected to stay that way through the rest of calendar 2026 because funding and PI (principal investigator) experimental design are uncertain, carrying costs are high, and expiration is a worry. The hope is to normalize in 2027 and pre-buy more to protect turnaround time.
- **Supplier inventory:** Issues only at some spatial and single-cell companies (delays, back-orders), not in sequencing components.

## Vendor support

- **Sales:** "Very good," very open to working on price constraints and lead times.
- **Technical support:** "Not as good as it once was" versus a year earlier, partly due to a shift to AI intake calls and chatbots, which the expert finds not yet effective.
- **Informatics/analysis support:** Also worse than before. Illumina's analysis tools are "not very intuitive," with little ability to tweak parameters, and its informatics support "seems to be learning as we go along."
- **Overall ranking:** Sales very high; technical support for kits, library prep, sequencing and informatics "modestly satisfactory" and could be "much, much better."
- **Q3 change:** Support levels stayed the same despite some personnel changes.

## Emerging technologies to watch

- **10x Genomics Atera:** Roadmap includes onboard sequencing; it will be interesting to see whether it replaces or augments standalone sequencers.
- **Trend toward making existing sequencers do more:** The expert likes vendors (Illumina, TruPath, spatial efforts) making current instruments do more rather than forcing new purchases every couple of years. Open question is the quality and value of integrated platforms versus standalone methods. [Opinion]

## Regulatory and geopolitical flag: DoD 1260H list

- The expert highlighted companies on the US Department of Defense "1260" list, citing Complete Genomics (formerly MGI) as an example. Complete Genomics historically had a decent market impact in certain areas.
- Government-funded academic institutions are being advocated not to engage with listed companies, which may change the market mix.
- Non-US providers such as Novogene are also listed. Novogene uses Illumina, Ultima and other platforms, so a pivot away could shift consumable and sequencing spend of companies like Illumina, depending on how much US-based academic and commercial providers can fill the gap.
- Speculation: sequencing providers might pivot toward Roche or Ultima (which is revamping its offerings). The expert called it an interesting dynamic to watch. [Opinion/speculation]
