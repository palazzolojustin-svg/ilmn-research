# Expert call with an R&D Director of Photonics at Fluxus, Inc. (Illumina customer): ecosystem lock-in, consumables cost, competition and switching costs

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-28_RD_Director_of_Photonics_at_Fluxus_Inc._Believes_Illuminas_Ecosystem_Strengthens_Its_Marke.md` · Date: 2026-07-28 · Expert-network transcript (published 30 Jul 2026), buy-side (investor-led) client interviewing a customer expert, R&D Director of Photonics at Fluxus, Inc. · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer-side expert who owns and uses Illumina's NovaSeq (and casually the MiSeq), and has used PacBio and Oxford Nanopore, explains why Illumina remains the default choice. Reasons given are its broad ecosystem, compatible consumables, software and libraries, and very high switching costs. He sees competitors catching up on accuracy, consumables pricing as a pressure point, a saturated market, and data analysis (including AI) as the next growth area. He is lukewarm on the NovaSeq X upgrade and declined to keep paying for DRAGEN.

## Expert background and how to weigh the views

- **Who the expert is.** The expert is the R&D Director of Photonics (the optics side of instrument design) at Fluxus, Inc., a biotech company. The transcript's bio says the role covers developing diagnostic tools for antigens, DNA and proteins, plus photonic integrated circuit (PIC, optical chips) development. The scope is global, the expert has 8+ years of experience, and reports to the CTO, one level below the C-suite. The expert says they hold a PhD in chemistry and have done biotechnology and DNA-technology research for "roughly 10+ years". Fluxus does instrument and biomarker development, mostly for diagnostics. [Expert: R&D Director of Photonics, Fluxus, Jul 2026]
- **Relationship to Illumina.** The expert is a **customer** (the call is tagged "Customer" perspective, with an investor-led buy-side client). The bio says they have used Illumina's NovaSeq, PacBio and Oxford Nanopore instruments, currently use the NovaSeq, and are a casual user of the MiSeq. In the call they also mention a previous employer had a NextSeq and then a NovaSeq. The bio notes familiarity with Illumina and Roche as competitors/collaborators. The call took place on 28 Jul 2026 and was published 30 Jul 2026, so the experience is current.
- **Direct experience versus speculation.** The expert speaks from direct use on NovaSeq, MiSeq, the DRAGEN trial and BaseSpace, and on purchasing considerations. On Oxford Nanopore the expert says they used it "only from the research perspective" and admits possible bias. On Illumina's optics, they say they "don't fully understand" how Illumina's optics work. On NovaSeq X pricing they say "I actually don't know". Those parts are opinion or speculation. Note the expert is a small-scale user (not a core facility), and said they send out work to test, which limits how far their utilization comments generalize.

## Instrument utilization (NovaSeq)

- Fluxus owns a NovaSeq (an Illumina high-throughput sequencer), and the expert's previous employer had a NextSeq (a mid-range Illumina sequencer) and then a NovaSeq. The expert says the use is mostly at companies and research labs.
- The instrument is not run at full capacity over a year. "Maybe when there are some projects for a few months, it was pretty full, but overall, in a year, not really." They add that core facilities (shared service labs) probably have higher volume, but they are not sure. [Expert: direct experience, Jul 2026]

## NovaSeq X (Illumina's newest high-throughput sequencer)

- **Upgrade intent.** The expert has heard of it and knows it has "much higher capability" and output, but says "I don't think we're going to switch to that." It depends on cost, and their existing NovaSeq was "quite an investment already." They state the general rule that when buying new equipment, buyers normally choose the newest model, so a new purchase would likely be the X.
- **The client's specs.** The client cited 35 billion reads and Q70 quality scores (a measure of base-call accuracy) for the X, and asked whether this opens new applications. The expert said they don't know what Illumina would quote. They recalled that the prior model was "$6,000 or $2,000 or something" (the figure is unclear and appears to refer to cost per run or per genome; the expert was unsure), and called the improvement "quite a large improvement if you were to buy new."
- **Not revolutionary.** Whether it revolutionizes the field depends on whether workflow and pipeline, such as ease of data analysis, improve with the throughput. They believe the X shares the same software, so it is "something nice to have but I wouldn't say it's the next AI or something, in my opinion."

## MiSeq (Illumina's small benchtop sequencer) and benchtop competition

- The expert likes the MiSeq's flexibility and small footprint.
- Many newer competitors are appearing, all benchtop and getting easier to use.
- MiSeq's edge is that it stays inside Illumina's ecosystem. Labs that already use Illumina's bigger instruments hold the consumables, kits and chemistry, which gives a "pretty big edge" against moving to a new platform.
- They call it "a pretty good start-up instrument for small labs, universities" and "a great product." [Expert, Jul 2026]

## Consumables cost and the business model

- **Industry model.** The expert says "You don't make money from the instrument. You do make money from the consumables" (consumables are the reagents and kits needed for each run). Fluxus is a similar instrument developer, so "we get it." The costs "still add up pretty quickly."
- **Competitors are no different.** Moving to PacBio or Nanopore "would still be a similar model" with consumables to buy, "unless there would be a company who has a different business model." Hence "I think the customer is stuck with it. That's a little bit why Illumina is dominating the market."
- **Lock-in.** "Once you start with it, you just keep buying the consumables, particularly the reagents, the kits and all the other stuff."
- **Fluxus's decision.** They budgeted Illumina's consumables against other products and "eventually chose Illumina, despite the consumable cost." They hope for lower prices "with the pricing pressure" but say "we'll see."
- **Pricing as a challenge (later in call).** The expert says Illumina's pricing "doesn't really go down over the years that much," which they call "a little bit challenging for them," while off-brand, less accurate models offer "quite a bargain."

## Why Illumina wins: service, ecosystem and platform choice

- **Sweet spot.** Illumina has "a pretty sweet spot for the majority of the sequencing needs in the market." They believe it has "the best per test cost for a medium level of sequencing" covering most current research interest. (The client asked about service and uptime, but the expert did not give specific service or uptime comparisons.)
- **Competitors specialize.** PacBio and Nanopore specialize in very long DNA strands (long-read sequencing), which Fluxus and many others do not need. When Fluxus needs long reads it outsources a few samples to other vendors rather than buying an instrument.
- **Breadth.** Illumina has a large portfolio to fall back on, an extensive data analysis pipeline and library, and easier-to-find sample prep and consumables that match other vendors, which the expert calls "a pretty major consideration." The expert is unsure whether Illumina has been around longer than PacBio, but believes it started early. "If you're just doing standard DNA or RNA sequencing, that would probably be your first choice."
- **Illumina versus PacBio decision factors (in order given).** (1) Accuracy. (2) Cost, including the whole pipeline: consumables and ease of data analysis, not just the instrument. (3) User-friendliness, which matters first for senior scientists and then trickles down to operators, including ease of transferring knowledge. On specs, both are "pretty good"; "Illumina was slightly better" on accuracy. Fluxus chose Illumina because it is compatible with many things, making the per-test cost "a bit cheaper, easier to use, easy to analyze."

## Oxford Nanopore view

- The expert used it only in research and describes it as a smaller company with very different technology (it reads an electronic signal as DNA passes through a pore). It came from "a pure research university" and "used to have a not very good accuracy when they first started."
- The expert admits being "personally a little biased or doubt" whether it is as good as traditional biochemistry-based reads.
- It is not widely used and certainly not as big as Illumina. They would worry about support and downstream data analysis ("you have a sequence, but what are you going to do with the information?"), and think Illumina's library and support are better than those of a company of Oxford's size. [Expert opinion, partly speculative]

## Switching costs and what would trigger a switch

- **Costs are very large for a continuously running lab.** Reasons: staff are familiar with the instrument, all prior data is tied to it, and stacks of consumables sit in storage. A complete switch would be "enormous." The exception: a lab with a sudden super-long-read core project might buy a PacBio and run it alongside Illumina.
- **Validation.** A switch requires quality control to check the new instrument gives results similar to the previous one, since earlier decisions were made on the old data. "At least for our development, it would be huge."
- **Triggers (Fluxus has switched instruments before).** (1) Outreach and education from the competitor, such as a PacBio team helping with setup, training and bridging differences. (2) A change in focus where the competitor adds much more value. (3) Cost: if, over the next roughly three years of expected volume and sample types, the lab expects to save "a considerable amount of money," a switch could be worth the investment.

## Accuracy and competitive catch-up

- The expert says "definitely the competitors are catching up." Industry accuracy has grown "enormously" over about the past 10 years. Illumina initially had an edge as "one of the most accurate machines in the world," but Nanopore's advertised accuracy is now "pretty close," and Illumina's technical advantage on accuracy "is definitely slowing down."
- Both the big companies and "off-brand companies outside of U.S." report pretty high accuracy. (Note these are advertised figures; the expert did not cite numbers.)

## Optics and barriers to copying Illumina

- The expert does not fully understand Illumina's optics. From their own field expertise, bio-detection optics "aren't super complex" and are not like fancy Nikon microscope optics. The design itself is not the barrier and "you can definitely copy those."
- Most optics designs are patented, so they do not think one can legally copy them unless operating outside the U.S. They add that many patents and papers describe similar designs.
- Nanopore, "once believed to be super hard to make," can now be made in a lab, though not sold. The true difficulty is the whole system: making it "reliable, low-noise, repeatable."

## Alternative technologies and how to challenge Illumina

- Many technologies can do DNA sequencing, but at the whole-system level (sample prep, liquid handling and so on), Illumina benefits from "inertia": people still use it heavily, and the system has matured and been "perfected."
- A good challenger "probably want[s] to start with okay accuracy but benchtop, quick, cheap, super user-friendly." The expert says Illumina is getting more complex as it grows, and small projects want fast, cheap turnaround, which "might be the next edge or the niche."

## Where innovation matters most: data analysis and AI

- The expert says the next innovation point is probably data analysis, not hardware. Optics and fluidics are "good enough" for most samples for Illumina, PacBio, Nanopore and "MGI or something." The question is "what do you do with these data? What does it mean?" Including AI to sort the information is "a pretty hot topic" and "might be the next growing point." [Expert opinion]

## Software: DRAGEN and BaseSpace

- **DRAGEN** (Illumina's bioinformatics platform, "Dynamic Read Analysis for GENomics", defined by the client). The expert calls it "the most advanced subscription software." Fluxus had a one-year subscription, which was "quite expensive." It is powerful and has features they never used, a nice GUI and appears user-friendly, but has a "pretty steep learning curve." They stopped using it because it is pricey and they only need heavy bioinformatics occasionally, which "doesn't really justify the cost."
- **BaseSpace** (Illumina's basic software and cloud platform, which the expert believes Fluxus subscribes to). The expert calls bio software generally "not top-notch," just "tolerated," sometimes confusing and complicated, but "probably one of the easier software to use. It gets the job done."
- **Is software a lock-in?** No: "It's mostly the hardware and the ecosystem and a lot of investment we already made." They say the software "could be better" and that the same is true of everybody else's.

## Challenges and risks for Illumina (expert's closing view)

- Pricing pressure from cheaper, less accurate off-brand models, and Illumina's prices not falling much over the years.
- A somewhat saturated market. DNA analysis "doesn't have too much of an outlet." It is "still blocked by the research": "You've got your DNA, then what? You only extract very little information," which limits volume growth. This applies to the whole sequencing industry: "The supply is good. What's the next demand point?"
