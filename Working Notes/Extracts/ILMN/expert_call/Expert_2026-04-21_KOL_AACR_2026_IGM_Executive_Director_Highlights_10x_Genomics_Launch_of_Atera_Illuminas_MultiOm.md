**Summary.** A university genomics core director who is a long-time Illumina-dominant customer reacts to AACR 2026. She is very enthusiastic about 10x Genomics' Atera spatial instrument ($495K, 50 units in first release, 2H 2026) and expects it to make Xenium obsolete, is lukewarm on Roche's Axelios (high throughput, not modular, slow library prep) and sees Illumina keeping the clinical market in the near term. # AACR 2026: UCSD genomics core director on Atera, Axelios, Illumina multiomics and academic funding

## Expert background and how to weigh the views

- **Who:** Dr. Kristen Jepsen (KJ), Executive Director of the IGM Genomics Center at the University of California, San Diego (UCSD). The call was moderated by Andrew Carmen (AC) of Guidepoint and took place on 2026-04-21, after AACR 2026 (the American Association for Cancer Research meeting). The viewpoint is labelled "Academic KOL – Physician Scientist". KOL means key opinion leader.
- **Relationship to Illumina:** She is a customer, not a former employee or competitor. She runs a mid-sized core facility (a shared-service lab that runs sequencing and other assays for university researchers), mostly in research, but increasingly partnering on clinical research. She said her background is unchanged from earlier conversations with the moderator, so this is a repeat advisor. Her experience is current: she cited her own Q1 2026 run volumes and recent product evaluations.
- **Her equipment (direct experience):**
  - Sequencing is "pretty Illumina dominant": one MiSeq i100 and one NovaSeq X Plus (one of each), plus older Illumina equipment no longer run.
  - Single cell (profiling individual cells): most samples run on the 10x Chromium using Flex, 3 Prime and GEM-X kits. She also has the equipment for Illumina's single cell protocol (PIPseq, a fluidics-free method) and supports it for customers.
  - Spatial (measuring gene activity while keeping tissue location): mainly 10x Xenium; some Visium on a CytAssist; a GeoMx for profiling; and access to a Bruker CosMx in another lab that she runs occasionally on customer request.
  - Long reads: she runs no PacBio (one sits in a neighbouring core) and the only Oxford Nanopore (ONT) use at UCSD is in individual labs on niche projects.
- **Where she speaks from experience vs speculation:** Her descriptions of Xenium, CosMx, her lab's run volumes, UCSD funding and CRO competition are first-hand. Atera details come from the 10x launch presentation and conversations with 10x staff, so they are launch claims plus her judgement. Her views on Axelios, Illumina's ctDNA position and the future of Atera cannibalization are opinion and have not been tested by her; she said she had not seen a bake-off including Axelios.
- **Compliance note:** The call opened with Guidepoint's request that the advisor avoid commentary on stock prices, valuation or non-public information.

## 10x Genomics Atera launch (spatial genomics)

### Overall reaction and why she calls it a field shifter

- Atera is 10x's new spatial instrument, launched at AACR 2026. KJ said 10x had built up anticipation: there were rumours at AGBT (the genomics industry meeting) in February and 10x kept a deliberately low presence there before announcing at AACR. She said Atera "exceeded expectations."
- She sees the same business model as sequencing companies: customers are "forced into a capital upgrade" to get cheaper per-sample cost and higher throughput. Her words: "If we don't buy an Atera, we're going to lose all of our spatial business within a couple of years for sure because the Xenium just won't be able to compete."
- **Data quality:** The whole-transcriptome data (the whole set of expressed genes, rather than a preselected panel) shown was "much more sensitive" than Xenium 5K (Xenium's roughly 5,000-gene panel). She said Xenium 5K "has been relatively disappointing for people," who mostly stuck with smaller panels. For those wanting whole transcriptome, Atera is "a very affordable" approach in spatial terms, with a much bigger slide area and therefore much higher throughput.
- **Panels too:** She expects Atera's targeted panels to be even more sensitive than earlier Xenium chemistry. Overall: "I think it's just a win-win."
- **Timing of her own preparation:** Over the next six to twelve months before full launch, she must work out how to get histology (tissue sectioning and staining) running so she can multiplex biopsies on the larger slide area and "get really the worth out of the system."

### Standard slide format and logistics

- Atera uses standard glass slides, roughly a 5 cm-square usable area per the moderator's recollection. A larger area lowers cost per square millimetre.
- Xenium slides cost about $1,000 each, which she says makes histology facilities reluctant to section tissue onto them. With a standard slide, a wrinkled section can be discarded and redone, so "the histology people are going to be very happy."
- The Xenium imaging area is about 10 x 22 mm near the top middle of the slide, with fiducials (alignment markers) that tissue must avoid or registration errors occur. This causes stress when packing tissue tightly. Atera uses the full slide.
- Shipping: Xenium slides to off-site collaborators must go on dry ice. Standard paraffin-section slides can ship at room temperature, which she thinks will ease outsourcing and open new collaborations.

### Single-cell-level whole transcriptome

- Atera uses essentially the same probe targets as the Flex single cell kit. 10x showed transcripts-per-cell data comparing a section run on Flex versus whole transcriptome on Atera.
- KJ: it is "not going to be as sensitive as single cell. It's still a little behind that," but looks very promising compared with 5K. For discovery work she calls it "a paradigm shifting sensitivity level" versus Xenium, depending on how much tissue can be packed and how much time is spent designing custom panels.
- She noted that some argue the MERSCOPE (Vizgen) is the most sensitive and quantitative platform, but it is limited in probe count, so people agonise over probe selection "only to have like a paper reviewer say, what about this gene?" Whole transcriptome avoids that.

### Pricing, capital budgets and economics

| Item | Figure | Source/context |
|---|---|---|
| Atera instrument price | $495,000 | KJ: "They're saying $495K" |
| Consumable cost per slide | about $9,000 | KJ, whole transcriptome |
| Throughput | 4 slides per week; 200 slides per year of about 5 cm² each | KJ's preferred framing |
| Sample claim | 800 samples per year | 10x framing (800 samples of 1 cm²; realistically about 4 cm² of usable data per slide) |
| Consumable spend at full capacity | about $1.8 million per year (200 slides x $9,000) | whole transcriptome only |
| Xenium capacity | about 100 slides per year | KJ's recollection |
| First release | 50 instruments | Planned for 2H 2026 |
| Early access | 3-4 units believed out in field; 15 early-access customers with POs | KJ |

- She said pricing is "cheaper per millimeter than the 5K and really not that much per slide than the 5K." The slide that circulated claiming "one Atera is four Xenium" is, in her words, "a massive overexaggeration"; she thinks about two Xeniums are needed to match one Atera.
- At full capacity the consumable spend is significantly higher than Xenium's. Nobody will run that much in year 1. When the moderator suggested realistic usage at half capacity gives pull-through (consumable revenue per instrument) under $1 million a year, she agreed: "well under $1 million."
- She believes 10x is "pretty creative" on capital constraints, using bundled reagent-rental style deals and deep box discounts for large reagent commitments. 10x has said it will be "generous" to Xenium owners on trade-ins, but she said "let's just wait and see." She expects regional sales managers to compete to place at least one Atera in each territory, because consumables bring long-run profit.
- **Capital budget headwind:** "They're releasing an instrument in a time when capital budgets are really quite impacted." Her lab will need to "raise some money to see if we can buy this thing," which she called the only downside.

### Stage and timing

- Early release to early-access customers only. The full launch is stated for 2H 2026; she expects shipping to start "maybe in December."
- 10x told her 15 early-access customers already hold POs. She expects 50 POs will be easy to fill and a queue for later releases to form.
- She advises early ordering: when she placed an early PO for Illumina's NovaSeq X Plus it still took six to nine months to get the box, so being early lets you "take advantage of the full tech cycle."
- Xenium's tech cycle was short: she bought about a year after its launch and "definitely won't be able to fully realize the value of that box" because she will retire it early, even if she does not buy an Atera, since customers will go elsewhere. "A lot of us got, frankly, a little bit burned on the Xenium."

### Xenium cannibalization and installed base

- Someone told her there are close to 500 Xeniums in the field (she found it high but notes three on the Mesa in San Diego alone). Therefore 50 Ateras will take a while to replace them, and capacity may consolidate initially.
- "The Xeniums are going to be dead as soon as all the mouse and panels come out." The transition will be slow because people finish existing projects, especially anything clinical-adjacent. At launch 10x offers only whole transcriptome and perhaps a multi-tissue panel; she does not know when custom panels arrive.
- She draws a parallel to sequencing: conservative clinical users still run the NovaSeq 6000 and "pay a premium for sequencing per base"; Xenium users will likewise pay a premium for worse data. She argued the NovaSeq X Plus "was [not] a significant upgrade" and "I could probably argue it was a little bit of a downgrade" in sequencing quality across sample types (her opinion). But Atera's data quality is also better, "a double reason to really transition quickly."

### Other Atera features

- **Base-by-base DNA/RNA sequencing claim:** She thinks this would be an extension assay (probes extending across a mutation). 10x talked about the same for Xenium two years ago "and they never really developed it," so "let's wait and see." Users at every Xenium user group meeting asked for somatic-mutation detection; it would be "quite a differentiating feature."
- **Proteomics:** She assumes 10x will roll out protein packages but was not advanced on Xenium. Some users run proteomics after Xenium on the same section, but registering images is an informatics burden.
- **Data output:** A bioinformatician at the launch was excited that Atera outputs files that are much easier to use downstream and work with open-access tools, reducing the need for instrument-specific expertise. She sees this as a value add (she is not a bioinformatician herself).
- **Automation:** Atera was built with microfluidics, so in theory it can perform onboard the workflow her technicians spend about 2.5 days on, but this is not implemented in the first launch and she does not know when it will be. Today, Xenium takes about three days of handling for two slides; onboard automation would reduce this to roughly an hour of slide handling, which she calls "a game changer." Histology (e.g. building tissue microarrays) remains labour-intensive and will limit filling capacity.

## Spatial competitors

- **Bruker CosMx:** "I think they're going to struggle." Her reasons, from direct experience: heavy turnover as the company was acquired out of bankruptcy by Bruker; leadership changes; "different sales rep every week, different FAS [field application scientist] every week"; commercialization challenges. "10x is much, much easier for me to work with. I can text my sales rep." The AtoMx software is hard to use and extract data from, and Atera's user-friendly exports widen the gap. She already tries not to sell CosMx to customers despite having access. On whether Bruker can gain a clinical presence: "I just think they're too hard to work with."
- **Stellaromics Pyxa (3D):** It allows thicker sections (about 20-100 microns, vs about 5 microns). It had a big AGBT presence. She sees it as niche and research-only; she cited a talk (using Bruker spatial) showing RNA is unevenly distributed within a cell, so thin slices may misrepresent gene expression, whereas proteins appeared more evenly distributed. She doubts it will "drive tons of business."

## Single cell outlook

- Single cell (e.g. Chromium) remains relevant for Perturb-seq (experiments that perturb genes in cell lines and read out effects) and large mechanism-focused projects such as the Arc Virtual Cell Atlas and collaborations with Ultima. Spatial is "very observational"; single cell allows manipulation and testing.
- Whole-transcriptome spatial will cannibalize some single cell work: more cells per slide, cheaper than capturing and sequencing single cells, though less sensitive. Some discovery projects (especially interesting tissues) will migrate, but "it will cannibalize some of the single cell work, but it's not going to end it."
- Her lab runs every scale of single cell project, mostly 3 Prime whole transcriptome, little probe-based Flex. Whole-transcriptome Perturb-seq screens are unlikely to move to spatial.
- Innovation continues in single cell epigenomics (hacking kits for CUT&RUN, ATAC-seq, histone marks) and single cell methylation, which cannot easily be done in probe-based spatial.

### Budget shifts and consumable dip

- Asked whether money shifts from single cell to spatial, she said "maybe, to some extent," but the question she gets more often is what to do with savings: "Are you going to do more sections... Or are you going to reserve that money and pay your technicians for an extra year?" Researchers may keep throughput flat and cut spend, hurting 10x consumable revenue.
- She says this happens with every higher-throughput, lower-cost sequencer: consumable spend dips in the short term until project sizes rise. She expects this here and "it may be even more dramatic" given funding stress.

## Roche Axelios, ctDNA and clinical sequencing

(ctDNA is circulating tumour DNA in blood, used for cancer detection and monitoring; MRD is minimal residual disease, tiny amounts of remaining cancer after treatment.)

### Duplex sequencing shifting the field

- The ctDNA field is moving fast because of duplex methods (reading both strands of each DNA molecule to error-correct and reach very low detection limits). Axelios has duplex sequencing; Ultima has ppmSeq. KJ: "People can do the same thing on Illumina" by sequencing individual strands. Illumina has no off-the-shelf approach that she is aware of, but homebrew methods have run for a couple of years.
- She expects this to lead to more sequencing: instead of making patient-specific panels (custom, time-intensive, expensive; she mentioned Twist as a panel maker) and sequencing very deep, users can run whole genome sequencing with sensitive duplex methods, "which is what Ultima is pushing." Spend shifts from customization to sequencing. "I think that will be adopted."
- She called it good for patients and the field: duplex reading of low-frequency mutations is "super cool in the clinical space" but applies less in research.

### Illumina's position

- "Illumina clearly still has the bulk of the clinical market. The clinical market is very risk averse and very slow to change. So I don't see them being impacted heavily by this initially."
- On competing with Ultima price pressure: Illumina has discounting strategies "but I don't know if they're good enough." It competes most in counting assays (e.g. single cell sequencing), pricing shorter-cycle kits more competitively so as not to lose that work to Ultima. Illumina seems not threatened on larger-cycle kits because it feels it has dominant share in clinical and whole genome sequencing (her interpretation).

### Axelios assessment

- **Speed:** She does not think Axelios is much faster. It holds a speed record but with modified protocols (shown at AGBT). On a NovaSeq X Plus turnaround "is really not going to be that different." Library prep is slow: Axelios uses linear amplification rather than traditional PCR, which is "an overnight library amplification" requiring extra PCR machines. The record used PCR-free prep, but ctDNA has too little material for PCR-free, so speed would not improve for ctDNA.
- **Bake-offs:** She has not seen a bake-off including Axelios. Roche's own mini bake-off (mixed tumour/normal samples at its spotlight) is promotional; she does not know if it is paradigm-shifting. Asked if "as good as Illumina" is good enough to switch, she said "No." Roche is counting on this being "the beginning of the tech development" and improving.
- **Modularity:** She was very excited about the Roche box in 2025, thinking it would be modular with dial-in output, but it is "clearly actually even less modular than existing boxes from Illumina Element" (Element Biosciences). As a core director with many small-output, fast-turnaround projects that want to pay high-output prices, she lost enthusiasm. She does not care about the super high accuracy because she does no tumour profiling.
- **Support concern:** Roche is a big company and her lab has trouble ordering simple library prep reagents (Roche owns KAPA). She worries Roche will not match the "concierge level support" she gets from Illumina, 10x, Element and other smaller firms.
- **Rapid diagnostics:** Axelios is very high throughput and not a clinical sequencer; economic use requires a large sample flow such as Foundation Medicine (Roche-owned, which says it will use Axelios) or a CRO. Adoption should come first in research, where users will find niches and cheaper creative uses; clinical deployment follows at an uncertain pace. She said adoption could make ctDNA work available to more hospitals and become standard of care "rather quickly" (hopeful view).
- **Modifications:** Axelios loses native DNA modification detection that ONT has; she thinks retaining native modification info with its expanded chemistry "seems like a heavy lift," and doubts clinical users use ONT/PacBio native methylation data anyway; these are not clinical boxes.

## Long reads and epigenomics

- ONT's epigenetic detection is not, in her view, the selling point; she thinks it is still assembly (de novo genome building) and growing use of longer reads for metagenomic/environmental DNA. She noted Illumina entered long reads with its "true path" offering (it cannot detect modifications) but she has seen no interest.
- UCSD is very epigenomic but not interested in native methylation on long-read machines; researchers focus on histone marks, chromatin structure and chromatin conformation (even some in spatial) and move bulk assays to single cell.
- **Illumina 5-base and TAPS-style methylation:** She believes Illumina's 5-base solution and a similar Watchmaker TAPS assay (converting methylated cytosines rather than unmethylated, as bisulfite does) capture variants and methylation together. She has two projects she is shopping for 5-base (one mainly interested in variants, one mainly in methylation). 5-base "is being priced very competitively right now." It may eat into whole-genome-only and methylation-only work since both can be had for "just a little bit more money," likely cheaper than separate targeted methylation or panels.

## Illumina at AACR 2026

- She attended a couple of limited events, including one at Illumina's headquarters for people in town for AACR, focused on multiomics and multiomics software integration/reporting. "Nothing new" on the data-generation or science side that she cared about.
- She sees academic resistance to company-run, closed pipelines versus open source; Illumina is making inroads but will not provide all its source code. Probably fine for the clinical testing market, but true multistage multiomics is still research-grade: "No one is doing a spatial single cell methylation and a whole genome for diagnostics."
- The moderator noted Illumina had nothing big at this meeting.

## Research demand and funding conditions

### Her lab's volumes (direct experience)

- Her sequencing runs in Q1 2026 were down about 20% versus Q4 2025. She cautioned that Q4 is seasonally very busy (people dump samples before holidays) and Q1 starts slowly. She did not compute year-over-year but suspects it is "not down that much." Moderator characterization of the quarter: "It looks yikes" (KJ).

### NIH funding

- KJ says people believe funding issues are solved because the budget was fully funded and flat, and indirect cost (IDC) cuts are off the table, but "the money is not coming out fast enough" and is lagging what should have been distributed this year.
- Multiyear funding takes about 20% of this year's budget to fund the next four years, so there is "effectively... a 15%-20% budget cut at NIH" (her estimate). Pay lines (grant funding score cutoffs) are very low and unclear, with "a lot of political decision-making."
- Researchers hoard money and stretch grants via no-cost extensions (NCEs), so a higher-throughput box may not raise consumable spend; money budgeted for spatial may fund a technician for an extra year.
- UCSD has about 16% less funding than in prior years (she does not know whether that measures grants or dollars). She thinks UCSD is not unique, and is unsure whether geographic redistribution of funds is happening. Researchers are turning to private, foundation, donor and California state money, but cannot make up the gap.
- Biotech industry customers are "not doing great either." Her 2026 plan: diversify her customer base; she does not expect NIH increases. She says the severity of cuts is "being glossed over in the public sphere."

### CRO competition

- Core facilities face growing competition from CROs (contract research organizations) as sequencing commoditizes and boxes get higher output, making it hard to control sample flow inside universities.
- She has lost some large, easy, high-throughput projects to CROs that undercut her rates (they obtain her rates and price just below). These projects "pay the bills"; cores are left with niche, challenging work, which they do collaboratively and often do not charge for. She says cores must adapt or "die off," and losing academic expertise would hurt the field because cores bring new users in and troubleshoot quality. She does not think sequencing can be fully outsourced.

## Closing views

- Her key areas to watch are spatial and MRD. "Even though there are spending issues right now, there's still a lot of exciting developments in science, and I just hope we can afford to keep those moving."
- She called nothing at AACR "earth shattering"; spatial was the biggest topic for her, since the meeting was otherwise too clinical. She noted genomic medicine still lags what is possible in research, hoping for standard of care in five years but "not going to hold my breath."
