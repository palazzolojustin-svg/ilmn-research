# Channel check with a genetics core lab manager in Arizona: falling Illumina volumes, Element AVITI24 adoption, funding pressure and Roche Axelios interest (Apr 2026)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-04-14_Manager_at_Genetics_Core_Lab_Sees_Funding_and_Competition_Challenges_Impacting_Sequencing.md` · Date: 2026-04-14 · AI-interviewer channel check transcript (published 16 Apr 2026); expert is a manager at a university genetics core lab in Arizona; expert name not given · expert call*

**Summary.** A manager of a university genetics core lab (an Illumina customer that has also bought an Element AVITI24) reports that its Illumina sequencing output fell about 15%-20% in Q1 2026 versus Q1 2025 and will likely end 2026 at about 80% of 2025. Its spending on Illumina next-generation sequencing consumables is down about 80%, which the expert attributes to shifting work to the Element platform. Illumina's annual February price increase (3%-5%) plus a 3%-5% tariff surcharge raised consumable costs, funding uncertainty and cheap contract research organizations (CROs) are hurting volumes, and the expert has no plans to buy new sequencers in the next 12 months. The expert still describes Illumina technology as "very proven and very highly favored" and sees Roche's pre-release Axelios platform as a possible future source of pressure.

## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

- **Role:** The expert manages a genetics service center (a "core lab", a shared facility that runs sequencing for other researchers) at a university in Arizona. The bio says the expert focuses on lab management and has autonomy over equipment decisions and planning of activities for sequencing instruments. Seniority is manager level.
- **Relationship:** Current Illumina customer and also an owner of a competitor instrument. This is direct, current experience, not a former-employee view.
- **Experience:** The lab has done next-generation sequencing (NGS, high-throughput DNA/RNA reading) "for many, many years," starting in the early 2000s, across several platforms, techniques, outputs and applications.
- **Customer base:** Mostly academic researchers (academic and applied research). Some clinical research. The lab used to do clinical diagnostics with NGS but no longer does. It also works with industry and pharmaceutical partners on request.
- **Instruments:** An Illumina NextSeq 550 (the lab has used the NextSeq 500 and 550 for many years). It recently retired its Illumina MiSeq. It now owns and operates an Element AVITI24 (a sequencer from competitor Element Biosciences).
- **Campus context:** Other core facilities on campus use Illumina sequencers as well as PacBio and Oxford Nanopore (ONT) sequencers, so the expert sees "a good amount of opportunity" for both Illumina and non-Illumina sequencing.

## Factors affecting sequencing volume and pipeline (Q1 2026)

- **Funding uncertainty:** Changes in funding available to researchers for large-scale, high-end, high-cost projects. Researchers may have funds but are less ready to spend them.
- **Source of the uncertainty:** The expert says it is mostly fear of reductions in federal research funding (grants and other resources). Researchers worry about obtaining and renewing funding and are being very selective with existing funds so that money remains available for the foreseeable future.
- **Contract research organizations (CROs):** Over several years there has been a large shift toward CROs offering sequencing at very competitive prices, including on products that outside organizations previously obtained less often. This affects sample quantities, project turnover and whether researchers choose the core lab over other labs and companies.
- **Instrument output limits:** The lab's sequencers have limited output capacity. When a project exceeds that capacity, the lab does all possible steps in-house and then helps researchers reach a higher-output platform.

## Sequencing output trends

| Measure | Expert's figure | Comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Q1 2026 output in gigabases (billions of DNA letters read) on Illumina instruments | Down about 15%-20% | vs Q1 2025 |
| Versus expectations | "Somewhat falling below" expectations | Expert's own plan |
| Full year 2026 output on Illumina instruments | About 80% of the prior year | vs 2025 |
| Outlook vs 90 days ago | About the same | Neither more nor less optimistic |

- **Drivers of the Q1 decline:** The expert says output is "somewhat decreasing," partly driven by availability of funds and by the prevalence of CROs over academic core facilities. The raw number of projects has decreased, so output decreased in turn.
- **Drivers of the full-year decline:** Fewer projects coming to the facility, fewer projects being funded, and possibly fewer samples run per project to keep costs down.

## Consumables and reagents

- **Spending trend:** Spending on Illumina high-throughput sequencing consumables decreased in Q1 2026 versus Q1 2025. The expert estimated next-gen consumables spending is down "about 80%."
- **Why:** Fewer Illumina runs, because the lab can use its alternate platform (the Element AVITI24) instead of relying solely on Illumina. The expert says the decrease in reagent purchases "has to do with adoption of an alternate sequencing platform." (This is the expert's direct experience.) Note the expert's output decline (15%-20%) is far smaller than the spending decline (80%), and the expert did not reconcile the two.
- **Pricing (past 90 days):** Prices increased. Two components:
  - An annual increase in Illumina consumables across the board, taken in February (within the last 60-90 days). The expert believes it is 3%-5% per year.
  - A tariff surcharge on top of consumable cost, roughly 3%-5%. The expert stresses this is "not from Illumina or by Illumina's design" but raised the purchase price of Illumina consumables.
- **Inventory:** "Sufficient." The lab stocks only as needed and keeps no uncommitted stock of Illumina reagents.
- **Lead times:** No change in the past 90 days for the reagents it orders; same delivery timeframes as past orders.

## Competitive landscape and platform sentiment

- **Equipment pricing:** The expert has not priced sequencing equipment in the past 90 days, so cannot comment on price concessions or incentives from Illumina or competitors.
- **Singular:** The expert has seen the Singular instrument "reduced in favor in the marketplace due to lack of adoption."
- **Roche:** "A good amount of interest" in what Roche will bring out, but it is not yet available for purchase.
- **Ultima:** "A good amount of interest," but the expert does not think it is widely adopted, at least domestically in the US.
- **Illumina:** Labs remain "still very enthusiastic and trusting of Illumina platforms." The expert says the technology is considered "very proven and very highly favored."
- **Cost-per-base caveat:** The best cost per base (cost per DNA letter read) with Illumina comes from running the highest-output, highest-capacity sequencers at full capacity, which is not attainable for every lab with an Illumina platform. The expert sees "a bit of a divergence" between how cost-effective Illumina sequencing can be and how realistic that is per facility.
- **Element:** "A good amount of enthusiasm and interest in Element sequencing."

## Equipment purchasing plans

- No plans to buy new sequencing equipment in the next 12 months, "mainly due to lack of funding for capital equipment."
- Plans unchanged over the past 90 days.

## New products and competitor developments

- **Illumina products of interest:** The expert has been following Illumina's participation in single-cell and multiome markets (assays reading many cell-level or multiple molecular layers), calling it "an interesting and value added component" for a company that has done mainly standard high-throughput sequencing.
- **TruSeq genome:** The expert does not expect to use it and has "not heard much" about it. The lab does little whole human genome sequencing, so it is off the expert's radar.
- **Competitor items watched closely:** Element's multiomic and "Teton" assays, Element's spatial assays, and Roche's SBX sequencing technology.
- **Roche Axelios:** The expert thinks it "could put some pressure in the future" but is not putting "a ton of pressure yet" because it is pre-release. Perhaps interest and discussion alone put "a bit of pressure on Illumina." The expert cites as potentially disruptive what Roche is discussing: the cost per base, the sequencing time and the potential for reusing flow cells (the consumable slides on which sequencing occurs). The expert expects Illumina to be well aware and looks forward to seeing its response. This is speculation based on published claims, not experience with the product.
- **SomaLogic:** Illumina recently acquired SomaLogic (a proteomics company, which measures proteins). The expert finds it "an interesting branch out" and notes other sequencing companies are moving into multiomics (combined genomics and proteomics readouts). The expert is "not certain" it will make them use an Illumina proteomics assay: "It remains to be seen."

## Customer support and scale issues

- The expert is satisfied with Illumina support, describing the people as "really great, kind, and helpful professionals."
- One critical point: in modern NGS the lab is "no longer considered a big player" because it does not run the highest-output platforms, and it finds "it very hard to remain relevant and competitive" with its Illumina platforms.
- Groups with larger consumables budgets seem to get much better pricing through volume discounts. For a smaller facility "it is hard to achieve the best cost per base value, both on our reagent and instrumentation side."

## Expert's view on future sequencing trends

- There was for a while a real effort to reach the lowest cost per base, but the expert believes "there must be a floor, a bottom," and says "just racing to the lowest cost-per-base doesn't seem to be a sustainable way to generate profit."
- The trend now is for manufacturers to work out what else their platform can do: multiomic datasets, using the camera in alternate ways, reducing consumable flow so components can be reused, and shipping consumables at room temperature (which the expert says Illumina has innovated on in later platforms).
- The expert also says affordable sequencing at CROs is "changing the landscape quite a bit" and calls it "the trend."

## Fact versus opinion and caveats

- **Direct experience:** the lab's own output, spending, pricing, inventory and lead times; Element AVITI24 adoption; funding conditions at the lab; purchase plans; support quality.
- **Estimates and hearsay:** the 3%-5% price figures are "I believe" estimates; views on Singular, Ultima, Roche Axelios, SomaLogic and market trends are impressions, not firsthand.
- **Single-source caution:** this is one small academic core lab, and the 80% consumables drop reflects a platform switch at that lab, so it may not be representative of Illumina's broader customer base.
