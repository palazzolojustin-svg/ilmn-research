# Expert call with former Illumina senior manager of commercial enablement on competition, strategy and market-share erosion (interview 8 Jun 2026)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-06-08_Former_Senior_Manager_at_Illumina_Inc._Believes_Competitive_Pressures_and_Strategic_Challe.md` · Date: 2026-06-08 · Expert-network transcript (published 10 Jun 2026); expert is a former Senior Manager / Associate Director of Commercial Enablement at Illumina (6 years, 2017-2023), business-led perspective; interviewer not named · expert call*

**Summary.** A former Illumina commercial-enablement leader (left Dec 2023) argues Illumina's share of the sequencing instrument market has fallen from about 92% in mid-2017 to roughly 70% and will keep eroding, possibly to below 60%, as Element, Ultima and Roche gain ground. He sees Illumina's pivot to software, data and "bio-insights" as slow and poorly executed, calls the current executive team a "maintenance" team, and names China, the closed-ecosystem model and strategic vacillation as the main headwinds. He is bearish on PacBio and Oxford Nanopore, expecting AI or acquisition to replace or absorb long-read sequencing. Much of the content is opinion and recollection, and some of his figures are unverified or inconsistent.

## Expert background and how to weigh the evidence

**Who the expert is.** The expert is a former Senior Manager of Commercial Enablement at Illumina, and earlier an Associate Director in the same function. They worked at Illumina for about six years, from 2017 to 2023 (the bio says they left in December 2023). Both roles were global. They have over 13 years of experience across biotech, cloud software and consumer electronics start-ups, and now work as a freelance consultant. [Expert: former Illumina senior manager, Jun 2026]

**What they did at Illumina.**
- In the first role, they managed a team of field application scientists (technical specialists who support customers) and API consultants working in informatics (the software side of sequencing). The scope covered the whole workflow: sample prep, LIMS (laboratory information management systems, which track samples and data in a lab), secondary analysis (turning raw sequencer output into genome data) and tertiary analysis (interpreting that data).
- In the second role, which lasted about three years, they managed a team handling global tenders, RFIs and RFPs (formal requests from customers, often governments, for information and proposals), and also commercialised professional services for informatics.
- The bio says they launched Illumina's first comprehensive proposal-management program (bids, tenders, RFI/RFP and security questionnaires). It also says they launched a commercial professional-services offering for informatics, which raised "stickiness" among early-adopter customers.
- They also have experience with the sequencing machines, both benchtop and high-throughput.

**Limits of their view.**
- Their direct Illumina experience ended in 2023, about two and a half years before the call. Statements about current leadership, morale, recent product news and 2026 events are second-hand, from public information or from "what I've heard".
- They say they do not come from a scientific background but from software and commercial work. They give no specifics on AI, drug discovery or the underlying science of sequencing.
- They repeatedly say they do not know exact market-share figures, especially for private companies.
- Several statements conflict with each other or appear loosely recalled (see "Inconsistencies and items to verify" at the end).

## The state of the sequencing market

### Three big shifts [Expert, 8 Jun 2026]

**1. More competition.** Over the past six to eight years the competitive landscape has changed significantly. Existing players include PacBio (PACB) and Oxford Nanopore. Newer entrants include Element, Roche and Ultima. The expert says "some of those players could be really direct threats to Illumina, and that has already been proven in just the decline in market share."

**2. A pivot from "boxes" to other businesses.** "Boxes" is the expert's term for sequencing instruments. Across the industry, and now at Illumina specifically, companies are moving from selling instruments to data management, multi-omics (measuring several types of biological molecule, such as DNA and proteins, together) and data insights. Illumina's department for this is called "bio-insights." The expert says this has been "very recently" the direction at Illumina, and that the same push toward multi-omics is visible at many players.

**3. Technology companies entering biotech.** The old joke was that there is little tech in biotech, which the expert says is becoming less true. This has been happening for at least five years. Examples cited are AWS with its Genomics CLI, NVIDIA's life-sciences division and Azure's life-sciences offerings. Later in the call they add Google Cloud (a genomics toolset and a life-sciences API), Oracle (clinical-trial data) and AlphaFold.

### Illumina's market share trajectory [Expert]

The table below summarises the share figures the expert gave for Illumina's share of sequencing instruments. The expert stresses that these refer to boxes (instruments) only, not informatics or other downstream products.

| Point in time | Illumina share of instruments | Expert's comment |
|---|---|---|
| Mid-2017 | 92% | "Effectively a near monopoly" |
| Now (Jun 2026) | About 70%, "maybe a little less" | A "pretty dramatic shift", "sub-70%" |
| Expert's expectation | Could fall to below 60% | "Inevitable"; would take "several drastic things" to hold share |

- The expert describes the fall as "a 20%+ decline in market share over eight years," or about eight and a half years.
- At one point they instead say Illumina was at 92% in "Q3 of 2021, or let us call it back half of 2021." This conflicts with the mid-2017 date used elsewhere.
- Even at about 70%, the expert says Illumina "still has a very dominant position within the market."
- The expert expects erosion "in the short, medium, and long-term." They say it "won't take that much. It will just take a couple of years."
- The expert notes that sales cycles lengthen as machines get higher-throughput, so the share shifts will take time to show up in numbers.
- Share figures for Element and Ultima are not known because they are private. Roche may not break out sequencing numbers within its diagnostics (DX) division. The expert says outsiders can only estimate.

### Market size [Expert, from reading, not direct knowledge]
- The sequencing total addressable market (TAM, the total annual spend available) is "approximately $15 billion-16 billion," which the expert calls "not that much, but not nothing."
- Illumina's 2026 revenue forecast is "around $4.5 billion," which the expert says is "less than 1/3 of the overall TAM."

### Drivers of market growth [Expert]
- **Clinical sequencing.** Historically the market was heavy on research and development and academic RUO (research use only, meaning not for diagnostic use). Clinical momentum is now a big driver.
- **Oncology and MRD.** The expert names oncology, especially MRD (minimal residual disease, detecting leftover cancer after treatment), as a potential driver, citing "a new MRD detection workflow for oncology" in the last couple of weeks. They call this "fraught" and "risky, especially for Illumina." Any disease prediction needs "a really solid plan of how to protect against your false positives."
- **GRAIL as a cautionary example.** They cite GRAIL, the early-cancer-detection company Illumina acquired in the second half of 2021. They say it "turned out to be not a good acquisition exactly because of that, the false positives and a lot of other legal issues."
- **Falling cost and commoditised technology.** The cost of sequencing has gone down. SBS (sequencing by synthesis, Illumina's core chemistry) is "now effectively commoditized."
- **Compute.** There is now compute power to run high-throughput sequencing at good accuracy, and to do something with the results. The "holy grail" is "from sample to answer." The sample part has always existed, but "the answer part of the workflow and the insight and the action part of it has never quite been there."
- **Long-term view.** On a three-to-five-year (mid-term) and five-to-ten-year (long-term) horizon, the expert says "the long-term future is not in biotech per se. It's in biopharma and the tech side of the house."

## Long-read sequencing and its competitors

**Context.** Long-read sequencing reads much longer stretches of DNA at once than Illumina's short-read approach. The main long-read players are PacBio and Oxford Nanopore. [Expert]

**Basic view.**
- There "has always been a need" for long-read. The expert notes that "13, 14 years ago" Illumina tried to acquire PacBio and the deal "did not go through," and says this shows Illumina saw the need.
- Illumina's ex-chief commercial officer went to PacBio to become the new COO, the expert says (Nanopore is mentioned in the same passage).
- Despite the need, "the numbers are showing that places like PACB and Nanopore are just not profitable."

**PacBio.**
- The expert cites the stock price at "$1" and market cap at "$400 million" (their figures, not checked against any market data here), calling it "clearly a niche player."
- They are not optimistic about PacBio or Nanopore: "It's just too small."

**Oxford Nanopore.**
- The expert does not "see Nanopore as a company" but as "an academic offshoot of Oxford that has really, really promising technology, but is not managed as a commercial company."
- It is "managed as an R&D quasi-company with a primary business model in reagent rental." Commercialisation "might be tricky."

**Size of the long-read need.** The expert's estimate is that 80%-85% of customers are fine with short-read. Perhaps 10%-15% doing specialised or high-throughput projects truly need a long-read tool. Elsewhere they say the gap AI would need to fill might be about 5% of a project.

**Their prediction.**
- Over "the medium-term, not even in the long-term," AI will "replace" the need for dedicated long-read. For example, AI could help fill gaps "without buying another machine, another PACB machine."
- The alternative outcome is acquisition by a larger company such as a biopharma or tech company.
- They see "much more promise" in start-ups such as Element and Ultima.
- Bottom line: "A need, but not big enough, and not needed enough by a majority of the customer base to really warrant several individual companies."
- They doubt long-read is "a really sustainable standalone business." They say this is "not quite here yet" but expect AI sophistication to help "in the next couple of years."

## AI in sequencing and Illumina's AI strategy

**AI's wider impact [Expert, speculation].**
- AI will have "really significant" impact on sequencing and biotech generally. AI is already gaining a footprint in drug discovery, with Schrödinger and AlphaFold named as examples of heavy-compute work that AI will affect.
- Drug discovery is "probably ahead of sequencing in terms of utilizing this technology" because the drug-discovery workflow is so long. The expert says they are not a drug-discovery specialist.

**Illumina's AI strategy.**
- The expert answers: "I'm not sure there is one."
- Illumina has "for the last 28 years since its inception" been about boxes and core consumables (the reagent kits customers must buy for each run). There have been "dabblings in LIMS and some acquisitions here and there."
- The bio-insights pivot is "very recent, like matter of weeks." The expert is "not really sure how much AI is part of that strategy."
- Illumina has recognised the need to grow beyond boxes because that business is "relatively saturated." It is largely about upgrades, "like a NovaSeq to an NovaSeq X or from a mid-throughput to a high throughput." Not capturing downstream workflow areas is "a missed opportunity."
- The expert thinks "biotech and certainly Illumina tends to be a couple of years behind the curve." The commercial value of data management has been recognised "for at least 10 years." They call Illumina "a relatively conservative company" that is "not a software specialist or a data specialist and trying to be," and say it will be "a couple of years behind of whatever the forefront of AI is." From personal experience, even the term "cloud software" was "recognized/not recognized" internally.
- Opportunity: "AI could be a huge opportunity in the future" and bio-insights and informatics "could be a really, really big opportunity."
- There is "quite a large team internally" on software and bio-insights. But strategic recognition differs from execution. Software, AI and data need "a completely different workforce and a completely different skill set" from boxes and reagents. Pivoting the workforce is "a much, much harder transition."
- Timing: the expert would not expect this to "pop up in the numbers for years, at least two to three years minimum, if that."
- The expert wishes there were more AI discussion, since tech companies are "much more aggressive with integrating the bio part of biotech into the tech" than bio companies are with integrating tech.

## Illumina's growth: stagnation and leadership

**Revenue history [Expert, opinion].**
- The expert says the 2026 forecast of $4.5 billion "was the forecast in 2021, 2022, 2023, 2024, and 2025." They conclude Illumina has been in "stagnation and recovery mode for five+ years." Even allowing for the COVID boost, it is "still just flat or net negative or incremental growth."
- Later they say 2026 guidance is "4%-6% growth year over year" but because Illumina has been in recovery mode the growth is "not real." (They do not cite where the 4%-6% comes from.)
- Getting "back into that $4.5 billion top line... is going back to mid-2021." Internal expectation in 2021, and perhaps the market's, was "high fours, fives," and now "low fives, mid fives." It "just hasn't gotten there."

**Leadership.**
- There is currently no chief commercial officer. The CEO, referred to as "Jacob", is the interim CCO.
- The ex-CCO came from Exact, "one of Illumina's customers," stayed "about a year and a half," and left "a couple months ago."
- The expert says a CCO is needed to work with product, R&D and bio-insights "to really execute on the commercial plan."
- The current executive team is "almost 100% completely different... versus just two years ago," and "hired for a very specific reason... to maintain, not for growth." "You need a different set of people in growth phase."
- Maintenance "has gotten harder and harder."
- Product and R&D people cannot be expected to think about the commercial side.
- Leaders must also stay long enough to execute. Biotech "is still not really that commercial."

## Headwinds facing Illumina [Expert]

### Headwind 1: China and MGI/BGI
- China has always been Illumina's smallest of four regions (North America, EMEA, APAC excluding China, and China). The expert estimates it as "maybe 4% or 5%" of revenue, "something very small."
- Illumina was blocked on a Chinese government list. The expert believes it has since been unblocked from that list but "is still being held on this unreliable entities list" (the Chinese government's list of foreign companies it can restrict).
- Practical effect: Illumina can technically sell in China but needs approval "on an individual basis," which is "a huge administrative hassle."
- MGI (a Chinese sequencing company) has benefited. Given the history of the Complete Genomics acquisition and the lawsuits, "the clear winner right now is MGI." BGI is "a big player in China." The expert describes both administrative and competitive headwinds.
- They do not know when Illumina will be off the list. These issues are "structural" and hard to fix: "It's just harder to partner with the Chinese government."

### Headwind 2: closed vs open ecosystem
- Illumina has always sold a closed ecosystem: instrument plus reagents plus software. BaseSpace Sequence Hub (BSSH, the secondary analysis tool) has been included for 13-14 years, with a certain amount of storage with each instrument purchase.
- Some customers like the "seamless" workflow when it works. Many see it as a hindrance.
- Element and Ultima both advertise "open platform." The expert compares it to "an Apple versus Google type of thing." To keep a closed ecosystem "it really has to work seamlessly." The weakest part "by far, is the software."
- Open platforms could let software be developed faster and in a more agile way, with biweekly sprints in the SaaS style. Software development at Illumina "has just been really difficult," even for RUO. The expert is unsure why, offering the closed ecosystem, lack of emphasis, or workforce skills as possibilities.
- Customers will not manufacture instruments or reagents, but can affect software, which often needs heavy customisation. LIMS "you could spend years with one customer just on professional services and customization." If customers can do this themselves or hire their own professional-services team, "it just makes it just so much more attractive," which Illumina does not really offer.
- Governments often want a single vendor in tenders, so a preferred professional-services vendor is "a big opportunity in the future."
- "Software developers are a pretty common skill set," so the open-platform advantage "could be easily overcome" by competitors. It is "a little early to tell" how customers will respond.
- Part B: if more customers choose open platforms for software, this "could potentially affect your core consumables attach rate" (how often customers buy Illumina's reagents with each machine), "definitely in the midterm."

### Headwind 3: unclear strategy and vacillation
- Roche, Element, Ultima and PacBio each have a clear story. Roche (per the expert) bought a sequencing company, sees SBS as mature and is doing something else, and "it doesn't have to be perfect. It just has to be good enough." Element has low-price benchtop sequencers and an open platform. Ultima uses new wafer technology (the expert does not know the details), goes after the high end for population genomics ("PopGen") and also offers an open platform.
- For Illumina: "I still think of Illumina as an instrument company and nothing else." The strategy "has vacillated so much over the past eight to 10 years that it's really just incremental things going on."
- The recently closed SomaLogic acquisition is "a statement of a recognition that proteomics has to be in the mix, that there are limitations to genomics." The expert says proteomics is scientifically "more interesting, more comprehensive."
- There have been "at least a dozen acquisitions over the past 10 years." The most successful was DRAGEN (hardware acceleration, "a chip embedded within an instrument," not software).
- The informatics and software strategy has "vacillated many times." The expert has "lost count." Repeated layoffs and rehiring make it "very difficult for the internal workforce to handle and to execute." They call the lack of a seriously executed strategy "a very big headwind."

### Headwind 4: morale and talent
- "Morale is not great." Things offered to employees have been taken away. Talent may go elsewhere, especially in San Diego, where Element, PacBio and others recruit from the same pool. The expert calls this "more minor" and "just my opinion."

## Competitors in detail [Expert]

**Element Biosciences.**
- Sells benchtop sequencers at lower price points and higher volume. The expert calls this "smart."
- Has "already commercialized their IVD systems and shipped a couple hundred units" (IVD means in-vitro diagnostic, cleared for clinical use). Founded around five to six years ago, it is "the furthest along" of Element, Ultima and Roche.
- Staffed by many ex-Illumina people (the CEO "used to be on the clinical side of Illumina"; a VP of Informatics is ex-Illumina).
- Does not have to offer a complete workflow; the "open platform" is "a huge advantage."
- Forecast: "I could see a situation in which Element is in hundreds of millions of dollars top line. Not right now, it's very early, but... very within reach." Could reach "low double digits" market share, which "would be really significant, really impressive." Expected to cut into Illumina's box share and benchtop segment.

**Ultima Genomics.**
- Targets the high end. The expert recalls a rack rate (undiscounted list price) of "$1 million to $2 million," about the NovaSeq X price point.
- Per what the expert has "heard," it has installed machines at several KOLs (key opinion leaders, influential academics) and is doing bake-offs (head-to-head comparisons). Proof-of-concept results will decide its fate; "it's really too early."
- Could cut into NovaSeq X, and perhaps HiSeq (older, "not that relevant"). Volume at the high end will be lower than low/mid throughput, but at higher dollars. At that end, the key is consumables pull rather than machine price.
- Different effect from Element: Ultima "could really cut into the core consumables, and the consumables is really high margin, and it's a printer and ink cartridge model." Probably a smaller share than Element.
- Expert's reasoning: biotech adoption flows from academia to commercial, not the reverse.

**Roche.**
- "Super early." The expert says Roche "just announced officially their commercialization recently at AGBT" (a genomics conference). Pricing is "pretty competitive" per what they hear.
- Roche's advantage: sequencing is "a very small part of their DX division," so it need not be a money maker. Roche has room for price moves and business-model flexibility that Illumina lacks because sequencing "is the business" for Illumina.
- Research customers are budget-sensitive. With "good-enough technology" and competitive pricing, Roche "could make some inroads," even though clinical customers are sticky.

**Illumina's response and its limits.**
- Clinical sequencing is stickier because "once you have a system in there, it's a lot harder to rip out than research customers." The expert says Illumina has therefore pushed clinical more aggressively over the last two to four years, which "I think that's smart."

## What Illumina should do [Expert]

- Maintain the core boxes and consumables business ("that is the bread and butter") and "get really aggressive" about it.
- Lean into software and data: offer one workflow of instrument, reagents and software "in as seamless of a way as possible." It "is not going to be perfect." This needs a different skill set.
- Competition may come from tech companies (AWS, NVIDIA, Azure, AlphaFold, Google Cloud, Oracle), which biopharma recognises but biotech is slower to see.
- Relying on boxes and reagents alone will be "very hard to maintain." The expert doubts Illumina leads in reagents: "I think QIAGEN is a really recognized leader, or one of the leaders in reagents."
- Pivot the workforce deliberately, which the expert does not see happening in the near term.
- Element and Ultima are "being smarter" by not claiming a complete workflow or bio-insights; being both hardware- and software-focused "is really hard."
- Hire a chief commercial officer and commercially focused leaders, and keep them in place long enough to execute.
