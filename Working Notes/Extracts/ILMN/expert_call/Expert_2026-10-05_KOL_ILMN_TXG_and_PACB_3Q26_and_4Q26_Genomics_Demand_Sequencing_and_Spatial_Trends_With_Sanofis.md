# Sanofi Head of Genomics on sequencing, single-cell, long-read and spatial demand (Guidepoint expert call, Oct 2026)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-05_KOL_ILMN_TXG_and_PACB_3Q26_and_4Q26_Genomics_Demand_Sequencing_and_Spatial_Trends_With_Sanofis.md` · Date: 2026-10-05 · Guidepoint expert call; moderator Andrew Carmen; expert Dr. Seth Garren, Senior Principal Scientist and Head of Genomics at Sanofi (customer viewpoint) · expert call*

**Summary.** A current biopharma customer describes accelerating use of Illumina NovaSeq X consumables, driven by 10x Genomics Flex v2 (Apex) and CRISPR Perturb-seq experiments of 1 to 8 million cells, with sequencing spend about 25% higher year over year and reagent budget exhausted for the first time. He sees single-cell as the new default, is evaluating a 2027 long-read purchase (PacBio vs. Oxford Nanopore), has preordered two Atera spatial systems, and views AXELIOS SBX as a cost threat that is slowed by eight-year depreciation cycles. He believes vendor end-to-end workflows are largely unusable because of pharma data-security rules, and that AI-driven demand for proprietary data has not yet reached his organization.

# Sanofi Head of Genomics: sequencing, single-cell, long-read and spatial demand

## Expert background and basis of the views

- **Who:** Dr. Seth Garren is Senior Principal Scientist and Head of Genomics at Sanofi, a large pharmaceutical company. The call was run by Guidepoint (an expert-network firm) and moderated by Andrew Carmen on Oct 5, 2026. The expert had spoken to Guidepoint before ("once again"; a previous transcript is referenced).
- **Relationship to Illumina:** He is a **current customer**. He buys NovaSeq X sequencing kits, 10x Genomics reagents, and spatial and long-read products. He is not a former employee or competitor.
- **Role:** He works in the research arm, mainly preclinical research (early lab work before human trials). His team generates "high-dimensional data" (data measuring thousands of genes or features per sample). Over the past year the team also took on complex in vitro modeling (lab-grown tissue-like models). This supports the NAMs initiative (new approach methodologies, aimed at reducing reliance on animal models) and gives more complex target differentiation.
- **Self-description:** "We are early adopters of new sequencing technologies in single cell bulk and spatial space," and he routinely advises stakeholders tracking these markets.
- **Direct experience vs. speculation:** Usage, budgets, spending and purchase plans are firsthand [Expert: Sanofi head of genomics, Oct 2026]. Views on Illumina's strategy, AXELIOS's competitive impact, industry-wide AI demand and Pyxa are opinion or inference, and he says so (for example "that remains to be seen"). He says he is still educating himself on long-read trade-offs.
- **Compliance note:** The moderator asked him to avoid comment on stocks, valuation, NDA material and material nonpublic information.

## Illumina sequencing volumes and NovaSeq X purchasing

### What is driving demand
- Garren's internal sequencing use "has been accelerating this past year." The driver is adoption of **10x Genomics Flex v2 (also called Apex)**, a single-cell RNA profiling kit, combined with **Perturb-seq** (CRISPR gene-editing experiments read out by single-cell sequencing).
- As CRISPR use rose, demand for experiments of **1 million to 8 million cells** grew versus prior years. These large experiments mean he is buying more **NovaSeq X 25B kits** (flow cells and reagent kits rated at about 25 billion reads) than before.

### Spend versus volume
- Previously the team bought a larger number of smaller kits; now it buys a small number of larger kits. Nominal dollar spend is "might be slightly higher," but sequencing volume is "definitely increasing."
- Larger kits lower the cost per read, which partly explains the pattern. The team is also running protocols more often, with runs "colliding a lot at the end of the year."
- When the moderator suggested 5-10% growth, Garren put total sequencing spend at "about 25% increase over last year."
- Consumables for the team now span cell culture plus 10x plus Illumina purchases, because the CRISPR work was added on top of existing mechanism-of-action and disease-profiling projects. The team that formerly bought mostly cell-culture reagents now buys all three.

### Installed instruments
- Sanofi's team has **two older NovaSeq 6000** instruments that are off service contract but still used for ad hoc "walk-up" work, such as small single-cell readouts.
- The current workhorse is **one NovaSeq X (Plus)**, which can run two 25B flow cells in tandem. That has been enough throughput for the team.
- A different Sanofi department doing secretome profiling (measuring secreted proteins, using Olink) outgrew capacity and **bought its own NovaSeq X**, which it runs constantly. Garren says the team's demand was driven by Perturb-seq and the other department's by Olink.

### Justifying the NovaSeq X Plus purchase
- Capex governance (internal approval for capital equipment) resisted because two NovaSeq 6000s were still within their depreciation cycle (the period over which an asset's cost is written off).
- The winning argument was cost: sequencing 25 billion reads on two NovaSeq 6000s versus one NovaSeq X Plus. The X Plus came to **about one-third (about 33%) of the price**. The team also argued it anticipated "an explosion in sequencing volume due to increasing numbers of single cells."
- He says each new instrument generation significantly drops sequencing cost, "balanced against the capex inertia from depreciation budgeting." Sanofi's internal finance depreciation period is **about eight years**, long for a sequencing platform.

### Larger 35B flow cell
- The moderator noted a 35B (35 billion reads) option. Garren said Illumina has described its output only in vague terms, and the team has **not yet scaled up** to the higher-output kit, "though we might." The team designs mainly around the 25B kit until it reallocates budget. He tries to track announcements, and newer sequencers are "more of a capex commitment."

## 10x Genomics and single-cell

- **Dominance:** 10x is the team's "workhorse." Outside occasional bulk RNA-seq and whole-genome experiments, "over 90%" of usage relates to the Flex v2/Apex kit.
- **Growth:** 10x utilization has accelerated because CRISPR validation was added. Previously the work was mostly mechanism-of-action (MOA) and disease-profiling projects.
- **Default method:** Single-cell Flex has replaced bulk RNA-seq as his "bread and butter" and the "new default project." Other approaches need a bespoke argument: "the culture is somewhat shifted to single cell until a reason to do something else."
- **Share of activity:** Over half of his own activities are single-cell. By spending, "pretty much half of it is pure single cell," split between preclinical project support and target validation. The rest covers spatial, complex modeling and bulk whole-genome.
- **Further growth:** The big upside is if translation and development groups adopt it more, or if "reverse translation" (using patient data to inform discovery) expands. He sees "no shortage of appetite for single-cell data." Sanofi is trying to collect and centralize it, and external atlas initiatives are drawing interest. Much bulk RNA data "has largely been harvested for the obvious target," so the field is moving to higher-dimensional data like single-cell.
- **Next year (2027):** The team will go into "combinatoric validations" (several targets perturbed in the same cell), which will have a "fairly aggressive" sequencing requirement. This year's pilots are expected to become larger projects.
- **Other modalities:**
  - Interested in surfaceome profiling (cell-surface proteins) but has not chosen a modality.
  - Some interest in DNA profiling; he "keeps flirting" with Mission Bio but may outsource, as it is a niche application outside normal scope.
  - ATAC (chromatin accessibility) and methylation have not become major decision drivers, though "that can always change" because pharma storytelling depends on communal comfort with a dimension of biology.
  - **Isoforms** (alternative versions of a gene's RNA) are newly relevant; "in the past, splicing was just something people ignored."
  - Single-cell isoform work was tried and exited. The team had a well-based full-length single-cell system (ICELL8) and discontinued it for lack of a use case, though that was a gene-counting context. Bulk splicing is done now. Hunting new fusion proteins or tumor-dependent isoforms could bring it back, but it is "not as front and center as like Perturb-seq or Iso-Seq."

## Budget and cost pressure

- "This is the first year we've run out of reagent budget. That's never happened before," attributed to Perturb-seq growth.
- "Everything is getting more expensive," he said, in response to a question on inflation.

## Analysis bottleneck and AI-assisted coding

- Compared with the prior call, the biggest change is increased adoption of Claude "vibe coding" (AI-assisted coding). He cites two effects, both anecdotal from his own observation:
  1. It accelerates computational biologists by removing tedious data wrangling and image formatting.
  2. It lets end users query data themselves (for example look up a gene or zoom into cell resolution), cutting email back-and-forth with computational biologists. Team members run routine Seurat-type analyses by asking Claude.
- Effect on demand: both directions. Projects are bigger with more analysis, and more people take projects end-to-end. His department's framing is "accelerate comp bio and democratize the user."
- Caveat: sustainability depends on AI cost. "Assuming the token prices don't skyrocket, we'll probably try going in that direction."

## Long-read sequencing (PacBio, Oxford Nanopore)

- **Trigger:** In 2026 Sanofi received multiple requests from neurology and rare-disease projects for isoform profiling.
- **Current approach:** Projects are outsourced to external providers, some to PacBio and some to ONT (Oxford Nanopore). Sanofi owns a PacBio system in its CMC (chemistry, manufacturing and controls) division, at a different site, so the workflow is "ad hoc and piecemeal."
- **In-house hardware:** One Vega (smaller, for pilots) and one Revio (larger throughput). Which is used depends on project scale and whether Kinnex chemistry (a PacBio kit that boosts throughput) is used. Aggressive projects move to Revio.
- **Plan:** Evaluate internalizing long-read, deciding between PacBio, ONT or others. A capex pitch is planned **in 2027**, after outsourced projects serve as a pilot to build a processing pipeline.
- **Preference:** There is "a lot of prejudice in favor of" Revio, but the comparison has been "apples and oranges," and he needs to learn the trade-offs. This is the first time he has seriously considered pitching for one.
- **Coexistence:** Illumina plus ONT or Illumina plus PacBio is the most complementary. Stacking Illumina with SBX, or PacBio with ONT, is "a little redundant."
- **Epigenetic (methylation) readout:** The ability of PacBio and ONT to retain methylation information is a potential advantage over AXELIOS if prioritized, but it "hasn't been high on the list" for his epigenomic profiling.

## AXELIOS (Roche SBX) as a competitor

- **First impression:** The numbers make "a very compelling case for adoption," but industry inertia from capex and depreciation budgets blunts it.
- **Framing:** "The AXELIOS is not competing with the NovaSeq of today. They're really competing with whatever Illumina will have on the menu by the time the existing depreciation budgets would loosen up." Buying on cheaper sequencing alone is a hard argument "unless the volumes become prohibitive."
- **Nature of product:** He sees SBX as a short-read alternative ("a long, short read rather than a short, long read"). It is compatible with 10x workflows and cheaper per kit to hit the same read count. Long-read use cases benefit from longer reads, so it is "less likely to eat the lunch of the other two traditional platforms."
- **Economics cited:** "You can get like 40 billion reads for like a tenth of the price." This is his best use case, if his NovaSeq X reaches end of life or volumes get very aggressive. Eight-year depreciation cancels the cost difference for now.
- **General principle:** At any moment some new offering will have better metrics, but superior metrics alone do not justify "ripping out everything."
- **Buying criteria (SBX vs. Illumina):** Cost and sequencing volume are top. A cheap sequencer capping at 10 billion reads would not work; it needs at least 25-50 billion reads, then price differential decides.

## Illumina's competitive position (expert opinion)

- Illumina "seems to be kind of branching in a variety of directions like trying to take on single cell and spatial offerings to just be more of a Swiss Army knife rather than a specialist." He called this a "dangerous strategy" if outcompeted on each axis, "a jack of all trades, master of none."
- It has a head start from the installed base and the cost of transition, but "that can't hold them indefinitely." Without "an answer to significantly cheaper sequencing at the same depth, then they will start to lose market share."

## Vendor end-to-end workflows blocked by data security

- The moderator suggested Illumina's ability to support whole workflows is an advantage. Garren: that is true only "in a world without strict digital security."
- In pharma, sending data to vendors or cloud solutions requires approval from the company's digital division, and he has been told no every time. Winning would take "massive lobbying" while every other vendor asks for the same. "It's a more fundamental problem than merely whether or not the vendor can offer the capability."
- Universities have far fewer guardrails and are a better fit for end-to-end solutions, especially core facilities with grants. Workflows only work "if it's physically in the instrument."
- His workaround: keep everything internal, adopt new technology, and process files through the same internal pathways. Vendors keep outputs compatible (SBX is compatible with 10x library prep and barcode-matrix output files, so it "slot[s] in more or less"). Convenience features Illumina offers are "not a point of competition for a customer like me."

## Spatial genomics

### Atera (Xenium successor, per his description)
- **Purchase plans:** Two Atera systems are "definitely preordered" (one for his team, one for another department), and a third for the European side "might" follow. Installation and running are expected around **Q1 2027**.
- **Why:** At least on paper it opens the potential of Xenium. Spatial's big problem is sample volume. A Xenium slide holds roughly two to three samples, and the team has two Xenium instruments. Atera offers larger slides and more aggressive TMA (tissue microarray, many tissue cores on one slide) designs, potentially moving projects from about four samples to more.
- **Full-transcriptome background:** It allows some discovery and might replace **GeoMx** (NanoString's unbiased spatial method), and could replace the paired single-nucleus work done from the same block.
- **Panel design:** He currently designs most Xenium panels with a 1,000-gene Flex custom add-on. He compared this to CosMx layering, where users added a 1,000-gene panel on top of an 18,000-gene panel to make relevant biology "louder."
- **Initial use:** Supplement to Xenium, giving "basically a third larger slide," run with a similar panel to see whether sensitivity differences throw off comparisons. Then test whether the wide transcriptome panel can replace paired single-cell Flex validation.
- **Key uncertainty:** Whether chemistry differences between Xenium and Atera prevent cross-comparison of samples. "If we're just counting genes, it should be ok." If results are not comparable, that "creates an unfortunate situation."
- **Open question:** Whether whole-transcriptome output is just "ones and zeros across everything" and "an echo of a hint" requiring follow-up with a biased panel, or can legitimately replace single-cell Flex validation.
- **Hedging:** He is not discontinuing anything in anticipation. GeoMx and Flex FFPE remain backups; if Atera compares well, they will transition away for routine design.
- **Limits on replacing single-cell:** Spatial needs embedded, sectioned tissue, and not every biology warrants it. In vitro experiments would not be embedded to run on Atera. For mouse in vivo tumor studies the default is a GeoMx run on an embedded section plus more sections for single-cell.
- **Automation:** Unsure which Atera automation segments they will use. They use the Leica BOND RX for upstream automation of GeoMx experiments.

### Current Xenium and GeoMx use
- Two Xenium instruments, used mostly for disease profiling: buying tissue from vendors, generating as many samples as labor allows, and supporting target-credentialing requests (seeing how a target looks in a disease indication). They have also used 10x services to run Xenium with both RNA and protein across programs.
- Typical spatial applications: immuno-oncology, tumor infiltration, autoimmunity (tertiary lymphoid structures), and broad-stroke geography of where targets appear.

### Pyxa (3D spatial)
- Deep 3D spatial is "interesting," but the team struggles to find actionable uses beyond focused questions such as endothelial proximity or highly structured biology.
- Pyxa could add about 100 microns of depth but is constrained on target volume. The team is "anchored to the Atera" and is asking which decisions suffer for want of a larger custom panel rather than for the Z-axis.

## Data demand, AI and sample access

- **Industry view:** The biggest change across pharma is data demand. Everyone has data lakes and centralized AI training, with debate over whether a "virtual cell" model is "utterly quixotic" or near. "Everyone is just kind of holding their breath and watching Genentech... attempt it." He believes the industry needs to be "more granular" about it.
- **Implication for vendors (his inference):** If everyone wants to train AI models on cell data, the data must be generated somewhere, putting "upward pressure on just global high-dimensional data generation" benefiting Illumina, 10x and Roche. Which companies generate it is "a more nuanced question."
- **Sanofi's approach:** Moderate. It gets consortia data that makes sense but will not launch a massive virtual-cell effort. It generates data internally for proprietary reasons and buys the rest externally (for example Tempus, Open Targets consortia).
- **No pull yet:** Over the past year the centralized AI division has mostly collected existing data into data lakes. It has "not yet gotten to the point where they started pulling on us" for new proprietary data generation. It "could be around the corner," but future demand depends on defining specific questions with reasonable dimensionality, and he has not seen it coalesce.
- **Samples:** Sanofi has biobanks and legacy banks just cataloged, with "close to 1,000 FFPE blocks" (preserved tissue blocks) across every indication of interest. It continues buying samples and negotiating with KOLs (key opinion leaders) at cancer centers for precious patient samples, especially non-responders to leading approved drugs. Deals vary: either the center provides data, or gives sample access for Sanofi to profile.

## Role of genomics across drug discovery

Garren says high-dimensional data enters nearly the whole workflow:
1. **Reverse translation:** comparing subcategories (endotypes) and responder versus non-responder signatures to explain tumor escape or drug failure.
2. **Target identification:** disease versus healthy comparisons producing candidate lists.
3. **Combinatorics:** finding synergistic treatment combinations.
4. **Ranking:** using safety, on-target off-tumor effects and efficacy evidence.
5. **Validation:** Perturb-seq in vitro to check expected behavior and surprises.
6. **Early programs:** mechanism of action and comparing candidates A, B and C.
7. **Biomarkers and patient stratification:** predicting success or failure and likely responders.

## Outsourcing, reshoring and geography

- No consistent lean toward internal or outsourced. Outsourcing happens when bandwidth or instrumentation is missing (now long-read). Internalization has focused on validation workflows. Sanofi is "not doing a lot of bread-and-butter outsourcing."
- KOLs sometimes generate data themselves because their governance does not want them treated "like a glorified biobank."
- **China:** "A very aggressive push among every pharma I've seen to do large molecule biologic screening in China," screening massive antibody libraries so large molecules can be treated "more like small molecules." Sanofi has its own footprint there; this is R&D and "not going anywhere."
- **Reshoring and tariffs:** He is not told to prioritize onshoring. His cluster spans two continents, so he prefers vendors with a European presence ("keep Europe in Europe and North America in North America"). One tariff anecdote: a microscope capex request carried a big tariff, and a team member had the software separated and downloaded, which reduced the tariff. This was "accounting agility," the only case where tariffs influenced a decision.

## Concluding views

- He watches two markets each quarter: (1) the **clinical market**, for progress in clinical adoption including reverse and forward translation in the clinic, which he calls "the holy grail of like 10x Genomics"; and (2) Illumina versus AXELIOS and other new technologies, where adoption inertia matters.
- Academia is "more agile, but less funded"; large pharma is "more funded but less agile."
- Closing quote: "It's not NovaSeq X versus AXELIOS. It's the whatever Illumina comes up with after NovaSeq X that would be competing with AXELIOS."
