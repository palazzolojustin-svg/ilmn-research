# Channel check with a pharma NGS core lab director (customer): steady 1-3% output growth, Illumina dominant, Roche Axelios interest cooling, PacBio and Ultima gaining on new assays

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-02_Director_of_NGS_Core_Lab_at_Pharmaceutical_Company_Sees_Incremental_Growth_in_Sequencing_O.md` · Date: 2026-10-02 · Channel check on an expert-network platform (published 2026-10-06; interview 2026-10-02); AI interviewer; expert is a Director of an NGS core lab at a pharmaceutical company (customer perspective) · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer-side channel check with the director of a pharma-internal next-generation sequencing (NGS) core lab running about 20,000 bulk samples and 5 million single cells a year, roughly 100 Gb of output. The lab is about 97% Illumina on short-read, expects 1-3% output growth in Q3 2026, full-year 2026 and 2027, held consumable prices flat, and plans to replace old MiSeqs with a MiSeq i100. The expert's interest in Roche's Axelios has dropped since Q1 because of missing assays, library-prep worries, pod reuse and Roche support, while Illumina's TruPath and SomaLogic/proteomics push are not changing behaviour. PacBio and Ultima are expected to gain share through new assays, and imaging-based spatial (Atera) is flagged as a possible headwind to sequencing volume.

# Channel check: Director of NGS Core Lab at a pharmaceutical company (interview 2 Oct 2026)

## Expert background and how to weigh the evidence

- **Who:** Director of an NGS (next-generation sequencing, high-volume DNA/RNA reading) core lab at a pharmaceutical company. The expert has visibility into operations and purchasing for Illumina, PacBio, Oxford Nanopore (ONT) and Ultima platforms. The platform labels the perspective "Customer". The interviewer is an AI.
- **Recency:** Current, first-hand experience. The interview was on 2 Oct 2026, and the expert answers about Q3 2026, full-year 2026 and 2027.
- **Lab setting:** A research-use-only (RUO, not clinically certified) core lab serving internal R&D scientists for target discovery, biomarker discovery and other work. It handles basic research, translational medicine research and some clinical samples that do not require CLIA certification (the US clinical lab standard). The expert confirmed the interviewer's characterization as "primarily a research and applied laboratory."
- **Funding:** 100% internal company R&D budget. There are no federal grants or philanthropic funds.
- **Scale:** About 20,000 bulk sequencing samples and about 5 million single cells per year. The expert did not know the gigabase figure offhand and estimated about 100 Gb per year when pressed. The core offers more than 50 protocols (assays).
- **Caveats:** This is one lab's view, and the core's volume is small, so it may not represent large genome centers. The expert repeatedly said they cannot always predict what projects arrive. Several answers were prompted by the interviewer's wording, which the expert confirmed. Where the expert speaks from direct experience versus speculation is flagged below.

## Headwinds and tailwinds for sequencing output [Expert: pharma NGS core lab director, Oct 2026]

The expert named three factors, and confirmed the interviewer's recap:

1. **Unpredictable project flow.** Projects arrive from internal R&D without planning, and the core may not be told what samples are coming, so workload fluctuates up and down.
2. **Application switching.** The expert sees more applications moving from single-cell to spatial (measuring gene activity while keeping tissue location). They see "some drop for the single-cell sequencing" and an increase in spatial.
3. **Budget.** About two years ago (around 2024) the lab was under stricter guidance to think carefully and avoid wasting time and money on NGS. This year is "a little bit better," and the lab is running more cost-conscious experiments. The expert said, "it's hard to predict."

## Output trends and outlook (all direct experience, plus forecast)

| Period | Expert's view | Reasoning |
|---|---|---|
| Q3 2026 vs expectations | Met expectations; "pretty steady workflow" versus Q1 and Q2 2026 | n/a |
| Q3 2026 vs Q3 2025 | Up about 1-3% | Natural year-on-year increase in requests, plus inflation-type price changes |
| Full year 2026 vs 2025 | Up about 1-3% | Overall work similar to last year |
| 2027 vs 2026 | About 1-3% up, possibly higher; "definitely will be the same or maybe increase to some level" | New equipment and assets added to the pipeline; higher growth not certain |

- **2026 as an innovation year.** The expert is "pretty positive." The lab did more new things in 2026 than in 2025, which was described as a stable year of routine work compared with 2024. In 2026 the expert learned ONT, PacBio and Ultima and ran pilot experiments with each, with "pretty positive results for many of them." Most of this is tech development that has not yet affected sample counts. The expert expects these pilots to become new workflows and services in 2027, which the interviewer summarized as groundwork for 2027 (confirmed).
- **Senior approval gate.** Some new assays need approval or support from senior management. The lab is waiting on results and meetings with senior leaders, so the expert said a better answer on share shifts could come at the end of Q4 2026.

## Short-read versus long-read mix

- **Current split:** About 95% short-read (reads of roughly 50-300 letters, mainly Illumina) and 5% long-read (ONT, PacBio).
- **Direction:** Long-read samples have increased this year. The expert forecasts a move to about **90% short / 10% long within 12 months**, because several long-read projects are lined up in the queue.
- **Why long-read is growing:**
  - New studies show long RNA-seq gives more information than expected. Requests are growing for isoform (alternative transcript versions of a gene) and splicing detection and for finding novel transcripts.
  - Scientists use long reads to confirm genomic DNA-level changes, for example how gene therapy or other genetic medicines alter DNA.
  - Peer effect: once biologists see other people's results they request similar experiments.

## Competitive share by vendor

### Short-read, as stated and then clarified

- The lab does not have Element Biosciences or Roche sequencers (the expert offered to explain why later; the Roche reasons appear below).
- The expert first said "90% on Illumina and the other three probably get about 3% of each, like ONT, PacBio, and Ultima." After the interviewer clarified, the expert confirmed ONT and PacBio are long-read, so **short-read is roughly Illumina ~97% and Ultima ~3%**, and the long-read side is split separately.
- **Why Ultima:** Short-read has always been Illumina, and the lab never ran Ultima before 2026. The 2026 Ultima use is a pilot for a specific application. The expert's reasons: (1) a need for double-strand sequencing, (2) the expectation that it may be used for large-scale clinical samples in future, requiring a cheaper option, and (3) Ultima needs high throughput to fill its capacity, which the lab expects to have for this application. (The transcript of reason 1 is partly garbled.)

### Throughput mix within Illumina

- Fleet: MiSeq (low throughput), NextSeq (medium), NovaSeq 6000 and NovaSeq X Plus (high). Long-read: ONT and PacBio. Ultima for some specific assays.
- High-throughput assays (bulk RNA-seq, exome sequencing, single-cell RNA-seq) mostly run on a NovaSeq X Plus **25B flow cell** (a consumable chip; "B" is billions of reads). Low-volume or customized requests run on MiSeq.
- The expert likes that Illumina offers low, medium and high throughput, and different flow cell capacities even within NovaSeq X Plus (1.5B, 10B, 25B, and "I think they are also adding 5B and then 10B"). This lets the expert balance cost, throughput and turnaround time.
- The mix of high- versus low-throughput instruments is "stable," with some dynamics driven by incoming assays.

### Long-read: PacBio versus ONT

- The lab validated PacBio and ONT side by side on long RNA this year (direct experience). **PacBio works better** for long RNA, specifically isoform and splicing detection. Only ONT offers direct RNA modification detection.
- Current split is about one-to-one (50/50), depending on application. The expert said that "in future, I think for all long RNA sequencing, we will go using PacBio," and the interviewer's read of PacBio gaining share was confirmed.

### Vendor share changes in Q3 2026 and next 12 months

- No new sequencing-capacity release from any vendor was seen in Q3 2026 and no big share changes. PacBio did release its new **Kinnex** RNA-seq assay (a kit that makes long RNA reads more efficient).
- Next 12 months: the expert expects increases for **Ultima and PacBio**, driven by new assays being added, not by displacing existing work. "I don't think we really lose for the Nanopore and Illumina" because existing work continues. The interviewer's summary that Illumina and ONT hold steady was confirmed.

## Roche Axelios 1 (new sequencer)

**Status and interest (direct experience):**
- The expert believes Roche released the sequencer sometime in 2026, "either the end of the Q2 or maybe Q3," and does not recall exactly.
- Interest was higher at the start of 2026, and **has dropped a little between Q1 and Q3 2026.**
- The lab has not adopted it. It is waiting for the assay it needs and for feedback from other users on whether it works as claimed.

**Reasons interest dropped:**
1. **Missing assay.** The lab's largest application is single-read bulk RNA-seq. The expert does not think Roche has released it. "My understanding is they still have the duplex assay."
2. **Roche customer and tech support.** The lab has used the Kapa RNA-seq library kit for about 10 years (Kapa was later acquired by Roche). "After Kapa was acquired by Roche, the overall tech support and also customer support experience is not very good. It's not very reliable on some levels." With the sequencer, the account manager will talk if asked but "they will never follow up." The expert said, "I'm still worried about their customer support and tech support for their biotech-related product."

**Why it is still attractive:** It is cheap and fast. Based on Roche's official numbers, the expert's quick calculation is that for a very high-throughput RNA-seq pipeline, **the lab could save about 50% of sequencing cost** (expert's own estimate from published figures).

**Reasons the expert may not switch despite the savings:**
1. **Reusable pods.** Roche's sensors/pods are reused. Even though Roche shows no cross-contamination between samples, the expert still worries about carry-over between batches and runs, and wants real-life user experience first.
2. **Library prep.** The expert has not seen the single-read RNA-seq workflow. Other users have voiced concerns about a "tedious" library prep (turning samples into sequenceable form), which would be a problem for a core running very high-throughput samples.

**Competitive threat to Illumina (expert opinion):** "Hard to tell." At first the expert thought Roche was "very competitive to the Illumina." At AGBT (the annual genomics technology conference) in February 2025, Roche's first announcement of the sequencer generated great excitement and packed rooms. At AGBT February 2026 Roche presented its workflow, and the expert's colleagues in similar roles began to voice library-prep concerns (secondhand; the expert did not attend 2026). Now the expert is "more reserved" and waiting. The expert says no one has updated them on single-read RNA-seq and "if I don't ask, probably they won't tell me." The interviewer's summary that initial excitement has tempered was accepted.

## Illumina products and strategy

- **NovaSeq X:** Adopted about two years ago (NovaSeq X Plus). Told that Illumina committed to raising NovaSeq X flow cell output by up to 40% and speeding whole genome sequencing about 30% (interviewer's statement, not the expert's), the expert is "happy" but it won't change much because the major workload is already on Illumina. The expert welcomed the increased throughput, more flow cell options (5B, 10B) and longer reads such as 2x250 on NovaSeq X Plus, and sees it as Illumina continuing to update its products. "I won't really say, 'Oh, I will run more things because of these changes'"; it makes performance better or cheaper. The expert stays open to alternatives and said "Maybe Roche can provide something even nicer, who knows?"
- **TruPath Genome** (interviewer: launched February 2026, removes library prep from the whole-genome workflow, list price about $395 per sample): **Not adopted.** The lab runs little whole genome sequencing; its main assay is RNA-seq. There is internal discussion, and some projects might benefit. The expert has considered TruPath for highly repetitive sequences such as the C9orf72 tandem repeat gene, but expects few samples for those uses. It is unlikely to shift much long-read work or consumable spend to Illumina (confirmed).
- **SomaLogic acquisition** (interviewer: closed January 2026) and multi-omics push: The expert sees Illumina trying to become a multi-omics company (acquired Fluent for single-cell, entered proteomics, started its own spatial) but uses none of it yet. For protein work the lab piloted **Alamar** this year, not Illumina. The lab lacks proteomics throughput, and Illumina's protein solution requires buying a bundled liquid handler costing "six figures," which isn't worth it at the lab's volume. "The protein profiling solution from Illumina does not really help me. For one to three years, I would say I do not have the interest to pursue." There was no change in spending with Illumina.

## Instruments and capital spending

- **Plans (not final):** Possibly buy a new **Illumina MiSeq i100** to replace two MiSeqs that are more than 10 years old.
- **No other purchases planned.** For long RNA-seq the lab uses a neighboring lab's sequencer and will keep sharing equipment, since it runs few samples.
- **NextSeq retirement:** The lab will probably retire most of its (old, rarely used) NextSeq instruments, since the NovaSeq X Plus 1.5B flow cell covers similar capacity and the MiSeq i100 covers very low throughput (confirmed). No placement plans were accelerated, delayed or cancelled in Q3 2026.
- **2026 instrument capex:** No sequencers bought in 2026, the same as 2025, so flat.
- **Instrument discounts and terms in Q3 2026:** Unchanged versus the prior quarter.

## Consumables and pricing

- **Spend in Q3 2026 vs Q3 2025:** Very similar, roughly flat to up 1-3%, a mix of volume and price. No stocking or destocking: "pretty straightforward."
- **Price:** Because of the company's overall volume, vendors kept prices the same as 2025.
- **Discounts, incentives or reagent commitment terms in Q3 2026:** Unchanged.
- **Inventory:** Sufficient. **Lead times:** Unchanged versus the prior quarter.

## Vendor support (direct experience)

- **Illumina:** Account managers, customer support and bioinformatics support are "pretty good"; the expert is "pretty positive or satisfied."
- **ONT and PacBio:** Positive. PacBio's account manager "always replies to your email in one second."
- **Ultima:** Support attitude is great and it follows up well, but the expert has "a little bit of concern" on quality of problem-solving, saying they "may overpromise a little bit" without delivering equally.
- **Q3 2026 change:** Support levels unchanged versus the prior quarter.

## Emerging technology and risks

- **Atera spatial transcriptomics (imaging-based):** The lab is evaluating it. If the lab runs more imaging-based spatial, it could reduce NGS sequencing volume, a possible headwind for sequencing demand. The expert does not know yet and expects clarity next year. The interviewer's cannibalization framing was confirmed.
- **Related shift:** Single-cell sequencing is declining inside the lab while spatial rises (see above).

## Key takeaways

- Illumina remains the lab's core platform (about 97% of short-read). Growth is low single digits (1-3%) in Q3 2026, 2026 and 2027, with no instrument purchases in 2026 and flat pricing.
- Share gains are expected at the edges: PacBio (long RNA) and Ultima (cheap, high-throughput pilots), mostly additive rather than displacing Illumina.
- Roche Axelios faces practical adoption barriers for this customer (assay availability, library prep, pod reuse, support) despite a self-estimated ~50% cost saving.
- Illumina's newer initiatives (TruPath, SomaLogic/proteomics) have not changed this lab's behaviour.
