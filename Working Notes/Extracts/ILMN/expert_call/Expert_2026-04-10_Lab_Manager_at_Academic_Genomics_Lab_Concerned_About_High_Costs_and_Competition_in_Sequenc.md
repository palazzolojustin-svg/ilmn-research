# Academic genomics lab manager (Massachusetts) on Illumina cost pressure, flat 2026 volumes, competition from commercial providers, and Roche AXELIOS threat

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-04-10_Lab_Manager_at_Academic_Genomics_Lab_Concerned_About_High_Costs_and_Competition_in_Sequenc.md` · Date: 2026-04-10 · Channel check expert call on an expert-network platform (published 14 Apr 2026; interview 10 Apr 2026), AI interviewer; expert is a lab manager at an academic genomics lab in Massachusetts · expert call*

**Summary.** A manager of a university DNA sequencing core facility that runs only older Illumina instruments (NextSeq 500/550 and iSeq 100) says sample submissions and sequencing output have fallen because their cost per sample is higher than commercial providers running NovaSeq X fleets. Output was down about 15% from 2024 to 2025 and about 5% in Q1 2026, and the expert expects no growth in 2026 and has no budget for new instruments. The expert reports higher Illumina list prices offset by discounts, slightly longer delivery times, weaker phone support, and sees Roche's AXELIOS as a mid-to-major threat while viewing the SomaLogic acquisition positively.

# Expert call: academic genomics lab manager on Illumina (interview 10 Apr 2026)

They have held the role for 10 years and handle day-to-day operations. The platform's bio says they have visibility into how Illumina next-generation sequencing (NGS, technology that reads DNA or RNA in bulk) is used for validation research, and autonomy over the lab's strategic budgeting. ## Lab sequencing volume and demand

### What is hurting volume (Q1 2026)

- The expert says cost per sample is "extremely high with the older technology" and the lab does not have XLEAP chemistry (Illumina's newer chemistry that is used on its newer instruments). Turnaround time is good.
- The limiting factor is multiplexing (pooling many samples into one run). The number of samples the lab can multiplex at the coverage depth clients need is limited, which makes its cost per sample higher than commercially available alternatives offered to its own clients.
- The result is a decrease in sample submissions. The expert has cut sample-preparation costs, but says "the sequencing costs are extraordinarily high."
- Work has shifted toward the iSeq 100 (Illumina's smallest benchtop sequencer). The lab is handling proportionally more iSeq projects, mainly library validation before large NovaSeq runs (the lab sends the big runs elsewhere or validates libraries first), plus some amplicon sequencing (reading one targeted DNA region) and 16S sequencing (a bacterial identification method).
- Funding backdrop (the expert's belief): research and start-up funding is limited, so there is "a bit of a holding pattern on expensive sequencing runs." Many companies, clients and academic researchers have turned to "low-risk, low-reward" work.

### Output trend (gigabases, the amount of sequence data produced)

- Q1 2026 total Illumina output is "staying the same or perhaps decreasing." After prompting for a number, the expert said it is **down 5%** versus Q1 2025.
- Between 2024 and 2025 output was **down about 15%**. The expert says the drop "happened between 2024 and 2025, and it pretty much held that low output since first quarter of 2025," and the decline has "pretty much plateaued."
- The iSeq 100 produces far fewer gigabases, so more iSeq runs do not make up for fewer NextSeq runs.
- Output in Q1 2026 is **falling below** the expert's expectations. The reasons given are sequencing cost per sample, limited multiplexing, competition, and the "extremely low expense" of commercially provided sequencing available to the lab's users.

### Full-year 2026 outlook

- Expectation is **no increase**, roughly flat versus 2025: about 5 to 10 NextSeq 500 runs for the year and no more than 20 iSeq runs for the year.
- The driver of the flat outlook is "too much stiff competition" from commercial sequencing providers running fleets of NovaSeq X instruments (Illumina's newest high-throughput system) for the lab's user base.
- The forecast is **less optimistic than 90 days earlier**, because of "more instability in the global economy and the national economy." The expert noted "no marked changes to federal policy on grants and grant award distribution."

## Consumables and reagents

- **Spending.** Q1 2026 spending on Illumina consumables (the reagent kits needed for each run) is flat to slightly down versus Q1 2025. Some kits bought in 2025 are still being used in 2026, so there has been no need to buy more given low project volume. The expert could not give a year-over-year percentage.
- **Cause.** Asked whether the trend reflects volume, price, stocking or destocking, the expert said "Strictly decrease in volume of projects."
- **Pricing.** Illumina prices have gone up. The expert believes this occurred within roughly the last 90 days (from the end of the last fiscal year to now) but is not certain. List prices rose. However, incentives and discounting keep the lab's actual expense "about the same."
- **Inventory practice.** The lab orders on an as-needed basis, historically adding about 10% or one extra kit per order so users do not wait for shipping.
- **Lead times.** Shipping now typically takes two to three days, versus one to two days in 2020 through 2023. Lead times increased in the past 90 days, adding about one extra day between ordering and receiving goods. The expert's guess at the cause (speculation) is staffing and shipping logistics. The expert now has to be more vigilant about inventory and order timing when projects are expected.

## Competitive and pricing environment for instruments

- The expert says Illumina is "offering steeper discounts for their products to be competitive with lower list prices from competitors" (answer to the question on price concessions over the past 90 days).
- **Platforms gaining favor.** "Completely" in favor of:
  - MiSeq i100, for read length and lower-to-mid output.
  - NextSeq 2000, which offers a cost-per-sample reduction over the lab's NextSeq 500 and 550.
  - NovaSeq 6000 and NovaSeq X, with "huge favoritism" because of multiplexing ability, which raises value and lowers cost per sample.
- Drivers of preference: "definitely cost," and to some extent read length for work such as microbiome 16S sequencing.
- **Purchase plans.** The lab has no budget room for new equipment. The expert is tasked with finding grants or private funding, an "ongoing and aggressive effort," and is trying to be strategic about winning funding for NovaSeq equipment. Purchase plans have not changed in the past 90 days.

## Products and strategic initiatives

- **Illumina products of interest.** The expert is most interested in the PIPseq single-cell platform for benchtop use and in the NovaSeq X for driving down cost per sample to be competitive with commercial providers.
- **TruPath Genome.** The expert does not know this product, so no view was given.
- **Competitor products.** QIAGEN's GeneGlobe analysis portal is praised for reducing the post-sequencing data-analysis bottleneck. QIAGEN also has a good ribodepletion product (removes ribosomal RNA). The expert says there is a market-wide need for products that minimize host contamination in metagenomic and metatranscriptomic samples.
- **Roche AXELIOS (new sequencing platform).** The expert calls it "a mid to major threat." Their reasoning (opinion, not experience, since they do not run it): cost per sample and consumables cost could be decreased, data quality and output could be increased, and the platform can handle a large array of applications at once.
- **SomaLogic acquisition (Illumina's proteomics purchase).** The expert says it "increases value." Their institute has a proteomics facility, and a companion proteomics capability would help validate and scale samples, with higher throughput than standard LC-mass spec (mass spectrometry) approaches. The expert's reasoning is that omics focus is shifting from DNA to RNA, and from mRNA to high-throughput proteomics, because each is a more direct explanation of biological outcomes. They conclude that proteomics is "critical in maybe the future of understanding cellular changes over transcriptomics and genomics."

## Customer support

- Over the last three to four years the expert has seen a change in Illumina phone technical support. There is a mix of veteran staff and newer people who learned the products quickly; the expert has "terminated phone calls and called back to receive a more veteran answer."
- The expert says this is typical of tech companies, which they believe move R&D staff into support, let them go over time and replace them with quickly trained staff, so service quality is not equal to that of those who developed the products. This is the expert's general impression, not confirmed knowledge of Illumina's staffing.

## Views on the future of sequencing

- Metatranscriptomics (sequencing RNA from microbial communities) and metagenomics (sequencing DNA from microbial communities) will grow as bacterial impact on host organisms is better understood in disease, drug metabolism and other areas.
- There will be a shift away from studying DNA, which becomes "more of a niche group," toward RNA expression levels and protein abundances, isomers and glycosylation.
- **Market concentration concern.** There is "a bit of a monopoly forming," with sequencing flowing to one or two commercial providers offering higher throughput and better cost per sample. This neglects smaller researchers in niche areas (including organism-level work) and keeps them from taking part in studies of potential long-term value. The expert sees high value in small academic institutes doing routine genomic and transcriptomic analyses outside clinical sequencing and big diseases such as autoimmune disease and cancer.

## Closing observation and suggestion for Illumina

- The expert observes that Illumina launches a new device every two to three years that raises throughput or read length to take on more applications. This is strategic for business, but well-endowed or grant-rich institutes can buy the newest instruments while smaller, hit-or-miss institutes are "left in the dust" and may "inevitably fall off the map."
- Suggestion: Illumina should investigate a program placing secondhand used equipment into less well-endowed universities and contract research organizations (CROs), possibly with free service contracts or free service. Examples given: a small institute getting a secondhand NovaSeq 6000 from a company that bought a NovaSeq X, or swapping a NextSeq 500 for a secondhand NextSeq 2000 after a larger institute upgrades to a NovaSeq 6000.
- The expert's rationale: this would keep competition alive, broaden use of genomic and transcriptomic studies and increase the variety of data produced.
