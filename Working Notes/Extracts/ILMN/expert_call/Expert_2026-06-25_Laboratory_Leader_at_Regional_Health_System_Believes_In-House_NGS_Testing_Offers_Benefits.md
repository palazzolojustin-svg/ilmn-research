# Regional physician-office lab leader on bringing NGS tumor profiling in-house (Illumina NextSeq 2000, co-op partnership, economics, volumes, staffing)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-06-25_Laboratory_Leader_at_Regional_Health_System_Believes_In-House_NGS_Testing_Offers_Benefits.md` · Date: 2026-06-25 · Expert call transcript (published 09 Jul 2026; interview 25 Jun 2026); expert is a laboratory leader at a regional physician office laboratory; interviewer is a buy-side (investor-led) analyst; platform not named · expert call*

**Summary.** A laboratory leader at a very large physician office lab explains why and how it brought tumor DNA/RNA next-generation sequencing (NGS) in-house about a year and a half ago, using an Illumina NextSeq 2000 and a bioinformatics partnership with a co-op. Economics are roughly break-even because the lab handles only about 6-10 samples a day versus an expected 50+, after high-ordering oncologists left. The expert says in-house testing beats send-outs on turnaround and control, rates ~90% first-pass collection, would not repeat the decision at current volumes, and sees break-even above about 20 samples a day.

# Expert call: in-house NGS testing at a regional physician office lab

## Expert background and context

- **Who the expert is.** A laboratory leader at what they describe as a "very large physician office laboratory" (not a hospital system). [Expert: laboratory leader, regional physician office lab, interview 25 Jun 2026]
- **Relationship to Illumina.** A customer. They run an Illumina NextSeq 2000 sequencer under a reagent rental agreement (see below). Experience is current: NGS was brought in-house about a year and a half before the call.
- **Practice footprint.** Multi-specialty (oncology, dermatology, pediatrics), six high-acuity urgent care centers, about 176 low-acuity urgent care centers in a tri-state area, and 23 phlebotomy (blood draw) sites.
- **Role.** Oversees all laboratory operations: routine chemistry, hematology, urinalysis, pathology, cytology and NGS, plus phlebotomy staffing, supervisors, managers and logistics.
- **Bio notes.** The expert can consult on in-house versus reference-lab decisions, quality systems, lab information systems (LIS/LIMS), automation, new lab builds, staffing and regulatory compliance.
- **Interviewer.** A buy-side investor-led analyst. The expert speaks from direct experience on nearly everything. The exceptions are the co-op's payer contracts, the co-op's regulatory approvals and Roche's sequencer, where the expert says they do not know or only offer opinion.
- **Terms used.** NGS (next-generation sequencing, reading many DNA/RNA fragments in parallel). Reagent rental (the instrument is supplied in return for a commitment to buy the vendor's consumables). Reference labs (centralized outside labs such as Foundation, Caris, Tempus and Guardant, which receive send-out samples). LDT (laboratory-developed test, run under the lab's own validation rather than FDA clearance).

## What NGS testing the lab does

- The lab runs both DNA and RNA tumor profiling on solid tumors, hematologic (blood) malignancies and liquid biopsies (blood tests for tumor DNA).
- Panels were designed with the chief of oncology: which genes, which indications, and what the report looks like. Gene counts range from about 300 to 1,600 depending on the profile.
- A smaller solid tumor profile looks at 434 genes, plus structural chromosome changes and the biomarkers TMB, MSI and HRD. A "solid tumor profile plus" covers fusions, BRAF, PD-L1 and methylation.
- A larger solid tumor profile with DNA and RNA goes up to 1,600 genes.
- A "liquid trace" test uses cell-free DNA from an EDTA peripheral blood sample, so no tissue block is needed.
- Hematology work covers lymphoma, multiple myeloma, acute lymphoblastic leukemia, MDS and CMML. A "hematology profile plus" covers JAK2, CALR and MPL and can run from peripheral blood, bone marrow or fresh tissue.
- Sample type for solid tumors is FFPE (formalin-fixed, paraffin-embedded tissue). Pathology blocks are kept in-house, so the lab can extract DNA and RNA directly.
- Hereditary cancer testing is only at a very early evaluation stage. A further constraint is that the lab's one molecular pathologist may not stay long term, and a replacement would need molecular genetics certification.

## How the lab was set up

- **Before.** The lab was not in NGS. Physicians chose Foundation, Tempus or whatever was available, using vendor-supplied kit tests with all work sent out.
- **Decision factors.** Whether an in-house test would give clinically the same or better information, and whether physicians would use it.
- **Partner.** The lab partnered with a co-op, the Genomic Testing Cooperative (a California NGS lab working with multiple hospitals on the East Coast and across the U.S.). The expert likens it to Guardant or Tempus, in that it can take whole samples, but it also supports labs that do the technical part themselves.
- **Division of work.** The lab does extraction and library prep and runs its own sequencer, then sends only the data to the co-op's bioinformatics pipeline. The co-op returns interpretation. The co-op also trains the lab's pathologists to interpret cases, and the lab bills for the technical component.
- **Why not go fully alone.** "It's a big IT lift" (servers, bandwidth, infrastructure) and, in the expert's words, "you want to make sure that the squeeze is worth the juice." The lab is "not ready yet just to bring everything in-house," and may go fully independent once physicians are comfortable interpreting.
- **Other co-op help.** Customized panels and equipment lists, validation and blind samples (validation can take three to four months without them), mock inspections for readiness, and troubleshooting support.
- **No competitive evaluation.** The expert did not run an RFP and looked only at this co-op, citing its seminars and the fact that some of their oncologists already sent samples there. Peer hospitals gave good feedback. The expert had not heard of SOPHiA GENETICS.
- **Outcome.** The lab removed third-party send-out kits (Tempus, Guardant and similar) from its test compendium, and physicians "gravitated to our in-house test."
- **Fallback for out-of-network payers.** The lab information system holds rules that decide per order whether to keep a sample in-house. If the payer is not in-network, the sample goes to the co-op, which runs an identical panel so the physician sees the same genes (not a 300- or 500-gene substitute for a 400-gene custom panel) and results still interface in.
- **Share in-house.** NGS is never 100% in-house because the tests need prior authorization and some payers are out-of-network.

## Why the lab brought NGS in-house

1. **Profit.** CMS (Medicare) reimbursement looked "very lucrative at above a grand" per test.
2. **Turnaround and workflow.** Send-outs lacked an interface, requiring manual paperwork, scanning results into the chart and chasing samples, which was a "staff burden, administrative burden."
3. **Control and patient care.** In-house results file into the EMR (electronic medical record) automatically. It enables faster targeted therapy and clinical-trial enrollment, and physicians can ask to expedite a patient. The expert also said, "We wanted to be on the cutting edge."

## Turnaround time

| Route | Turnaround reported by expert |
|---|---|
| In-house | 5-7 days or 7-10 days depending on the order |
| Send-out to reference labs | "north of three weeks or four weeks" |

- In-house time includes batching, a library prep that takes about a day (four to five hours of hands-on manual work), and sequencing that runs up to 44 hours (the expert also mentions 24 hours), plus occasional repeats.
- The interviewer noted vendors advertise about one week. The expert said "I never got a one-week turnaround time from any of these vendors," and that it depends on vendor and backlog.
- The expert says that with higher volume they could shave about a day (7 instead of 10 days) by running daily, but results are never same-day. This is not a stat test.
- Currently runs happen every other day, with DNA and RNA on separate days (both on one day would make days too long).

## Sample handling issues with send-outs

- Reference labs often want the whole tissue block rather than H&E slides (stained thin tissue slices), and can exhaust it by cutting for more tumor. The lab later chose to send unstained H&E slides instead.
- This is not universal. Some vendors accept slides, but may request the whole block if a lab's slides don't show enough tumor.
- Blocks are kept on-site for three years, then archived off-site (adding a day or two to retrieve). Blocks are sometimes released to other institutions for second opinions, or reused for PD-L1 testing after NGS.

## Economics

### Cost per test

- The lab's blended cost was "around a grand" (about $1,000) per test. This covers library-prep reagents, DNA and RNA consumables, pipettes, tips and overhead for running the test, but excludes staffing, which is a separate line. RNA costs more than DNA.
- The expert estimated that 50% or more of that cost is reagents (checking the order sheet would be needed for a precise split).
- The co-op charges a separate flat rate for professional interpretation (reads). It is "way less" than $1,000, and is negotiated by volume and by DNA/RNA split. Sometimes the co-op wants RNA sent back to it. If the lab buys reagents from the co-op it is more economical.
- Reagents are bought through the co-op, which buys in bulk and passes down savings ("like Costco"), and this is cheaper than a direct Illumina contract would be at the lab's volume. The co-op reagent purchases are library prep reagents and are a separate agreement from the Illumina sequencing consumables.

### Revenue and reimbursement

- Average CMS reimbursement across the relevant CPT codes (billing codes) was above $1,000 for the technical component. The lab considers the interpretation component separately and said it could bill globally but pays the co-op a flat rate.
- The lab compared payers on a blended basis. Some, like Aetna, pay a bit less than CMS and others, like Cigna and Horizon, pay more, and the lab's payer mix showed an upside.
- To establish coverage, the lab filed a "new service request." Its compliance team reviewed CPT codes and the policies of the top five commercial payers. There is no way to submit a "dummy claim," so it was "trial and error." The lab built an in-house prior authorization process with the clinic staff.
- For CMS, the expert said you need a CLIA certificate (the federal lab license), correct CPT and diagnosis codes. There was no clearance by MolDX or the MAC (Medicare Administrative Contractor), and the lab just submits claims using known CPT codes. The expert did not know whether the co-op needed any MAC approval.
- Diagnosis codes were the biggest issue. The lab educated oncologists, nurses and front-desk staff, and built check-box test requisition forms listing profiles, genes and common diagnosis codes. There was no EMR compliance checker.
- **Collection rate.** The lab collects on about 90% of claims on first pass. The interviewer noted that reference labs say they collect 50-60%. The expert attributed the difference to (a) sending out samples whose payers are known not to cover the test, and (b) incentives. Reference labs bill third-party and a middleman practice has no incentive to ensure correct diagnosis codes. An in-house lab, which has to pay staff, does.
- Reference lab claims run "like a quarter behind." When reference labs have denials, they send weekly spreadsheets, and the expert's QA team works with office managers to fix codes. The expert keeps reference lab reps out of physician offices to avoid "leakage" (physicians hearing about other assays) and to protect cancer patients from collection letters. Reference labs end up writing off what they can't collect.

### Profitability

- The expert says the operation is breaking even, including staffing costs. "We're not losing money." The expert did not agree to "pull the plug right this second" but is monitoring it.
- The interviewer's summary, which the expert confirmed as correct: reimbursement of just above $1,000 per test averaged across the 10% unpaid, against costs of around $1,000 for reagents and direct consumables, plus labor and some start-up amortization, making the operation just above break-even.
- Staff is sometimes moved to other departments on low-volume days. The lab is simultaneously evaluating whether it needs three technicians or only two. It does not want to lose the staff because of how hard these skills are to find. If volume rose suddenly (for example from hiring many oncologists), "I'm going to be in a pinch."

## Volume

- **Current.** About 6-10 samples a day.
- **Original expectation.** North of 50 a day, based on two large oncology centers with six physicians each, plus two operating rooms and outside contracted ORs, and a one-stop cancer center with on-site transfusion and chemotherapy.
- **Benchmark.** A "very lucrative, busy NGS lab" does north of 60 a day.
- **Why volume fell short.** The higher-ordering physicians left, including the chief physician who initiated the project (who moved to another organization). New oncologists have been onboarded, but ramp-up has been slow because they may come from institutions using Tempus or Guardant and face a learning curve on the lab's panels.
- **Share of NGS in-house.** About 10-20% of the practice's NGS testing. The interviewer calculated, and the expert agreed was fair, that the system might generate about 70 samples a day, of which the lab captures 6-10, and that north of 50 would equal roughly 60-70% in-house.
- **Overall lab in-house share.** About 85% of all testing, up from about 50% before the pandemic and before the lab was rebuilt in 2020. An NGS lab was carved out within the new lab.
- **Physician choice.** Physicians cannot be forced to use in-house tests, since they can order out at any time. The lab makes ordering out harder by design: "three, four additional clicks" in the EMR or a different compendium. The expert said the most effective lever is service line chiefs (the head of oncology) who persuade peers. Losing that chief was "a little bit of a hit."
- **Outlook (three to five years).** The lab is recruiting oncologists (hard to find), keeping panels current so physicians don't go outside, and reviewing new genes with the co-op. It has spare capacity: one NGS supervisor, three technicians and one lab assistant, and the sequencer is not at capacity.
- **Effect of volume on cost.** Higher volume would use fewer consumables per sample and allow daily runs. At low volume, small runs waste plastics and a sequencer cartridge ("one shot done"). The expert could not recall how many samples fit on one run and would need to check. The lab receives roughly 6,000-7,000 samples a day overall and uses a special bag to identify NGS samples at intake.

## Break-even and the expert's hindsight

- Break-even is "above 20" samples a day. About 30 a day "would make sense" for three technicians and a supervisor.
- At 6-10 samples a day, the expert said they would not have made the same decision: "If I was going to be at a break even, and if I had known that we would have lost oncologists, then I wouldn't have gone down it." They would rather be "at the cutting edge, not at the bleeding edge," and might have used the space for a larger pathology lab with more cutters.
- The expert described the opportunity as "financially lucrative" at high volume but warned that "you can't just blindly go into it and then have buyer's remorse."
- The expert advised due diligence on volume, payer behaviour, prior authorizations and denials, and personnel, and reviewed the retirement plans and send-out volumes of each oncologist (e.g., whether they send 10-15 tests to Tempus a week), plus the five-year P&L.

## Capital investment and infrastructure

- The largest cost was the sequencer: the Illumina NextSeq 2000, which the expert said was "maybe six figures." Other items were workbenches, dead-air boxes, pipettes and consumables, and a Promega product for DNA extraction.
- The lab built three rooms in about 1,600 sq. ft. of previously unused shell space (originally held back for either NGS or on-site phlebotomy): accessioning (sample receipt), extraction (the "dirty room"), then sequencer and library prep. The workflow is unidirectional (samples never move backward), which helps avoid contamination.
- Construction was done with an architect before NGS work began.
- The lab runs only one sequencer, with no backup, because of cost, and accepts the risk of no redundancy.
- Everything is run as an LDT (not FDA-cleared), so the lab needed validation and inspection readiness.

## Staffing

- Staff must have NGS or related experience (extraction, HLA typing). The expert said you cannot just pull someone from the core lab. Errors in a 24-44 hour run may not be discovered until results come back from bioinformatics.
- Such people are "rare to come across." The supervisor came from HLA typing (similar to NGS, like blood banking), and others came from research. Library prep is entirely manual and the reagents are costly, so attention to detail matters.
- Hourly pay for these staff is higher than for core lab hires. The expert and technicians were trained at the co-op's California site. You need to understand the science behind the assay to troubleshoot.
- Current NGS staffing: one supervisor, three technicians, one lab assistant. A molecular pathologist on staff may leave.

## Prevalence of in-house NGS elsewhere

- The expert has seen the trend "pop up more and more over the years" but says it is not common. It depends on having enough samples, typically from several hospitals via an IDN or hospital chain, centralized at one location.
- The expert knows of at least three health systems doing it.
- The expert observed that reference labs often manage hospital labs and take cheaper tests (CBC, CMP, microbiology) but hospitals keep NGS because of reimbursement.

## Illumina-specific points

### Instrument choice

- The lab uses an Illumina NextSeq 2000 because the co-op uses Illumina and recommended it, supplying a list of equipment. The lab was "replicating a cookie cut" in a new space. Co-op troubleshooting support is available only if the lab uses the same equipment.
- The expert said the co-op "is standardized" on Illumina equipment (confirmed in answer to the interviewer).
- The sequencer is brand new and "not even heavily used," and the lab "is not going to switch."

### Reagent rental agreement

- The lab has a five-year reagent rental contract with Illumina based on aggregate spend over the contract term.
- Volume has been slow in the last couple of years because of the physician departures. Illumina has not told them they are non-compliant. The expert hopes to make up the gap in year five.
- If the volume commitment is not met, the expert understands Illumina would extend the contract without a volume commitment and give more time to close the gap. It would not cut off at five years.
- The lab's contracts with Illumina are separate from the co-op's. The lab gets no pricing leverage from the co-op's use of Illumina and no master agreement discount. Co-op library prep reagents do not count toward the Illumina program.

### Roche sequencer

- Asked about Roche's new sequencer and the need to reuse a flow cell about 20 times to reach the net pricing, the expert said "I think it's doable." Roche gave an initial presentation, but the lab leans toward Illumina because of the co-op route. The expert said they won't switch now.

## Competitors named

- Foundation, Caris, Tempus and Guardant are the centralized labs the interviewer cited. The expert mentioned Foundation, Tempus and Guardant as prior send-out vendors. No market share, price or contract data for them was given beyond turnaround and sample-handling observations above.
