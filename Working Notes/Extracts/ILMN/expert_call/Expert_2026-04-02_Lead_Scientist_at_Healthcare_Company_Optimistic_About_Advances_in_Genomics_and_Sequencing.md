# Channel check with a senior principal scientist (head of genomics) at a global biopharma, a NovaSeq X Plus customer evaluating Element AVITI24, on Illumina demand, pricing, competition and Roche Axelios (Apr 2026)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-04-02_Lead_Scientist_at_Healthcare_Company_Optimistic_About_Advances_in_Genomics_and_Sequencing.md` · Date: 2026-04-02 · Channel check grid / AI-interviewer expert call platform; interview 2 Apr 2026, published 6 Apr 2026; expert is a customer (Senior Principal Scientist, head of genomics at a global biopharmaceutical company); no named analyst (AI Interviewer) · expert call*

**Summary.** A customer in preclinical pharma research says its Illumina sequencing output is rising in Q1 2026 because 10x Genomics' new higher-volume Flex kits allow millions of single cells to be profiled, and expects full-year 2026 volume to be higher. Spending on Illumina reagents may be flat or lower because the move from NovaSeq 6000 to NovaSeq X Plus cut kit costs. The expert is price-insensitive, is evaluating Element Bio's AVITI24 for new capabilities (not cost), and sees Roche's Axelios competing mainly in cost-sensitive CRO (contract research organization) settings rather than in internal pharma labs.

## Expert background and how to weigh the evidence

- **Who the expert is.** The expert is a customer of Illumina, not a former employee or competitor. They are a senior principal scientist who acts as head of genomics at a global biopharmaceutical company [Expert: senior principal scientist, biopharma customer, Apr 2026]. The platform's bio line says the expert has visibility into sequencing operations and volumes, with direct oversight of purchasing decisions.
- **Team and mandate.** The team sits in a group called Target Disease and Systems Biology. It generates data for bulk RNA-seq (measuring average gene activity across a sample), single-cell RNA-seq (gene activity cell by cell), spatial transcriptomics (gene activity mapped within tissue), and complex in vitro modeling (lab-grown disease models). The work supports preclinical research: disease modeling, mechanism of action, reverse translation and assay profiling.
- **Customer base.** The team works only internally, serving the research teams in each therapeutic area. Any preclinical work needing internal data generation comes to them. They are not a clinical lab or an external service provider.
- **Recency.** The interview was on 2 April 2026 and covers Q1 2026 plus the preceding 90 days. The expert describes current direct experience.
- **Direct experience vs. speculation.** Most of the content is first-hand: their own volumes, kit costs, purchasing plans and support experience. Views on Roche, CROs and market-wide share shifts are explicitly second-hand or speculative. The expert says they are not in touch with the CRO space, have not followed Roche's platform, and that those questions belong with a CRO expert.

## Installed base and platforms

- The expert uses Illumina exclusively for sequencing.
- The main workhorse is the NovaSeq X Plus (Illumina's newest high-throughput sequencer), used predominantly for high-volume single-cell projects. The expert later calls it a "million dollar" instrument.
- The lab also has two NovaSeq 6000s (the previous-generation model). These handle other applications, including single-cell or bulk RNA-seq, depending on sequencing volume demand.
- There are no non-Illumina sequencers today.
- The lab is evaluating Element Bio's AVITI24 with a functional genomics team, testing its optical pooled screening (genetic screens read out by imaging) and 3' RNA-seq capacity.
- The NovaSeq X Plus was bought at the end of the prior year and installed in 2025. The move from NovaSeq 6000 to X Plus took place in 2025.

## Sequencing volume, output and outlook

### Drivers in Q1 2026
- **Internal strategy.** Reorganizations changed which projects the team works on. The expert describes the effect on volume as "an interruption followed by an increase." The team is moving toward more aggressive, larger-scale projects, including more disease profiling and more combinatorics projects.
- **Technology upgrades, mainly 10x Genomics.** 10x Genomics' v2 kits let the team push the total number of single cells profiled "well into the millions." Sequencing demand "has gone up quite considerably just from the raw sequencing angle." Beyond this and internal strategy, the expert cites no other drivers; routine usage evolves with the technology upgrades.
- **Output vs. a year ago.** Output in gigabases from Illumina instruments in Q1 2026 is increasing versus Q1 2025, "due to the release of new 10x kits that have enabled vastly higher volumes of single cells."
- **Vs. expectations.** Output is "slightly exceeding" expectations. The expert attributes this to 10x's higher-volume Flex kits plus the team's own work adapting Flex chemistry for functional genomics, which allows sequencing it could not use last year.

### Full-year 2026 view
- The expert says full-year 2026 output is "very likely higher" than 2025, and notes this is without counting Q1, which is already higher.
- The forecast is more optimistic than 90 days earlier, because of promising results from newer protocols being tested.
- The reason: "Before, we weren't able to do high volume functional genomics using single cell with the Flex chemistry, and now, we can."

## Consumables spending and pricing

- **Spending may be flat to lower despite higher volume.** The expert says the 2025 shift from NovaSeq 6000 to NovaSeq X Plus means reagent kits (consumables, the kits needed for every run) are less expensive. Overall Illumina sequencing spend in Q1 2026 versus Q1 2025 "might be equal if not lower," even with more sequencing. The expert believes that over the long run spending "will definitely increase" because of accelerating demand.
- **What drives volume.** Volume changes come from new capacities unlocked by new protocols or technologies from adjacent providers such as 10x Genomics or NanoString, or other sequencing-based readouts.
- **Pricing incentive.** The most notable pricing feature was Illumina's strong incentive to move from NovaSeq 6000 kits to X Plus kits. The expert recalls (hedged with "I want to say") about 80% more reads at about a third of the cost. This was one of the main arguments for adopting the X Plus, and it is what lowered per-kit cost despite higher overall production.
- **Price sensitivity.** The expert is "not sensitive to price differences amongst competitors" in equipment, and says cost per sequencing "has historically not been any metric that we've ever been sensitive to." Reagent spend "is rarely our limiting resource." The expert also says that when a new capability is much more sequencing-hungry, reagent budgets are rarely the point of contention.

## Equipment purchasing plans

- **Element AVITI24 CapEx reservation.** The expert has put in a capital expenditure reservation for an AVITI24. The lab is running a technology access collaboration with Element: it will send samples to run an optical pooled screen supporting a live project, and the result will justify the purchase.
- **Planned use.** If Element delivers high-quality data, the lab will resend samples and then internalize the platform. Use would be at minimum optical pooled screens, possibly also 3'-prime sequencing, Cell Painting (imaging-based cell profiling) or spatial proteomics on adherent or suspension cells.
- **Condition.** The purchase goes ahead unless poor data can clearly be traced to the platform technology rather than upstream issues.
- **Changes in the last 90 days.** Only subtle tweaks: the team identified a modestly priced cell counter from another provider. Nothing as large as a new NovaSeq X Plus.
- **Why the cell counter.** Single-cell counting volume has been accelerating, and counting is a known bottleneck upstream of sequencing. Accelerating single-cell sequencing therefore requires faster cell counting.
- **No new Illumina instrument purchase is mentioned.** The expert's only named platform purchase plan is the AVITI24.

## Competition and share

- **Platforms the expert watches.** Singular Genomics' G4 and Element's AVITI24, chosen for abilities beyond brute sequencing, not raw throughput or cost.
- **Roche and others.** The lab has not focused on upgrading to the Roche sequencer or other competitors because of the infrastructure lift needed to transition.
- **What drives platform preference.** The expert says it is the unlocking of new capabilities by other technologies. Example: when 10x Genomics releases a plate-based fixation method that can unlock over one million cells per lane, that alone "is going to vastly accelerate raw sequencing demand." Everything else is routine bulk RNA-seq, which has not changed much.
- **Share expectations (next 12 months).** The expert is "vaguely optimistic" that Element should gain some share, potentially Singular too, but for reasons unrelated to brute sequencing volume. They have seen no clear evidence that Roche is edging out competitors, while stressing they are not in touch with the CRO space.
- **Differences between buyers.** The expert says CROs are much more sensitive to per-read cost than internal pharma sequencing facilities like theirs.
- **Adoption pattern (personal experience).** Platforms offering more than sequencing, such as in situ de novo sequencing (sequencing directly inside tissue on the flow cell) on Element and Singular, seem to show a stronger adoption case.

### Roche Axelios (formerly SBX)
- The interviewer said Roche plans to launch Axelios next year.
- The expert's view (speculation): competition would likely come from operations more sensitive to cost per sample, "which is not my own operation." It could make a difference for centralized CRO-type operations handling large numbers of samples.
- The expert says they have not closely followed brute sequencing performance gains because they do not realize the marginal benefit. Features such as higher accuracy, flexibility, throughput, flexible read lengths, workflow options and cost efficiency are value adds that "a CRO would absolutely actualize."
- Quote: "I think the real space where that major competition will happen is going to be in the CRO solution provider space rather than in the end user space."

## Illumina products, strategy and M&A

- **Fluent (PIPseq).** The expert notes Illumina bought Fluent and wrapped it into some of its single-cell technology, and they keep an eye on it. They are "still primarily 10x," but it has potential applications such as single-nucleus RNA-seq. Illumina has not yet shown that its solutions are worth dropping 10x Genomics, for example by adopting Fluent-based PIPseq in place of current encapsulation technology.
- **Downstream analysis.** The lab has evaluated Illumina's downstream analytical processes but was blocked internally by other digital strategic initiatives. The expert says that decision was above their pay grade.
- **Following Illumina news.** The expert has not followed Illumina's other announcements closely, being centrally focused on Illumina's core role in the workflow. They understand Illumina is trying to build a one-stop shop.
- **Wish list.** Mostly quality-of-life improvements, sequencing volume and general cost reductions.
- **SomaLogic acquisition.** The interviewer said the deal is expected to close in H1 2026. The expert calls it "a good synergy" because SomaLogic is already an NGS (next-generation sequencing) readout, so high-volume readouts are a natural application. The lab would pick SomaLogic's technology on its own and use an Illumina readout. Keeping it in the Illumina ecosystem "definitely works better for Illumina's bottom line," but from the customer's viewpoint it matters little whether Illumina or SomaLogic is the owner.

## Customer support and service

- Illumina's customer support is "very satisfactory."
- Service contracts are "a bit pricey." The lab has been consolidating them to focus on the X Plus to realize savings, and has sunsetted a number of smaller instruments for that reason.
- FASes (field application scientists) are "very high quality." They show up routinely for preventive maintenance checks and are quick to help with issues. The expert says they have been "quite satisfied."

## Expert's view of the future of sequencing

- **Direction of interest.** In situ de novo sequencing and sequencers that integrate multiomics (several kinds of molecular measurement) in one flow cell. The expert says that if such a platform could compete with 10x Xenium (a spatial instrument), giving robust, potentially whole-transcriptome in situ sequencing with biomaterial placed straight on the flow cell, it "would be a massive game changer." They have the intuition the technology is pointed that way but "not quite there yet." Current state of the art works well for adherent or suspension cells.
- **Value of cheaper sequencing.** "Counting each gene more cheaply doesn't make that big of a difference in our value proposition," because the lab is not at its throughput limit day to day. It runs thoughtful strategic experiments followed by high-volume runs, and those experiments are not make-or-break on kit cost.
- **CROs.** CROs run constant high-volume output for large customer bases, so they are "extremely sensitive" to cost differences. The expert says that is where baseline sequencers would battle.
- **Game changer.** A competitor able to outperform CosMx or Xenium (spatial platforms), do single cell on chip, or "let us do all the sequencing inside the biology" would be a game changer for them.
- **Clinical translation.** Efforts are pushing for more high-dimensional data generation in the clinic, but how best to translate it is still under discussion. The expert jokes that every vendor envisions "a sequencer sitting in a Walgreens" providing detailed endotyping data. Today that would require massive infrastructure and non-automated downstream analysis, although downstream steps are becoming increasingly automated.
- **Advice to analysts.** Pay more attention to multiomics developments (SomaLogic, epigenomic readouts, single cell, spatial, dry-lab analysis) and not only brute sequencing. In the expert's space "everything's based on multiomics."
- **Closing point.** When a new technology announces it uses sequencing as a readout, "that should be probably the first place to look for making predictions about what brute sequencing demand will look like." Otherwise, the expert says, you are "basically just modeling CRO behaviors."
