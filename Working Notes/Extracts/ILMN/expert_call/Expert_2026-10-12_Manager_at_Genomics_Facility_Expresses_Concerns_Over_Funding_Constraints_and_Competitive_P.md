# Academic genomics core manager on funding squeeze, CRO competition, Element-first fleet and Roche AXELIOS interest (channel check, Oct 2026)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-10-12_Manager_at_Genomics_Facility_Expresses_Concerns_Over_Funding_Constraints_and_Competitive_P.md` · Date: 2026-10-12 · AlphaSense channel check (AI interviewer); expert is a customer, a manager at an academic genomics core facility (published 2026-10-15, interviewed 2026-10-12) · expert call*

**Summary.** A manager of a research-use-only academic sequencing core (about 45,000 Gb per year) says Q3 2026 output was down about 10% year over year because federal funding is shrinking and CROs and large sequencing centers can offer much lower prices. The lab runs 100% of its in-house data on Element Biosciences instruments and outsources about 15% of data to Illumina NovaSeq X for large projects. The expert plans no instrument purchases, is interested in Roche AXELIOS and Element VITARI if money appears, and expects output to be flat to down about 10% in 2026 and possibly down again in 2027.

## Expert background and reliability

- The expert is a manager at an academic genomics facility ("core lab") at a state institution. This is a customer perspective, not a former Illumina employee or competitor. [Expert: academic core manager, Oct 2026]
- The core runs research-use-only (RUO) sequencing, meaning for research and not for clinical diagnosis. It is not a CLIA-certified clinical lab (CLIA is the US clinical lab certification).
- It serves four groups: researchers inside the institution, outside academic researchers, industry partners, and international clients.
- The lab is a "cost-recovery facility": it must cover its costs from user fees, so higher sequencing volume directly helps its sustainability.
- Annual output is about 45,000 Gb (gigabases, billions of DNA letters read).
- The interview took place 2026-10-12 and covers Q3 2026 and the outlook. Almost all of it is direct first-hand experience at this one lab. The expert explicitly says that vendor discounts to other labs and vendor share trends across the country are outside their knowledge, and those comments are presumption.
- The interviewer was an AI. Two statements were revised mid-call (funding mix, below). Some product facts (NovaSeq X roadmap, TruPath, SomaLogic) were supplied by the interviewer, not the expert.

## Funding mix and funding trends

- The expert first estimated 40% federal, 10% biopharma and 0% philanthropic, which left 50% institutional. The expert then revised this: 60% federal, 30% institutional, 10% biopharma, 0% philanthropic.
- Grant funding available to the researchers the lab serves decreased in Q3 2026 versus the prior quarter. The main cause, in the expert's words, is "new limitations on federal funding, grant availability, and researchers's general ability to interface with federal funding mechanisms."
- Biopharma-funded work in Q3 2026 was "about as expected."
- Because 60% of the lab's work is federally funded, the federal squeeze is the largest single exposure.

## Output trends and outlook

- Factors named as driving output: per-run output of current machines, user requests, project size and budgets, and the commoditization of sequencing through outsourcing to CROs (contract research organizations, outside firms that sell sequencing as a service).
- Q3 2026 output was slightly below expectations, about 80%-90% of the lab's Q3 goal. Cause: fewer projects coming through because of limited funding, and struggles competing with outside providers.
- Q3 2026 output was down about 10% versus Q3 2025. Cause: researchers struggle to get funds, there is less money for projects, and outside providers offer "a very, very low-cost option."
- Full-year 2026 versus 2025: the expert said it could be as low as a 10% decrease; optimistically flat; "ultra-optimistic" up 10%-20%. The numbers "are bearing out to be either about the same or about 10% lower." The interviewer summarized this as flat to down about 10%.
- Q3 made the expert "slightly pessimistic": "It's a tough environment, tough field, tough climate for research in general. We're just trying to weather the storm here."
- 2027 versus 2026: possibly down about another 10%. The reasoning is that the lab cannot afford a capital expenditure to move to a higher-output sequencer, so it is "locked in with the tools we have." Unless the tools' output or the cost of consumables changes, its cost per base (price per DNA letter read) will not improve against high-output sequencers. The expert expects "the same headwinds and stronger competition."
- The expert hopes output will rise, since the lab is cost-recovery, but concedes decreases are possible from funding changes and competition from higher-output sequencers.

## Fleet and platform mix

- In-house instruments: an Element AVITI24 (Element Biosciences' benchtop sequencer) and an Illumina NextSeq 550. The NextSeq 550 was not used for sequencing in Q3 2026 or Q2 2026 and is used only for array detection (reading microarrays).
- No long-read instruments in this lab. A long-read instrument exists on campus, run by a different ("sister") lab. The expert would refer a suitable project to it.
- 100% short-read. This reflects the lab's operational area and its service menu. The expert expects that to stay roughly unchanged, and believes the application (not the facility manager's preference) decides short versus long read, since the two types of projects are mostly different.
- Vendor split: all internal sequencing (100% of in-house data) is on Element. About 15% of project data, counted by data volume, is sent out to Illumina NovaSeq X, so the overall mix is about 85% Element in-house and 15% outsourced to NovaSeq X (interviewer's summary, confirmed by the expert).
- Why outsource: only when a project has enough samples and needs enough depth of coverage that an external provider is more cost-effective. "It's all about providing value to our end users."
- The vendor split did not shift in Q3 2026. The expert presumes it has changed elsewhere in the country.
- Next 12 months: the only likely change is more use of Illumina (outsourced), driven by cost per base on deep-sequencing, high-output, many-sample projects. The expert thinks Ultima (Ultima Genomics, a low-cost sequencing entrant) is gaining traction at other labs, but this lab does not use or send work to Ultima, and local researchers do not demand it.
- Throughput mix: many current projects suit a mid- to low-throughput instrument. The lab hopes eventually to adopt a somewhat higher-output instrument, driven by user demand, and keeping projects of a certain size is key to justifying one. A mid- to high-output instrument (lower cost per base, more competitive) is the realistic target, not the highest-output machine. Filling a NovaSeq X-class machine "doesn't seem achievable with our current utilization."

## Roche AXELIOS

- The lab has not adopted it. The expert finds it "a very compelling and interesting platform." Points the expert likes: the reusable consumable (reusable sensor), an instrument cost "not completely out of line in the field," cost per base that "looks really good," and good runtime.
- Capital equipment is "quite hard to fund these days," but AXELIOS would be on the list when expanding the fleet. It is not on their campus, so there is no conflict with other campus cores competing for instrument decisions.
- The only critique: the launch period was a bit long, with too much advance publicity before availability.
- Competitive threat to Illumina (expert opinion, not first-hand data): "somewhat of a competitive threat," at least initially in mid-throughput. The expert does not believe it matches the massive output of the largest NovaSeq X flow cells. It could threaten Illumina where mid-to-high fast output is needed and has a niche in lower-throughput labs and health diagnostic labs where throughput is not the main buying driver.

## Illumina products and portfolio

- NovaSeq X (Illumina's highest-output sequencer): not adopted and not being pursued. Reasons: capital cost, "very high service contract cost," and the lab could not fill its capacity.
- Roadmap (stated by the interviewer: flow cell output up by up to 40%, whole genome speed up about 30%): does not change willingness to buy. It is relevant only as an outsourcing destination: if cost per base keeps falling, the lab may rely on it more.
- TruPath Genome (interviewer: launched Feb 2026, removes library prep from the whole genome workflow, list price about $395 per sample): the expert calls it "a very compelling price" but it gives no reason to buy a NovaSeq X. The lab gets few whole human genome requests, would refer people to providers offering it, and it makes participating in human genome sequencing itself "very unlikely."
- SomaLogic (interviewer: acquisition closed Jan 2026): no effect. The lab did no SomaLogic work, and it does not change the inclination to adopt Illumina technologies. Portfolio awareness helps in pointing researchers to solutions.

## Capital spending and instrument plans

- Plans to acquire, replace or upgrade a sequencer in the next 12 months: "likely no," because of high capital cost and the "very tough financial situation." If a new platform were adopted it would be mid-to-high throughput; the expert "would highly consider" the Element VITARI and/or Roche AXELIOS to fill the open niche in the campus fleet.
- No placement plans were accelerated, delayed or canceled in Q3 2026 because there were none.
- 2026 capex versus 2025: flat, effectively zero in both years, since no sequencer was bought last year and no funds are expected this year.
- Vendor instrument incentives: unchanged in Q3 2026. The expert was not offered notable incentives and does not know what others receive.

## Consumables, pricing and supply

- Q3 2026 sequencing consumables and reagent spending versus Q3 2025: about the same, perhaps a small decrease driven by volume rather than price. Element reagents have a "fixed and locked price." Spending on outsourced NovaSeq X work was also about level.
- Sequencing chemistry prices: unchanged 2026 versus 2025. Ancillary consumables (library prep, plasticware, gloves, plate seals, tips, tubes) are all rising in price, which raises overall service costs. Sequencing reagents are "staying very level."
- Reagent discounts and commitment terms: unchanged in Q3 2026. The lab lacks purchasing power to drive incentives; larger cores or those in purchasing consortia may get more aggressive discounts (the expert's understanding, not first-hand).
- Inventory: "sufficient." The lab avoids overbuying but keeps enough for queued projects.
- Lead times: unchanged, with no backorders or delays in sequencing reagents.

## Vendor support and service

- Sales and technical support: satisfactory. Vendors have become "a little more strict" about replacing reagents after a failed or erroneous run.
- Illumina's LIMS (laboratory information management system, software for tracking samples and runs) is used by the lab. It "has gotten to be quite expensive," and its development cycle "seems...to have decreased over time."
- Support levels were unchanged in Q3 2026. The expert's contacts at Illumina and Element are the same people as a year ago, and support from both is very good. Industry-wide, the expert sees workforce contraction and turnover in next-generation sequencing, giving less continuity and fewer hands, and reduced customer-service efficiency generally.

## Emerging technologies and closing concern

- The expert is watching platforms in spatial (sequencing within tissue context) and multiomics (measuring several molecule types together), and expects more competition in single-cell sequencing.
- Biggest issue facing the lab: competing with outside CROs and large sequencing centers that get discounted reagents. "The same reagents that large organizations are buying are the same reagents that I'm buying, but there's a very different cost scale." The expert calls this a large concern nationwide: how smaller sequencing cores stay relevant and efficient when reagent pricing differs so much by buyer size.
