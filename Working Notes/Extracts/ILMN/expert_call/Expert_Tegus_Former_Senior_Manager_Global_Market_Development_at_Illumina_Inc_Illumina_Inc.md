# Expert call (Tegus) with former Illumina Senior Manager, Global Market Development: clinical NGS bottlenecks, Roche AXELIOS, MRD, NovaSeq X, DRAGEN, SomaLogic

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_Tegus_Former_Senior_Manager_Global_Market_Development_at_Illumina_Inc_Illumina_Inc.md` · Date: 2026-01-15 · Tegus expert network interview; expert is a former Senior Manager, Global Market Development at Illumina (now global precision medicine lead at Astellas); interviewer is an unnamed Tegus client. The document gives no date, so the date is a best estimate. · expert call*

**Summary.** A former Illumina market-development manager (about seven years at Illumina, now at a pharma company) argues that the main barriers to clinical sequencing are reimbursement, regulation and doctors' ordering habits, not sequencing speed or cost. They say Illumina's recent clinical growth comes mainly from a handful of large US labs and MRD testing, that most "clinical" volume is lab-developed tests rather than FDA-approved assays, and that Roche's same-day genome and sub-$100 genomes from Element and Ultima matter little for oncology. They are positive on Illumina's "more applications per instrument" strategy, NovaSeq X, DRAGEN and SomaLogic, but skeptical of MRD and see Illumina's unresolved identity as a technology company versus a clinical company as its biggest challenge.

## Expert background and how to weigh this call

The expert is a former Senior Manager, Global Market Development at Illumina. Today they are the global precision medicine lead for Astellas (a pharmaceutical company), running the international region. They are therefore a former employee, no longer a customer or competitor, though they now sit on the pharma side. The expert noted twice that this may bias their views.

Career summary, as the expert gave it:
- About seven years at Illumina in several roles: sales specialist, oncology specialist, market development, and global market development.
- Before Illumina: regional marketing manager at 10x Genomics, and before that technical sales at Thermo Fisher Scientific.
- Education: PhD from Nanyang Technological University in Singapore, with post-doctoral training at the Genome Institute of Singapore and at Harvard Medical School.

The call date is not stated. The expert's Illumina experience is not dated either, but they spoke in the past tense about "my time there." The latest facts the interviewer cited (Roche AXELIOS marketing, Illumina's SomaLogic acquisition, Thermo owning Olink) suggest a call from late 2025 or 2026. The expert has left Illumina, so their view of Illumina's current internal plans is secondhand or dated.

Numbers about Illumina in this call (20% growth, 65% clinical share) came from the interviewer, not the expert. The expert did not verify them. ## The main bottleneck in the NGS market: clinical adoption

The expert framed the clinical market (using sequencing to diagnose and treat patients) as the "holy grail" for all sequencing companies, because that is where the big revenue is, and said everyone wants to be a clinical NGS company. [Expert: former Illumina senior manager]

The expert's view is that the main bottleneck is adoption of NGS as a clinical diagnostic test. They named three places where it gets stuck:
- **Reimbursement**: whether insurers and health systems pay for the test.
- **Regulation**: approval pathways for tests.
- **Prescription behavior**: how oncologists order tests, and how readily pathologists adopt them.

## Illumina's clinical consumables growth and its sustainability

The interviewer said Illumina's clinical consumables (the reagent kits customers must buy for every sequencing run) grew about 20% excluding China for two straight quarters. The expert agreed this matched what they knew, and said it is largely driven by big accounts: large US clinical and commercial labs such as Natera, Tempus, Caris and Foundation Medicine. [Expert: former Illumina senior manager]

The expert's reasoning and view:
- They guessed the growth reflects the recent rise in MRD testing (minimal residual disease, i.e. blood tests that look for tiny traces of cancer left after treatment) over the last six to twelve months. This is their inference, not a stated fact.
- On sustainability, they expect the big accounts to keep sustaining and increasing volumes because of the MRD trend.
- From direct experience: Illumina relied heavily on these big accounts. The hard, unanswered question internally was how to get more "organic or democratized" clinical sequencing, meaning broad use by many smaller labs. They called this "always the tough question to answer."

## How much of "clinical" is really FDA-approved?

The interviewer cited Illumina's statement that clinical is over 65% of its consumables revenue and asked how much is IVD-approved (FDA-cleared or approved diagnostic kits) versus labs running their own tests on research-use instruments.

The expert said the figure has "always been a very loose statement" and that "Illumina has been very generous and very broad in terms of classifying clinical use." [Expert]
- The only FDA-approved comprehensive genomic profiling (CGP, a test that checks hundreds of cancer genes at once) assay they cite is TruSight Oncology (TSO) Comprehensive, which is the FDA-approved version of TSO 500.
- Besides that, there are some legacy assets that the expert is unsure anyone still runs.
- Their answer: most clinical volume runs as laboratory-developed tests (LDTs, tests a lab designs and validates itself), mostly at the large companies named above (Natera, Foundation, Tempus and others).

## Reimbursement of TSO Comprehensive: in-house testing versus send-out

The interviewer noted that TSO Comprehensive is now reimbursed and asked whether hospitals will bring CGP in-house and pull volume from Foundation Medicine's send-out model (where hospitals ship samples to Foundation's central lab). The expert focused on the US.

- Reimbursement "helps expand access," but the expert would not say TSO 500 will take share. They said this needs to be seen from research.
- The share of patients covered by CGP is not high at the moment.
- Expanding reimbursement to several CGP tests would at first not eat into incumbents' share. They see "market expansion as opposed to a cannibalization kind of scenario," though cannibalization may come in future. [Expert opinion]

## Community oncology: can smaller practices adopt NGS at scale?

The expert called this "the billion-dollar question" and thinks it will be difficult. [Expert opinion]
- The market is currently trying to get community physicians used to CGP reports, which is what happened in academic centers five to ten years ago.
- Expertise in community hospitals is still missing. A hub-and-spoke, centralized or regionalized approach (samples sent to a regional center) would work best at present.
- Running a CGP-capable test is getting easier because of workflow, bioinformatics and reporting advances. What still needs to develop is the expertise to use the data.
- Turnaround time is mostly driven by batching. A lab without enough patients to fill a weekly run may wait for the next week to run for cost effectiveness. This is another barrier for community hospitals.

## Roche

### Same-day whole genome sequencing on AXELIOS
The interviewer said Roche is marketing same-day whole genome sequencing on AXELIOS (Roche's sequencing system). The expert thinks speed matters little for oncology. [Expert opinion, with technical estimates from experience]
- Sequencing alone, including library prep (preparing DNA for the sequencer), takes about a day to a day and a half at most, so roughly 24–36 hours on Illumina.
- Cutting that in half or more, which is what AXELIOS promises, saves about 12 hours. Across the whole workflow (biopsy acquisition, sample processing, library prep, sequencing, bioinformatics, reporting) the expert thinks that saving is not worth much.
- Fast whole genome sequencing is more relevant to rare and pediatric rare disease, for example babies in neonatal intensive care units. The expert has seen little or no data on AXELIOS in oncology and does not know whether Roche is pushing it there.

### Roche's diagnostics sales force
Roche already has a diagnostics sales force calling on pathology labs and hospitals. The expert said this is "a big advantage" but remains skeptical of the Roche solution, and acknowledged this may be bias from knowing Illumina technology for so long.
- Converting labs that already run NGS (with Thermo or Illumina) to Roche is one opportunity.
- The bigger prize is getting labs that have never done NGS to adopt it. The expert thinks that is still a challenge, and the only way is a "push-button system" that pathologists and molecular pathologists have confidence in.

### An IVD-cleared AXELIOS with a companion diagnostic tied to Roche cancer drugs
A companion diagnostic (CDx) is a test required to decide whether a patient should receive a particular drug. The expert, speaking from the pharma side, raised two cautions. [Expert]
- Pharma ethics and compliance carry a risk whenever a test, or sponsorship of a test, is tied to drugs.
- There is precedent. After Roche bought Foundation Medicine, some markets saw cases where a patient who obtained and paid for a FoundationOne assay could end up with the oncologist putting them on a Roche drug. The expert said other pharma companies have since become wary of using the FoundationOne test for their programs, CDx and LDTs. They suspect the same would happen if Roche drugs are linked too closely to Roche's sequencing technology or CDx.

### Roche partnering with 10x Genomics on single-cell for AXELIOS
The expert said Illumina's strategy is to add more functionality to current instruments rather than build new sequencers, and that Roche is following the same playbook. [Expert]
- They said the field appreciates this. Illumina previously had a reputation for new sequencing technology every few years, forcing customers to buy a new instrument, which "leaves a bad taste in people's mouths."
- Sequencing capacity per instrument has reached a high level, so investment has moved to which applications can run on the same machine.
- Illumina has its own single-cell and spatial technology, which the expert says it is doing "very well," and is adding analytes (things measured). The expert mentioned "five-Base" and methylation as examples. They consider this strategy sensible.

## Sub-$100 genomes from Element and Ultima

The interviewer said Element and Ultima now offer genomes for under $100. The expert called it "a very nice, sexy number" but said it has limited impact in oncology. [Expert opinion with technical reasoning]
- A germline genome (the patient's inherited DNA) is read to roughly 30X depth, meaning each position is read about 30 times. Tumor sequencing needs far more, "easily more than 120," and "not even 200 times," so the real cost rises well above $100. The expert said this is rarely mentioned in marketing materials.
- Sequencing is only one cost. Others include library prep, biopsy, getting samples into an FFPE block (preserved tissue) and cutting slides, and bioinformatics. As with speed, sequencing cost is "at a point where it will not make that much of an impact."
- Quote: "even if sequencing is free," getting regulatory and reimbursement mechanisms in place takes time. For somatic oncology (tumor-acquired mutations) the clinical impact will be "very limited."

## Thermo Fisher Ion Torrent versus Illumina in clinical oncology

The expert, who worked at both Thermo and Illumina, summarized as follows. [Expert, direct experience]

**Where Thermo wins:**
- A simple, seamless workflow from DNA or RNA to a report, which appeals to labs and pathologists who do not want to deal with technical detail.
- Smaller gene panels.
- Somewhat faster turnaround than Illumina.

**Where Illumina wins:**
- Strength of the sequencing technology itself.
- Larger sequencing capacity, so it can run more samples of a small panel and also bigger panels, up to whole genome.

The expert said Thermo is strong in small panels at small start-up labs. They saw cases where labs that wanted to scale moved to Illumina to run more patient samples, even on small panels, and usually then wanted to scale to CGP (not necessarily whole genome yet).

## Illumina versus 10x Genomics in single-cell and spatial biology

The expert (a former 10x employee) thinks 10x is ahead for now, as incumbent market and technology leader in both single-cell and spatial. [Expert opinion]
- Illumina's strategy of putting more applications on a single box has lowered the barrier to trying Illumina's technology compared with trying 10x's.
- The expert recalled a couple of published bake-offs: Illumina data ahead of 10x data on spatial, and 10x ahead on single-cell. They said it is nuanced, because there are two benchmarks: number of cells assessed (breadth) and transcripts per cell (depth). If they remember correctly, Illumina was ahead on transcripts per cell and 10x ahead on cells per sample.
- Overall "it's up in the air," with 10x holding an incumbent advantage. The expert thinks Illumina "can close it really, really quickly" because its current customers can try the technology at very low initial cost.

## TSO Comprehensive versus FoundationOne CDx

The expert said the comparison depends on the audience. [Expert]
- Labs cannot run Foundation's test, which is centralized, so the like-for-like in-house comparison would be Roche's AVENIO kit.
- A savvy pathologist or lab would look at gene count and biomarker coverage. TSO's 500 makes more sense, since FoundationOne has "300 over genes" (the expert's recollection, hedged with "if I'm not wrong").
- Oncologists are influenced by the FoundationOne brand, its many companion diagnostic claims, and the large amount of public data and publications and clinical evidence from a centralized test. The expert said these shape oncologist behavior strongly through confidence in the brand.

## MRD (minimal residual disease) testing

The expert said they have "always been a bit skeptical" about MRD being popular globally, and flagged this as personal bias. [Expert opinion]

Reasons for skepticism:
- It works now because of strong US interest and some US reimbursement. MRD can be expensive and reimbursement outside the US will be very challenging.
- Many MRD tests are developed by large central US clinical labs. The expert has yet to see that translate to Europe and the rest of the world other than Japan, noting that even Foundation has found it hard to gain a foothold outside the US.
- Developing MRD in-house as an LDT is "quite difficult" compared with a CGP assay or small panel, both technically and practically.
- The clinical driver would be drugs or assets with MRD on the label for treatment escalation or de-escalation. The expert said some completed trials tested de-escalation (reducing or removing treatment when MRD is undetectable), and they "did not pan out": there was not much survival difference between patients whose treatment was reduced and those whose treatment continued.

On Illumina's MRD kit: the expert understands Illumina is bringing out a kit to make MRD accessible as an LDT in different markets. The approach is broad-based and agnostic (tumor-informed targeting is not required), which needs a lot of sequencing and therefore high cost. If adopted it is good for Illumina's revenue, but the expert finds adoption hard to see given unclear patient and drug benefits and the cost of the assay.

## Instrument strategy

### NovaSeq X
Why labs upgrade (expert's understanding, noting they might be wrong):
- Illumina wants to put new applications, such as spatial and single cell, on NovaSeq X first, with benefits trickling later to NextSeq and older platforms.
- On the research side, heavy sequencers get a lower per-base cost on the larger instruments.
- Customers now see the X as "the pinnacle of the technology" for some time, so they need not worry that "the next new iPhone is coming out next year." The expert tied this to Illumina's shift away from releasing a new box every few years.

The expert did not give a growth forecast for NovaSeq X.

### Smaller instruments (NextSeq, MiSeq)
The expert said the high-throughput segment is the "sexy" one, where Ultima (and Element, to a lesser degree) is going after Illumina. They see a real role for smaller systems and think Illumina may need to put in more effort to grow the segment. [Expert opinion]
- Uses: microbiology, which needs little throughput, and fast sequencing where quick turnaround matters. The new MiSeq is "really, really fast" and the NextSeq "is pretty much there as well."
- These instruments compete with Thermo's small-panel systems.
- The expert does not think Illumina has "really worked that hard to grow that segment" in its history.

## DRAGEN (Illumina's bioinformatics software)

The expert called DRAGEN "a very important aspect of the strategy." They described three software layers: [Expert, technical explanation]
- **Primary analysis**: converting the instrument's color or laser signal into base calls. The expert said labs stopped worrying about this more than 10 years ago.
- **Secondary analysis**: aligning the reads and curating the data into a usable form. This is what DRAGEN does.
- **Tertiary analysis**: turning data into a clinical report. SOPHiA is an example of a company working here.

Since Illumina data is the de facto standard and the bulk of the world's sequencing data, DRAGEN has "taken out the guesswork" and become the standardized state of the art for secondary analysis, so labs no longer need to choose pipelines for alignment or SNP calling (single-letter DNA variants). The expert thinks this has moved the field technically but that Illumina has not marketed it hard, partly because it is "not a very sexy thing to talk about" and hard to market as an advantage. They see better marketing as something Illumina could improve.

## SomaLogic acquisition and proteomics

The expert called putting more applications and technologies on one box "absolutely the right strategy." [Expert opinion]
- In life sciences there is always a bake-off (Olink versus SomaLogic, 10x versus NanoString). Thermo's purchase of Olink "sets up a new battleground."
- In oncology research, having more solutions under one roof helps. A lab that already owns a NovaSeq X can add a SomaLogic workflow rather than take on an Olink workflow, which lowers the barrier.
- In oncology clinical, NGS-based protein detection is some time away: cost is high and, as they understand it, setup is difficult (a robot is needed for library prep), which is a big barrier.

## Biggest challenge and opportunity: technology company or clinical company?

The expert said that from their experience, Illumina "has not decided whether it wants to be a technology company or it wants to be a clinical company." [Expert, experience-based opinion]
- A technology company advances sequencing and adds applications. A clinical company focuses on getting assays and tests to patients. Illumina has tried to do both, and doing both well is the challenge for current management.
- If clinical is the goal, Illumina currently has very few IVD or CDx assays. Scaling that means building R&D, commercial and business development teams, engaging pharma, and proving the ability to take an assay from research or LDT through CDx to regulatory approval.
