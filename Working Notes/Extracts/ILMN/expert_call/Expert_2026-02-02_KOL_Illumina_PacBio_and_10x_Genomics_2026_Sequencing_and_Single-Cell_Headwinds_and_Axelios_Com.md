# Expert call with Alvaro Hernandez (University of Illinois DNA core director) on 2026 sequencing and single-cell headwinds, Axelios competition, PacBio strength and new entrants

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-02-02_KOL_Illumina_PacBio_and_10x_Genomics_2026_Sequencing_and_Single-Cell_Headwinds_and_Axelios_Com.md` · Date: 2026-02-02 · Guidepoint expert network; moderator Andrew Carmen; expert Alvaro Hernandez, Director of DNA Services, University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (academic KOL, physician scientist viewpoint) · expert call*

**Summary.** A long-time academic core-facility director says billing fell about 15% in the last four months of 2025 (mostly Illumina work) because of grant cancellations, a funding shift toward human research, and aggressive low-price entrants such as Parse, Plasmidsaurus, Ultima and reagent vendors turned service providers. He sees Illumina's new 5-base kit as a bright spot, Roche's Axelios as not yet a threat, PacBio as a surprise winner (10% to 20% of his revenue) and Oxford Nanopore as unreliable. He has paused buying a second NovaSeq X and expects sequencing demand to stay strong but revenues to be squeezed in the short term.

## Expert background and basis of views

The expert is Alvaro Hernandez, Director of DNA Services at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign (UIUC). The call was run by Guidepoint (an expert-network firm) and moderated by Andrew Carmen on 2 February 2026. It is a repeat call ("once again"; "it's been a while since we talked"). Guidepoint classed the viewpoint as "Academic KOL - Physician Scientist". The primary companies are Illumina (ILMN), PacBio (PACB) and 10x Genomics (TXG); Oxford Nanopore, Element Biosciences and Roche are associated.

- He has worked in the core facility since 2001 and says he has run his lab for 25 years. [Expert: Hernandez, Feb 2026]
- A core facility is a shared university lab that sells sequencing and related services to researchers. His serves UIUC, other universities, private companies, federal agencies such as the USDA, and customers abroad, so he sees a wide range of applications.
- Instruments in his lab: Illumina NovaSeq X Plus, Illumina MiSeq i100, PacBio Revio, and Oxford Nanopore GridION. A neighbouring core in the same center runs a Xenium (10x spatial instrument), and its output is sequenced in his lab.
- Services offered: DNA or RNA extraction, library preparation (preparing samples for the sequencer), sequencing and post-sequencing analysis.
- The majority of his sequencing is still on Illumina, specifically the NovaSeq X Plus.
- His customer base is described as "very agriculturally oriented" (plants, animals), though human projects have recently risen sharply.
- He has a strong partnership with Illumina and does alpha and beta testing (early product testing) for them.
- His experience is current (direct, 4Q25 billing and early 2026 plans). Where he is speculating, he says so, and this is flagged below.

## Demand and volumes: 4Q25 and outlook for 2026

**Direct experience: volume decline.** 2025 was "a really stressful year" for core facilities, and he says other core directors report the same. In the last quarter (he later specifies "the last four months") his billing was about 15% lower than in other years. Causes he gives: grant cancellations, and researchers being careful with money because they do not know if more funding will come. The decline was "mostly in Illumina" work. PacBio was not yet hit because a very large PacBio project has been running for several months.

**Profitability.** His lab's numbers have been "in the red in the past three months," which he says never happened in 25 years. He does not expect to downsize staff because a university usually covers big gaps, though money is "not as plentiful as it was just a few months ago." (Earlier staff-cut concerns from the prior call have not materialised.)

**Outlook for 2026.** He is "not very positive," for these reasons:
- Many grants remain cancelled, although some large grants have been funded.
- The administration shifted priorities toward grants that use humans rather than animal models, so more human research is being funded.
- Price competition from new private entrants is intense (see below).
- He could not quantify the 2026 budget change versus 2025.

**Capacity.** Asked whether there is unused sequencing capacity, he said that before the current administration capacity was still below demand. With less funding, he would not call it overcapacity but supply and demand are "very close." He noted that Ultima in particular is starting to create more competition.

**Capital spending.** A few months earlier he was "ready to pull the trigger" on a new instrument but has stopped, given lower demand and "much lower profits." He buys instruments with the lab's own funds and depreciates them over two years. His NovaSeq is two years old, so he has been building a fund for the next one, but he does not expect enough demand to fully use a second instrument and will keep what he has.

**Pricing and lane sharing.** New private companies are trying to grab sequencing share with "unrealistic prices," and core facilities have been forced to cut prices. Previously his lab sold only entire Illumina lanes (a lane is a channel of the flow cell, the consumable slide on which sequencing runs). Now it shares lanes: a researcher with only 15-20 samples is offered half a lane. Researchers hear of very low prices through mass mailings to campuses and demand the same, so the lab is "reducing our margins by sharing lanes."

## Funding environment (NIH and universities)

- **NIH budget mechanics (expert's account).** The NIH has "$40 billion or so." This year it gave multiyear (five-year) grants all the money up front and funded "like 3,000 less grants." He says this is "definitely not good for us" and that funding more grants would be good. The interviewer called it "an accounting trick"; the expert agreed. Unless more grants are funded this year, he sees no shift, but he expects one.
- **Expectation (speculation).** He thinks faculty will adapt to the new priorities, for example switching mouse models to human cell cultures, removing DEI language and gain-of-function work from proposals, and winning more funding.
- **ARPA grants.** UIUC has a $23 million ARPA grant, mostly benefiting PacBio and partly Illumina. One or two more such grants would put the lab "in excellent shape."
- **Campus losses.** UIUC lost $67 million in grants across many departments, not just biology.
- **Cancelled flu grant (anecdote).** His major single-cell customer, a well-funded influenza researcher working on predicting mutation for a universal vaccine, had a grant cancelled "for no reason." The expert called it "100% political" and said the researcher was told it did not align with "new directives." He lost his main single-cell customer as a result.
- **Student and postdoc pipeline.** New PhD students dropped to about 80% (of normal) last year because admissions coincided with announcements of grant cancellations. He fears for postdocs, since many need H-1B visas and universities would not pay $100,000 per visa; he says negotiations are underway to exempt universities. He says short-term damage over 2-3 years will be "very serious," PhD students will be fine, and losing postdocs would be "irreparable damage to universities." Interviewer and expert agreed postdocs do most of the real work.

## Illumina

**NovaSeq X Plus use.** The main revenue driver is whole genome methylation (reading chemical tags on DNA that regulate genes), where a few samples fill an entire flow cell. [Expert, Feb 2026]

**5-base kit (new Illumina product).** It lets one library and one round of sequencing read both whole genome methylation and whole genome variants. He calls it "something amazing" that "never existed." He has already run his first project on it.
- Depth: methylation alone still needs 30x coverage (each base read about 30 times); Illumina recommends 40-50x if variants are also wanted. His first trial used about 60x; he will reanalyse the data at 60x, 40x and 30x to see if anything is lost. "The jury is still out."
- Adoption: variant-focused researchers care only about variants, so he is running seminars with Illumina to show that methylation adds information. He is "really hopeful" it will drive interest and utilization. Whole genome methylation remains expensive because of the high coverage needed. Because of the higher recommended depth, the kit points to somewhat more sequencing per sample, not less.

**Methylation types (5mC vs 5hmC).** New kits can distinguish 5mC from 5hmC (two different chemical tags). Customers have only looked at methylated versus unmethylated and are slow to see why differentiating matters, though papers show different patterns in different cancers. Current approaches need two libraries and twice the sequencing, making it twice as expensive, which limits uptake. He speculates that more human and cancer funding would increase interest, and that human funding would also lift single cell, spatial, ATAC-seq (chromatin accessibility) and chromatin conformation work. His human project inflow over the past six months is "unprecedented."

**SomaLogic (acquisition recently completed by Illumina, per interviewer).** He finds the proteomics technology "extremely interesting" and "really amazing" because proteins are where genetic and methylation changes act. The limitation is cost: the initial probe set is "several dozens of thousands of dollars" ($30,000-$40,000). He expects the same path as single cell and spatial: researchers apply for grants, funded a year and a half to two years later. "It has a very high potential. But the time is not now because it is expensive."

**Apton Biosystems.** The interviewer said GenomeWeb reported Illumina bought Apton assets from PacBio. The expert had not heard of it and gave no view.

**Illumina behaviour.** Illumina has told customers to flag real competitive threats, and he does not think Illumina will "sit there" while others outcompete it. Illumina is not being aggressive with Fluent (its single-cell product) at his campus because of their partnership; he does not know about other campuses. He sees Illumina pitching an ecosystem rather than a single product.

**10x split (expert's account).** At the start of 2025 and mid-year, when Illumina launched its single-cell offering, 10x Genomics "broke off from Illumina" and recommended Ultima rather than Illumina for sequencing its libraries, removing NovaSeq instruments from its brochures. Core facilities "revolted" because they buy 10x kits and were told to send customers elsewhere. He says 10x has since understood and now says libraries can be sequenced on any system including Illumina. He listed this as another stressor for 2025.

## Competition in sequencing instruments

### Roche Axelios (SBX, expandomer chemistry)
- The interviewer cited a posted price of about $750,000.
- He says released data showed short read lengths, and quality scores "dropped drastically after 100 bases." The claimed 400-600 bases with duplex reads were not seen in the data. "That's not the threat for Illumina."
- Roche has said nothing about reagent costs. He is meeting Roche reps the following week for more information.
- Price view: "today, you can buy a NovaSeq for that money. Today, Illumina will sell you a NovaSeq for any money that you have." So $750,000 is "not that competitive." Even a $0.5 million price would not matter until the cost of a human genome is known. It would have to compete with Illumina's 25B flow cell (a high-capacity flow cell), and it would have to be very easy to handle.
- Versatility: the NovaSeq X Plus runs microRNA, many RNA-seq types and whole genome libraries. He will not buy an instrument that handles only two or three applications.
- Use as leverage: if Axelios is credible he would ask Illumina to compete on the 25B flow cell. The interviewer summarised this as negotiating leverage; he agreed: "Absolutely."
- Technology comparison: expandomers read each base directly, so accuracy should be higher than Oxford Nanopore, which infers bases from changes in electrical voltage across about 5 bases. Roche could compete with Oxford Nanopore only if it had real long reads. "I don't see that Roche has anything of interest to anyone" absent that.

### Other entrants
- **Ultima:** "Illumina, for the first time, has competition from Ultima." Parse sequences on Ultima.
- **Element:** the expert says Element will announce a new sequencer in March (2026). If it competes with Ultima rather than Illumina, "that's going to put a lot of pressure on Illumina."

## PacBio

**Revio versus Axelios for methylation and long reads.** He calls Revio "way better." It currently detects 5mC and m6A; in May 2026 an update adds native 5hmC, so it will read all three markers. It reads m6A in bacteria. Circular consensus (reading the same molecule repeatedly) gives accuracy "at least comparable" with Axelios "if not much higher," plus very long reads. "The Revio to me is the winner."

**Demand.** PacBio "year after year... has been surprising us." The queue is nonstop. A huge new grant for a human oral microbiome project, with thousands of samples over three years, will overwhelm the instrument. Large plant projects and private companies are choosing long reads. PacBio rose from about 10% of his lab's revenue to about 20%.

**Reusable flow cell (due May 2026).** He is "really looking forward" to it.
- Customers can buy reusable or non-reusable flow cells; both use the SPRQ chemistry (PacBio's newer chemistry).
- A reusable cell can be reused twice: if the first use yields 100 Gb (gigabases), the second gives about 70% and the third about 50%. Reagent cost is much lower.
- Price comparison for whole genome methylation per sample: about $350 on NovaSeq versus $700 on PacBio today. The reusable cell may close the gap enough that some projects shift to PacBio. He said it may also raise utilization enough that he would need another PacBio instrument.
- Typical PacBio yield for him is 70-80 Gb, "good enough."

**Vega (PacBio's lower-cost instrument).** Not for his lab because reagent costs are higher, so he would have to charge users more. He hears positive feedback from smaller labs and universities, and says it suits bacterial genomes.

## Oxford Nanopore (ONT)

- His lab owns a GridION but does not use it. He took it off the maintenance contract last year. He says ONT never had good customer support, and he always lost money with it, unable even to cover the maintenance contract.
- Last week ONT told him it is discontinuing the P2 Solo, which he had considered buying for ultra-long read requests. He will not buy the P2 Integrated: "that put the nail in the coffin."
- Reliability: for human DNA it works, but plant and animal samples contain polysaccharides, polyphenols and metabolites that co-purify with DNA and interfere with nanopores. GridION is supposed to give about 20 Gb, but he often got 1-2 Gb, and ONT would say it was "sample-related." He has not seen this with other technologies.
- Channel conflict: ONT sells to both facilities and end users (MinIONs). Postdocs who used it become faculty and bring their own MinION, which makes ONT "my enemy."
- Remaining use: ultra-long reads for de novo assembly across repetitive regions. Demand is too low to justify buying, so he outsources to a Wisconsin facility with ultra-long capability.
- He notes other facilities may have different opinions.

## Single cell and spatial

**Volumes.** Single-cell projects fell in the last quarter, partly because of funding and the loss of the influenza customer. Visium (10x spatial) projects were at most about one a month, sometimes fewer. Interest in single cell remains; demand is down. He says spatial is "not overwhelming anyone."

**10x pricing.** New 10x fixed-RNA applications may bring some samples to about $700 instead of $2,000, but cell dissociation and analysis keep total cost high.

**Visium share.** His lab was one of the few (maybe the only) in the Midwest with Visium; now Northwestern and UIC also have it (his speculation). He still thinks not offering spatial "doesn't look good for a facility." He hears no use of NanoString GeoMx. Xenium is excellent but expensive for whole-transcriptome work, since his users want all genes rather than a panel. Visium microscopy work moved to the neighbouring core.

**Parse (Ohio-based) as aggressive competitor.**
- Parse approaches PIs directly, does work in-house in Ohio, and sequences on Ultima. Prices quoted around $300 versus an actual full price of about $2,000.
- It won a large UIUC campus project, the first time a company has taken a project from his university: "Parse is our enemy. We will never buy absolutely anything from Parse," and he believes many cores feel the same.
- Quality: Parse's method has no microfluidics machine and relies on correct cell dilution, so it is "very error prone." With 10x, failures are usually core-facility failures; with Parse and Illumina single cell, failures stem from the technology. His opinion is that Parse's approach "is going to be the mice," that is, it will not last.
- Scale was sold to 10x Genomics (per expert), so Parse is the main other competitor to 10x.

## Reagent vendors and service entrants

- In 2025, vendors (QIAGEN, Zymo Research, Twist) also became competitors: Zymo offers sequencing services, QIAGEN offers single-cell services, Twist offers sequencing services. He calls it "the most strange thing that happened in 2025."
- Marketing is "very untruthful": an RNA-seq at $50 is only 3' mRNA-seq (end-of-transcript counting, not the full transcriptome), only from human cells or excellent quality RNA. People remember "$50 per RNA-seq."
- His negotiating stance: "if you want to be my competition, then forget about being my vendor"; he offers to sponsor seminars instead. Some agreed to stop; others said they would think about it.
- Plasmidsaurus: the company "trying to become the only sequencing service in the United States," "extremely aggressive this year." It takes business from everyone, and he sees the purchase orders showing lost business.
- He thinks this will continue because private companies are suffering and see revenue in sequencing, so "everybody eating each other."

## Expert's concluding view

"I think the demand for sequencing is still strong. It's just that there is no money." If funding returns, demand will rise. The problem is competition: Ultima against Illumina, and a possible Element launch in March. Core and sequencing-company revenues will be lower in the short term, but increased demand will "at least, keep us alive."
