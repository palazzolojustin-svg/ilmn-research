# Channel check with a senior director of genomic technologies (core lab, epigenomics) on Illumina demand, pricing, competition and new products, July 2026

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/expert_call/Expert_2026-07-06_Senior_Director_at_Biotech_Company_Optimistic_About_Sequencing_Technology_Growth_and_Marke.md` · Date: 2026-07-06 · AI-conducted channel check (expert-network platform, "AI Channel Checks", Advanced Sequencing: Illumina); expert is a senior director of genomic technologies at a biotech company (profile tagged "Doctor/Medical Professional"); AI Interviewer; published 2026-07-08, interview 2026-07-06 · expert call*

**Summary.** A senior director who runs a sequencing lab (two Illumina NextSeq 2000s, a PacBio Revio, ONT devices) says their Illumina output and consumables spend are both up about 25% year over year in Q2 2026 and for full-year 2026, as academic spending has normalized after a weak 2025. Illumina list prices were unchanged, but freight charges rose and the product range (SKUs) widened. Their lab's consumables budget has shifted from 75/25 Illumina/PacBio to roughly 50/50 because of the long-read Fiber-seq assay. The expert sees Element as the most successful challenger, Roche's AXELIOS as an initial minor threat, and is most excited about Illumina's StrataMap spatial product, while having little interest in TruPath.

## Expert background and relationship to Illumina

- **Role and seniority:** Senior director of genomic technologies at a biotech company (unnamed). The platform labels the perspective "Doctor/Medical Professional." [Expert: senior director of genomic technologies, Jul 2026]
- **Experience:** Has worked with sequencing since graduate school, nearly 20 years ago. Started on ABI SOLiD (an early sequencing platform), then moved to Illumina and several other instruments. More recently has used both PacBio and Oxford Nanopore (ONT) long-read sequencers (long-read machines read much longer stretches of DNA at once than Illumina's short-read machines).
- **Relationship to Illumina:** A current customer (direct, first-hand experience). It is a channel check, not a former employee or competitor. The experience is very recent: the interview was 2026-07-06 and the questions focus on Q2 2026.
- **Facility:**
  - Two Illumina NextSeq 2000 instruments (a mid-sized benchtop sequencer typical of sequencing cores).
  - One PacBio Revio on site.
  - Some ONT MinION devices (small portable sequencers) on site.
  - A third-party ONT sequencer for deeper PromethION runs (the "120 gigabase" runs).
- **Customers:** Split between academia and pharma/biotech. Academic customers mostly buy products and reagents; pharma and biotech customers mostly go through the lab's services department.
- **Illumina use:** Mainly epigenetic sequencing, meaning studies of chemical and structural DNA changes that control genes. Examples: DNA methylation detection; ChIP-seq-like assays (using CUT&RUN and CUT&Tag, which need less sequencing and give better signal-to-noise); ATAC-seq (chromatin accessibility, i.e., how open the DNA packaging is); and sequencing DNA from FFPE (preserved tissue) material. They rarely use the sequencers for whole genome sequencing.

## Shift of spend from Illumina to PacBio (Fiber-seq)

- The PacBio Revio competes with the Illumina machines for all the short-read assays above.
- Fiber-seq is an emerging long-read, multi-omic assay (it measures several layers of biology at once). In a single run it measures chromatin accessibility, DNA methylation, transcription factor footprinting, nucleosome footprinting and genetics. The expert called it "really powerful in the richness and depth of the data."
- Many customers are interested in Fiber-seq as a long-read alternative to ATAC-seq and DNA methylation, since it combines the two.
- **Spend mix (direct experience):** Last year about 25% of total sequencing consumables spend was on PacBio and 75% on Illumina. It is now "closer to 50/50." In dollar terms, usage is "approaching one to one."
- **Driver:** The lab shares new technologies with its large customer base through marketing emails. Customers are experienced epigenetics researchers who "get the value" and "just need help getting started." The expert described customer demand as the pull.
- The lab vetted Fiber-seq with its inventor, Andrew Stergachis (2020 Science paper), and has launched it as a product. "Customers get it. They love it when they see the data."

## Demand and volume (Q2 2026 and full year 2026)

- **Academic vs pharma:** Pharma and biotech demand has been "relatively steady." Academic demand was volatile and uncertain in 2025 because of the 2024 change in US administration, which had ripple effects on the NIH grant system. In 2026 versus 2025 the expert sees "a return to essentially normal academic spending." Last year their Illumina consumable spending was down; this year it is back up to normal.
- **Emerging worry (speculative):** The expert received an email that day about colleagues' concerns that the Office of Management and Budget is proposing changes to the grant review system, with fears of NIH budget cuts. The expert called this speculative and "very fresh."
- **Positives:** (1) Products in the lab's own pipeline, which it sequences heavily before release, lifting volume last quarter; (2) services, since as conditions normalize more customers return to the services group, raising both short- and long-read volume.
- **Q2 2026 output (gigabases, i.e., billions of bases sequenced) on Illumina instruments:** Up about 25% versus Q2 2025. Cited drivers: upcoming product releases and the uncertainty around NIH grants and funding. Output is exceeding expectations: "I've been pleasantly surprised." The expert had to re-up the consumables purchase order much sooner than anticipated.
- **Full-year 2026 forecast:** Output up about 25% year over year ("definitely increasing"). Drivers: confidence in market stabilization, new products (Illumina's releases bringing in new customers), and general health of science and clinical science. The expert would not tie this to the economy per se, but "things are looking more positive."
- **Sentiment change:** More optimistic than 90 days earlier, due to consistent growth in the epigenetic assays they track and customer interest.
- **Conference evidence:** At the AACR lymphoma conference (a cancer research meeting), there was much discussion of single-cell, clinical and spatial uses of short-read sequencing. These are high-throughput assays needing many reads each. No speaker voiced reluctance to continue with single-cell and spatial work in lymphoma.

## Consumables: spending, pricing, freight, inventory

- **Spend:** Illumina consumables and reagents (the kits needed for every run) spend is increasing in Q2 2026 versus Q2 2025, driven by overall optimism, R&D projects and "consumer take-up."
- **Volume not price:** The increase is "primarily due to volume, not due to pricing changes."
- **List prices:** Unchanged as far as the expert knows. The NextSeq 2000 P2 cartridge (their most-used) is still roughly $1,400 for 100 cycles, unchanged since launch.
- **Freight:** Illumina has raised its freight rate over roughly the past six months, by "a few percentage points of the total purchase order." Illumina quotes in six-month intervals, so the freight change can mean "a few extra cartridges or not." It is "noticeable."
- **SKU expansion:** Rather than price rises, the expert noticed more SKUs. The NextSeq 2000 now has four cartridge options: P1 (came out over the last year), P2 (most used), P3, and P4 (close behind P2).
- **Discounts and incentives:** No change in the past 90 days; discounts stable since they bought their NextSeq 2000. Reps have discussed new products and offered sample kits and reagents.
- **Inventory:** "Sufficient." In 2025 they thought activity had slowed and held "excessive inventory," worked it down, placed a six-month PO that was used about twice as fast as expected, then placed another. They like to hold one to two months of consumables, and that is where they are now.
- **Lead times:** Unchanged over 90 days; no delays.

## Competition

- **Instrument pricing:** The expert could not answer; they had not looked at instrument pricing in the last 90 days.
- **Top three Illumina competitors named by the expert:** BGI (Chinese), Element and Ultima.
- **Ultima:** Competes at the ultra-high-throughput end against Illumina's NovaSeq X (the "25-billion read" sequencers and above), serving "ultra-high-end genetics sequencing all day, every day." The expert has no good sense of Ultima's trajectory.
- **Element (AVITI):** Competes head to head with sequencing-core throughput (NextSeq 2000 class, "several hundred million reads"), for on-demand, versatile, quick-turnaround work. It has placed "quite a few," the expert believes hundreds, of instruments. The expert's former graduate school mentor runs one.
  - Historical, first-hand data point (two to three years ago, just before the NextSeq 2000 launched, not the last 90 days): the lab considered buying an Element. AVITI was competitively priced and its consumables were roughly half the cost of Illumina's.
  - Today: Illumina "has since competed better," but is still more expensive than Element's consumables. Element has improved its reputation and consistency. "Element is building its user base and doing quite well."
- **BGI:** Market in Asia is stable and growing. In the US, government restrictions, hesitancy, NIH grant-award rules and disclaimers about working with Chinese companies complicate growth. Stateside, BGI is "not growing quite as well as Element or Ultima."
- **Drivers of platform preference:** "Niche and cost." Ultima and Element serve two throughput tiers.
- **Next 12 months (opinion):** Because Illumina has the largest share, if competitors grow it may lose some share in some areas, but it remains competitive through its multi-omics and spatial proteomics portfolios and new products. Element will keep growing. Roche's AXELIOS (Xpandomer sequencing technology) should have "an initial burst" when released, expected within the next year though delays are possible.
- **Roche AXELIOS (the competitor product the expert watches most closely):** It could place itself at the forefront of clinical diagnostics because it is "fast, it's cheap, it's accurate." If pre-release specs are believed, "$100 genome in a short period of time." Clinical-oriented conferences (AACR lymphoma and ONT London Calling in London a few months earlier) focus on supporting physicians, with speed, cost and reliability the key criteria. Threat level: "initially, minor threat" until it proves itself; it could rise to "medium" if it delivers. This is based on marketing and pre-release specs, not direct use.

## Equipment purchase plans

- Probability of buying new sequencing equipment in the next 12 months: "50/50"; within two years: "very likely."
- If buying, there is an incentive to stay with Illumina for the ecosystem, software support, tech support and experience. Proteomics and spatial work is already being done on Element, whereas Illumina is only now bringing StrataMap (its spatial genomics product) to market, so its performance and cost-effectiveness are not yet clear.
- Over 90 days, likelihood of considering another instrument has increased because of market optimism and rising consumables use, but "we're still a ways away." A project needing a unique capability would have to drive any purchase.

## Illumina new products

- **StrataMap (spatial genomics/transcription profiling): the expert's most exciting Illumina product.** Spatial has been very expensive for researchers; with Illumina's experience and scale the expert expects a competitively priced offering. Interest at AACR lymphoma was high, since lymphoma biopsies contain heterogeneous cell populations, and spatial transcriptomics suits biomarker discovery and minimal residual disease profiling. Spatial is still a "frontier technology" where early adopters pay the highest costs, which should fall as throughput improves.
- **TruPath Genome:** Intact DNA is placed on the flow cell and tagmented in situ with Tn5, and a digital map of location gives a sense of the 3D genome, with advantages for translocations and some structural variants. The expert's criticism: "it's pseudo-long-read. It's not true long-read." Because they have PacBio Revio and ONT, which detect those variants experimentally rather than by inference, their likelihood of using TruPath is "quite low." It is "more of a curiosity, not a serious interest."
- **SomaLogic acquisition (proteomics):** Not the expert's field; it "doesn't really move the needle" for them and does not change their likelihood of increasing Illumina use.

## Customer support

Satisfied with Illumina's support; no recent changes that they know of.

## Expert's view of the future of sequencing

- The best platform balances cost, throughput, accuracy and read length. Short reads are typically under 1 kilobase; long reads are more than a few kilobases. In short reads the focus is cost per read, throughput and, increasingly, time to result (AXELIOS's speed is notable).
- The key trend is multi-omics (measuring several things in one assay). The expert believes Illumina has noticed this: SomaLogic for proteomics, StrataMap for spatial transcriptomics, TruPath to compete with long reads in structural variants.
- Quote: "the days of doing independent assays and then correlating them... has less value than measuring two things from the same material." Example: SHARE-seq measures chromatin accessibility and transcription in the same batch of cells.
