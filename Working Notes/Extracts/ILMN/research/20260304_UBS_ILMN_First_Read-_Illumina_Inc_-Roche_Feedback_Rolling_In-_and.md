# UBS First Read on Illumina: Mixed Expert Feedback on Roche's Axelios Sequencer Update, Competitive Overhang Seen Softening

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/research/20260304_UBS_ILMN_First_Read-_Illumina_Inc_-Roche_Feedback_Rolling_In-_and.md` · Date: 2026-02-26 · UBS Global Research (UBS Securities LLC); analysts Dan Leonard, Lu Li, Nathan Bolanos · sell-side research*

**Summary.** A short UBS "First Read" note says feedback from four experts on Roche's updated Axelios sequencer (plus a Doc Debates expert call hosted the evening of the note) is mixed: one very negative, one very positive, two muted. UBS concludes the update is relatively benign for Illumina and should soften the competitive overhang. The note keeps a Neutral rating and a US$135 price target on Illumina (stock US$127.40), with a forecast excess return of -2.5%. It lists strengths (yields, reusability, price per genome) and weaknesses (library prep, software, pricing, clinical reusability, launch timing) of Roche's platform.

## Overview and bottom line

UBS (Dan Leonard, Lu Li, Nathan Bolanos) published this "First Read" on 26 February 2026 (the recommendation was finalized 27 February 2026, 03:20 GMT). It reports early feedback on Roche's recent updates on its Axelios sequencing platform (Roche's new competitor to Illumina's sequencers). The feedback comes from four people (n = 4) plus a "Doc Debates" expert call UBS hosted that evening. [UBS, 2026-02-26]

UBS says its "initial take still holds": "the update is relatively benign for Neutral-rated Illumina (ILMN) and should soften the competitive overhang." (The "competitive overhang" is the worry that Roche's entry could take share or pricing from Illumina and weigh on the stock.) [UBS, 2026-02-26]

The feedback range is wide: one expert was very negative, one very positive, and two were muted or indifferent. UBS acknowledges this is a small sample but "imagine[s]" the distribution is reasonably representative of what it will find in the research community. UBS will keep gathering feedback and plans another Doc Debates call on this topic on Friday, March 6th (2026). [UBS, 2026-02-26]

## The positives (from expert feedback)

- **Yields were impressive.** Roche disclosed a figure of 40B reads per run (40 billion DNA reads, the short sequence fragments a sequencer produces, in each run) and 16 genomes per chip. Both were new. UBS describes them as upside to what Roche had disclosed previously, which was 7 genomes per chip.
- **Reusability data was clean.** The data showed no run-to-run carryover (no leftover material from one run contaminating the next). The expert said the chips can probably be reused "well more than 20 times." Chip life is "months, not weeks"; the latter "would have been a problem."
- **$150 per genome is attractive**, especially when judged against per-lane pricing (Illumina's flow cells are priced by lane). In this context Roche is described as cheaper than Illumina "by a wide margin," and the price per million reads is "especially attractive."

## The negatives (from expert feedback)

- **Library prep is a real issue.** Library prep (preparing DNA samples into a form the sequencer can read) is "very complicated." A user wanting duplex sequencing (reading both strands of a DNA molecule for higher accuracy) needs two different adapters, which means two different library preps. Roche also will not share the adapter sequences.
- **Software.** Anyone buying the instrument will have to "go through hoops on analysis like in the early days of Illumina." The data format will be different, and existing interpretation algorithms are not designed for Roche data.
- **$150 per genome is not compelling to this expert.** The expert says they are already at $175 per genome with Illumina discounts, and Illumina's new 35B flow cell likely brings Illumina pricing to $150 anyway. Larger users are already at $125 per genome, the expert believes. The expert "had expected Roche would price at $100 / genome."
- **Reusability not fully addressed, and expected to be a big problem in the clinic.** The expert queried their clinical users: 50% had no problem with reusability if properly validated, while for the other 50%, the pathologists, reusability was "a big no, a nonstarter." They did not believe Roche's slide fully addressed these concerns, especially in applications like minimal residual disease (MRD) testing (detecting tiny amounts of residual cancer DNA), where small errors matter.
- **Shipment target of summer seems like a stretch.** The expert said the platform is "nowhere near ready, lots of unanswered questions." They estimate at least six to eight months to ready the instrument for launch, and had thought it would be available sooner.

(The source text does not clearly mark which expert gave each point. The pricing, clinical reusability and timing comments are attributed to one expert who works with clinical users; the document does not name any expert.)

## Risks (UBS stated)

Company-specific: the NovaSeq ramp (Illumina's flagship high-throughput sequencer line) slows significantly; the sequencing market becomes saturated; pricing pressure drives sequencing prices down faster than expected; the clinical diagnostics market fails to open up; academic funding is cut.

Industry-wide: (1) reduced pharma spending due to major M&A, weaker funding for small companies, or trial failures; (2) slower global GDP growth, including escalating trade tensions; (3) pressure on academic and government budgets; (4) diagnostics pricing pressure from the Protecting Access to Medicare Act (PAMA); (5) changing competitive dynamics, including disruptive new entrants; (6) inability to compete effectively in China, a critical growth market; (7) foreign exchange or tax-policy changes.

## Company description (UBS)

Illumina (NASDAQ: ILMN, based in San Diego) is described as the largest provider of next-generation sequencing instruments and related consumables and services, as well as microarrays. It sells to academic and government labs, clinical labs, pharma, agriculture and consumer genomics companies, and has differentiated itself through rapid product cycles that have raised throughput and lowered the cost of DNA and RNA sequencing.
