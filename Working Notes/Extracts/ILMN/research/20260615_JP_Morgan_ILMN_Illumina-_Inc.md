# J.P. Morgan Illumina (ILMN) Customer Survey: Academic and Clinical Views on Usage, Spending Outlook and Roche's Axelios 1

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/research/20260615_JP_Morgan_ILMN_Illumina-_Inc.md` · Date: 2026-06-10 · J.P. Morgan Securities LLC, North America Equity Research (Life Science Tools & Diagnostics); analysts Casey Woodring (lead), Jaden Rismay, Sebastian Sandler, Marta Nazarovets Zaremba; completed and disseminated 10 Jun 2026 (prices as of 09 Jun 2026 close) · sell-side research*

**Summary.** J.P. Morgan surveyed 62 current Illumina customers (30 academic, 32 clinical; about 65% North America, the rest Europe) on how they use Illumina, how they expect to spend through 2028, and how they view Roche's new Axelios 1 sequencer. Customers expect instrument spend to rise roughly 19-31% per year and consumables spend to rise about 9% in 2027 and 13-14% in 2028. Axelios 1 interest is modest (purchase-intent 6.2/10 academic, 4.7/10 clinical, 40% unfamiliar), but 65% of those who would buy expect to reduce their Illumina footprint. Illumina is rated Neutral by J.P. Morgan at $160.03 (9 Jun 2026); the report states no new price target.

## 1. What this document is

This is a customer-survey report from J.P. Morgan's life science tools and diagnostics research team, led by Casey Woodring, dated 10 June 2026. It is a survey-based report, not a company model note. The stated purpose was to understand "how current ILMN customers view the competitive environment, with particular attention to the impact that the introduction of the Axelios 1 instrument may have on the sequencing space" [JPM, 2026-06-10]. Axelios 1 is a DNA sequencing instrument from Roche (the survey questions refer to it as "Roche Axelios 1"). Illumina (ILMN) is the market leader in next-generation sequencing (NGS, machines that read DNA at large scale), so a new rival from Roche is a potential threat to its instrument and consumables business.

The report carries a standard caution that survey comments are reproduced unedited, "should not be attributed to J.P. Morgan and are not representative of its views." The respondents' own comments are lightly garbled in places, which suggests free-text answers of mixed quality; they are used below only as colour.

## 3. Survey design and who responded

### Sample
- 62 current Illumina customers: 30 academic and 32 clinical. Lab location was about 65% North America and the rest Europe. North America was the focus because the report is about the Axelios 1's US-relevant competitive impact.
  - Academic: 22 North America, 8 Europe.
  - Clinical: 18 North America, 14 Europe.
- **Illumina access:** 33 access instruments through a core or shared facility, 23 own them, and 6 lease or rent them.
- **Purchase influence (all 62):** 23 joint decision-makers, 18 strong influencers (recommendations typically followed), 15 primary decision-makers (final approval), 6 with some influence.
  - Academic (30): 16 joint decision-makers, 8 primary decision-makers, and the rest influencers.
  - Clinical (32): 12 strong influencers and 7 primary decision-makers, with 7 joint decision-makers and 6 with some influence, as read from the chart.
- **Most common primary roles (all 62):** scientist/researcher (14), procurement/purchasing (8), lab manager/supervisor (7), lab director (7), then lab manager, lab or core facility director, principal investigator, bioinformatician, technician, molecular scientist and medical director/pathologist. Academic respondents were mostly scientists/researchers (14). Clinical respondents were most often lab managers/supervisors (7) and lab directors (7).

### Academic respondents (30)
- Organization types: 13 university/college research labs, 10 non-profit research institutes, 7 core/shared resource labs.
- Research areas (respondents could pick more than one): single-cell biology (22, studying individual cells), human genomics (22), cancer biology/oncology research (21), immunology, metagenomics/microbiome, infectious disease/microbiology, neuroscience, plant/animal genomics, plus single mentions of regenerative medicine, neurodegeneration, drug discovery and recombinant DNA.
- Budget: about 27% have an annual operating budget of $250k-$999k, about 47% $1.0M-$4.99M, and about 27% $5.0M-$9.99M. The respondent-weighted midpoint average is about $3.2M. For later analysis these became small, medium and large tiers.

### Clinical respondents (32)
- Organization types: 14 hospital-based clinical labs, 10 non-hospital reference labs, 8 academic medical center labs.
- Testing types (multiple allowed): oncology somatic, meaning tumor profiling (24), hematologic malignancy, meaning blood cancers (22), infectious disease/pathogen sequencing (18), germline hereditary testing (17), pharmacogenomics (13), HLA/transplant (8), reproductive/NIPT (5), other (6).
- Volume: 50% process 250-999 patient samples a month, about 41% process 1,000-4,999, and about 9% process 5,000 or more. The weighted average is about 2,140 samples per month. Tiers are small (250-999), medium (1,000-4,999) and large (5,000-8,000+).

## 4. How much customers spend on sequencing today

Clinical labs spend about twice as much as academic labs on both instruments and consumables (consumables are the reagent kits, flow cells and library-prep supplies bought for every run) [JPM, 2026-06-10].

| Measure | Academic | Clinical |
|---|---|---|
| Weighted average annual instrument spend (purchases, leases, rentals, major upgrades) | about $516k | $965k |
| Share of annual operating budget spent on instruments | about 16% | not given |
| Weighted average annual consumables spend | about $428k | $857k |
| Share of annual operating budget spent on consumables | about 13% | not given |

- Across all 62 respondents, about 53% spend under $499k a year on instruments, about 40% spend $499k-$2.9M, about 5% spend $3.0M or more, and about 2% don't know. The most common single bucket was $100k-$499k (about 34%).
  - Academic: <$100k and $100-499k were each 30% of academic respondents.
  - Clinical: 38% chose $100-499k and 31% chose $499-999k.
- For consumables, about 42% of all respondents spend under $249k a year, about 40% spend $250k-$999k and about 18% spend $1.0M or more. The most common single bucket was $100-249k (27% of respondents).
  - Academic: 30% chose $100-249k.
  - Clinical: 25% chose $100-249k and another 25% chose $250-499k.

## 5. Illumina installed base and share of customers' sequencing

### Instruments owned
The survey asked which Illumina instruments each lab has (multiple allowed). Across all respondents, MiSeq (a small benchtop machine) was cited by 77%, NextSeq 1000/2000 (mid-throughput) by 69%, NovaSeq X/X Plus (Illumina's newest high-throughput line) by 58%, and NovaSeq 6000 (the previous high-throughput generation) by 56% in the chart commentary. The summary page quotes NovaSeq 6000 as 16%, which conflicts with that 56% figure and with the clinical number below; treat the 56% as more consistent with the chart.

- **Academic:** "Other" and NextSeq 1000/2000 were each about 70% of respondents, followed by MiSeq at about 66%. By throughput class, high-throughput was 26% of instruments selected, mid-throughput 34% and low-throughput 22%.
- **Clinical:** MiSeq was about 88%, NovaSeq 6000 about 75%, NextSeq 1000/2000 about 69%. High-throughput was 38% of instruments selected, mid 26% and low 28%. Clinical labs lean more toward high throughput.
- **Non-Illumina platforms named:** Oxford Nanopore PromethION (both groups), Singular Genomics G4, PacBio Revio, 10x Genomics Chromium, and Thermo Fisher (S5, 3730xl, QuantStudio 5 in academic; S5 and Genexus in clinical). The discontinued Illumina HiSeq was the most-cited Illumina platform under "Other."

### When they last bought
Of all respondents, about 47% last acquired an Illumina instrument in 2022 or earlier, about 27% in 2023, about 21% in 2024, and only about 5% in 2025 through 2026 year to date. JPM's reading: clinical acquisitions were fairly steady over 2022-2024 and then dropped off in 2025-2026 YTD, while academic acquisitions faded as 2025 approached, "likely reflecting slower and/or tightening academic budgets." Recent academic purchases (2023 onward) came mainly from mid-sized labs, while some large academic labs bought earlier. Clinical purchases came from a more balanced mix of lab sizes.

### Share of each lab's NGS activity that runs on Illumina
This table shows the weighted share of each lab's total NGS work currently on Illumina and what respondents expect in three years, by lab size.

| Segment | Today | In 3 years |
|---|---|---|
| Academic small ($250k-$999k budget) | 47% | 50% |
| Academic medium ($1.0M-$4.99M) | 55% | 57% |
| Academic large ($5.0M-$9.99M) | 71% | 72% |
| Clinical small (250-999 samples/month) | 63% | 63% |
| Clinical medium (1,000-4,999) | 50% | 57% |
| Clinical large (5,000-8,000+) | 54% | 54% |

Takeaways from JPM: in academia Illumina's share rises with lab size. In clinical the pattern is less linear, with small labs the most Illumina-centric at about 63%. Medium-sized clinical labs are expected to raise their Illumina share the most of any segment (50% to 57%, a gain of about 7 points). Academic customers in all three size groups expect Illumina's share to rise about 1-3 points.

### Application mix (top three applications by sample volume, today vs. in three years)
The mix is expected to stay largely stable.
- **Academic:** whole genome sequencing (WGS) 26% today and 27% in three years; single-cell RNA-seq/multiomics 21% and 21%; bulk RNA-seq 19% and 19%.
- **Clinical:** oncology somatic panels (targeted gene panels on tumors) 23% rising to 26%; clinical whole exome/genome sequencing 21% and 21%; RNA-seq/fusion panels 14% and hematologic malignancy panels 14% today, with hematologic malignancy rising to 16% in three years. JPM notes that clinical remains "panel-led", with WES/WGS, RNA-seq and hematologic panels gaining share.

## 6. Spending outlook for 2027 and 2028

These are respondents' own expected growth rates versus the prior year. Each pair is the average for 2027 vs. 2026, then 2028 vs. 2027.

| Measure | Academic 2027 | Academic 2028 | Clinical 2027 | Clinical 2028 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Consumables spend growth | +9.0% | +12.8% | +9.4% | +14.0% |
| Consumables volume growth | +11.4% | +17.3% | +14.6% | +19.1% |
| Instrument spend growth | +18.6% | +19.0% | +22.2% | +31.0% |

### Consumables spend
- Academic: 93% expect an increase in 2027, most commonly +1-10% (60% of respondents). By 2028 the most common answer tilts to +11-25% (47%). The step-up from 2027 to 2028 is +3.8 percentage points.
- Clinical: 94% expect an increase in 2027, mostly +1-10% (56%) and +11-25% (38%). In 2028 the mix is led by +11-25% (66%). The step-up is +4.6 points. JPM says clinical respondents are expected to raise spending more than academic from 2027 to 2028.

### Consumables volume (runs and samples, as opposed to dollars)
- Academic: in 2027 most expect +1-10% (46%) or +11-25% (43%); in 2028 most expect +11-25% (53%). Average step-up +5.9 points.
- Clinical: in 2027, 66% expect +11-25%; in 2028, 69% expect +11-25% and 15% expect +26-50%. Average step-up +4.5 points.
- Volume growth (11-19%) is higher than spend growth (9-14%) in every case. That gap is consistent with respondents expecting lower price per unit of output. JPM does not state this inference itself; it only says "spending [is] underpinned by strong volume growth with step-up anticipated in 2028."

### Instrument spend
- Academic: 90% expect an increase in 2027, led by +11-25% (53%). In 2028 expectations are split evenly between +11-25% (33%) and +26-50% (33%), while 10% now expect a decrease of 26-50% or flat. The 2027-to-2028 step-up is only +0.4 points.
- Clinical: 91% expect an increase in 2027, concentrated in +11-25% (50%) and +26-50% (34%). In 2028, 69% expect +26-50%. The step-up is +8.8 points, the largest of any group.
- JPM's summary: both groups most commonly expect +11-25% in 2027; in 2028 clinical shifts to +26-50% while academic stays split.

## 7. Roche Axelios 1: awareness, purchase intent and timing

### Awareness and likelihood to buy
- 40% of all respondents were not familiar with the Axelios 1 and did not rate their purchase likelihood. The 37 who were familiar (18 academic, 19 clinical per the chart; the chart on the footprint question shows 19 academic and 18 clinical, so the academic/clinical labels may be swapped between slides) gave a 1-10 score (10 = very likely to buy).
- Average score: academic 6.2 vs. clinical 4.7. JPM says clinical customers "were a bit more hesitant." The most common academic score was 3 and the most common clinical score was 4, so the averages are pulled up by a minority of keen buyers.
- Of the 37 familiar respondents: 35% rated 7 or higher, 43% rated 4 or lower, and 22% were neutral at 5-6.

### Expected purchase timing (among those considering it)
- 8% in 6-12 months (skewing clinical), 35% in 12-24 months (skewing academic), about 32% in 24+ months (skewing clinical), 24% not sure (skewing clinical).
- Academic: 50% said 12-24 months, 22% said 24+ months, 22% not sure; only about 6% said 6-12 months.
- Clinical: 42% said 24+ months, 21% each said 12-24 months and not sure; about 11% said 6-12 months.
- JPM's read: the 6-12 month window was the least chosen, "suggesting customers likely want more time to assess the Axelios 1 before committing."

### Effect on Illumina if they buy
If the lab purchased an Axelios 1, 65% of all respondents expect to reduce their Illumina footprint.
- Reduce utilization of Illumina but not decommission: 63% of academic and 66% of clinical respondents (12 each in the chart).
- No change to the Illumina footprint: 26% of academic and about 17% of clinical.
- Decommission at least one Illumina instrument: about 11% of each group (2 respondents each).
- One academic-chart respondent was unsure.

### Reagent rental
The survey asked how an Illumina reagent rental option (where the instrument is supplied in return for committed reagent purchases) would change likelihood to buy the Axelios 1. This is a sign of how Illumina might defend its position. 39% of academic and 37% of clinical respondents said they would be somewhat or much less likely to buy the Axelios 1. About 10 academic and 9 clinical respondents said no impact, a small number (about 1-2 each) said somewhat more likely, and one said they already use reagent rental. JPM called the result "interesting."

## 8. What drives the decision on Axelios 1

Respondents ranked up to three factors. The percentages are shares of selections.

**Customers evaluating or considering the Axelios 1**
- Academic: data quality (accuracy, Q-score, error profile; 26%), turnaround time (15%), throughput/capacity (15%), cost per sample (15%).
- Clinical: data quality (18%), cost per sample (14%), throughput/capacity, vendor service/support and turnaround time (12% each).

**Customers NOT evaluating it (barriers)**
- Academic: cost per sample (18%), data quality (18%), workflow ease/automation (14%), assay/menu availability (14%).
- Clinical: data quality (21%), then cost per sample, throughput/capacity and turnaround time (13% each).

Data quality is the first or joint-first factor in every group, both for and against.

### Open-ended comments from those considering it
JPM summarises that they want high data quality and reliability, fast turnaround, high throughput, cost effectiveness, ease of use and automation, strong vendor support, and smooth integration with bioinformatics and IT workflows. Selected comments (unedited, from survey respondents, not JPM):
- Positive on cost: "the cost per sample is the most interesting part of Axelios and it is very good for gene counting applications."
- The same respondent's concerns: "the three-day prep turnaround time and low Q value for simplex mode are concerning." (Simplex mode is a sequencing mode the respondent associates with lower accuracy.)
- On maturity: "Read about its performance which is quite good but the clinical run is still not available." Another said the platform increases "speed, scalability and automation even though it is still an emerging technology."
- A respondent who is happy with their current supplier said they would only consider it "if only they will have good price offering and highly supportive with queries."
- A clinical respondent said it would be attractive for "real time sequencing output just like nanopore" and top-notch data quality, and another wanted flexible contract terms or "unique deals like dual hardware."

### Open-ended comments from those NOT considering it
JPM groups academic reasons as: already happy with the current vendor, data quality and Q-value concerns that must be proven, workflow friction (longer prep or complexity), budget limits, and the need for easy protocols, automation and vendor support. Clinical reasons: no intention to switch without a compelling reason and a strong contract, strict precision requirements, need for a wide assay menu and a user-friendly bioinformatics pipeline, cost per sample that is clearly competitive, and reliable support and workflow integration.

Selected comments:
- A core facility user described lock-in: their facility is built around NovaSeq X, with adapters and automation set up, so "It will take Tremendous amount of service, labor, and time to set up a new instrument," and staff are trained on the Illumina workflow.
- Several respondents simply did not know the product: "I am not able to consider since i do not know the brand."
- Clinical respondents cited satisfaction with incumbents: one is "extremely satisfied" with Thermo Fisher NGS systems and finds Illumina best at throughput of large samples; another uses "almost every product of Illumina" and does not want more vendors; another said "We are using Illumina and promethion for a pretty long time being and never faced any issues."
- A respondent noted they are "already contracted" with Illumina.
- One would consider the Roche model "mostly due to low cost per sample" and an easy-to-use machine.

## 9. Key conclusions from J.P. Morgan's summary

- Customers across both groups expect spending on Illumina-type sequencing to keep growing through 2028, with clinical outpacing academic, especially for instruments in 2028 (+31.0% vs. +19.0%).
- Academic interest in the Axelios 1 is higher than clinical, but awareness is incomplete (40% unfamiliar) and timelines are long, with the largest group saying 12-24 months.
- Most would-be buyers (65%) would cut Illumina usage rather than replace it outright, and only about 11% would decommission an Illumina instrument.
- Data quality and cost per sample are the decisive issues both for Roche's pitch and as hurdles; entrenched workflows and vendor relationships protect Illumina.
- Illumina's recent instrument placements are slowing (only about 5% of respondents bought in 2025-2026 YTD), which JPM ties to tighter academic budgets.

## 10. Caveats worth knowing

- The sample is small (62 labs, with only 37 familiar with Axelios 1), so averages move noticeably on a few responses. Spending growth figures are respondents' stated intentions, not forecasts by J.P. Morgan.
- The report contains a few internal inconsistencies: the NovaSeq 6000 share (16% on the summary page vs. about 56% in the chart text), and the academic and clinical sample counts on the Axelios slides, which appear swapped between slides. The "hypothetical purchase timeline" and "reagent rental" slides use N = 18 academic and N = 19 clinical, while the footprint slide uses N = 19 academic and N = 18 clinical.
- Respondents were all current Illumina customers, so the survey cannot show how non-customers view Illumina or Roche.
