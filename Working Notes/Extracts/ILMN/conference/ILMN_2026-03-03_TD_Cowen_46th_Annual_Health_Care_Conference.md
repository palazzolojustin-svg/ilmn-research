# Illumina CEO Jacob Thaysen at the TD Cowen 46th Annual Health Care Conference (fireside chat, post-AGBT 2026)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/conference/ILMN_2026-03-03_TD_Cowen_46th_Annual_Health_Care_Conference.md` · Date: 2026-03-03 · Seeking Alpha transcript of TD Cowen 46th Annual Health Care Conference; host analyst Daniel Brennan (TD Cowen Research Division); speaker Jacob Thaysen (CEO, Interim Chief Commercial Officer & Director, Illumina) · conference*

**Summary.** Illumina's CEO recapped the company's AGBT 2026 product roadmap (new NovaSeq X flow cells, Q70 accuracy, the $395 TruPath genome, large-tissue spatial, protein prep) and defended Illumina's position against Roche's new sequencing entrant. He characterized the 2026 guidance of 1%-3% organic growth ex-China as prudent with upside if clinical or research outperform. He also laid out Illumina's strategy to sell end-to-end "cost of insight" workflows and its BioInsight AI/data business, and reiterated a goal of at least high-single-digit growth by 2027.

# Illumina at the TD Cowen 46th Annual Health Care Conference, March 3, 2026

**Format and participants.** This was a fireside chat (a one-on-one interview on stage). The host was Daniel Brennan of TD Cowen's Research Division. The speaker was Jacob Thaysen, Illumina's CEO, who is also serving as Interim Chief Commercial Officer. Brennan opened by noting it was Day 2 of the conference and that the session wrapped up the day. The conversation took place shortly after AGBT (the Advances in Genome Biology and Technology conference, the main annual genomics industry meeting). Thaysen's tone was confident and upbeat. He was relaxed about competition, said repeatedly that he "feels good" about Illumina's position, and described the company as growing "very strongly" despite a competitive field.

## 1. Message from AGBT 2026: Illumina's product roadmap

Brennan asked what message Illumina took from AGBT about its own roadmap and how it viewed Roche and the competitive landscape. Thaysen said the show was "a really good conference" and that Illumina had been "very much looking forward" to showing its roadmap. His core message to customers was: if you invest in Illumina, the company will keep innovating so you succeed. [Thaysen, TD Cowen, 2026-03-03]

### NovaSeq X (Illumina's current flagship high-throughput sequencer)
- Thaysen called the NovaSeq X "a tremendous success so far."
- Illumina showed that it will bring more flow cells (the consumable slides on which sequencing happens). Some will serve higher-capacity applications and some lower-capacity ones, because "different customer segments look for different things."
- Illumina also showed a path to faster turnaround time on the NovaSeq X.
- Customers in research and academia reacted especially well, because funding there is currently tight ("not a lot of funding"). They liked that investments they have already made keep getting extended value.
- Thaysen said customers "really love actually the fact that we're not launching an instrument." Their existing installed machines continue to be improved. He called this "a big part of our confidence."

### Q70 accuracy
- Illumina showed it will reach Q70 quality scores. A Q score measures sequencing accuracy, and Q70 is a very high level.
- Thaysen said this opens the door to high-sensitivity MRD (minimal residual disease, meaning detection of tiny amounts of leftover cancer after treatment), "the fact that nobody can do this today." He described MRD as where "the whole market is moving right now."
- Timing: Illumina gave no specific date. Thaysen said that over the next 18 months "things will come out," and cautioned that this should not be read as everything arriving by a date such as "June '27." (Answer to a Brennan question on when Q70 is available.)
- Uptake: he said this kind of technology is "not a pop," meaning it will not go from zero to huge growth overnight. Clinical customers need time to get access, build it into their tests and obtain clinical validation. He said it "can really change how you think about MRD going forward" and will be "very attractive for many of our customers."

### TruPath (formerly codenamed "Constellation")
- TruPath is a technology that essentially eliminates library prep for whole genomes. Library prep is the lab step that prepares DNA for sequencing. Thaysen said library prep takes about 10 minutes and gives "extended insights from your genome."
- It combines short-read and long-read information: "all the power of the short read, but also all the power of the long read." He expects it to become "the new standard of the genome" for germline applications (inherited DNA) and hematology cancers (blood cancers).
- Price: launched at $395 per genome, which he called "the absolute lowest and highest quality, but the lowest cost of that type of genome."
- Multiplexing (running many samples together) roadmap: currently 16 samples per run, rising to "approximately likely around 96 samples over the next 18 months," which will lower the per-genome price further.
- Anyone with a NovaSeq X "can get started immediately."
- Pricing practice: Illumina discussed Constellation/TruPath for 9-12 months but only set the price when ready to launch. Thaysen said "We don't see any value in coming out too early with pricing. I don't think that benefits anyone."

### Spatial technology
- Spatial (measuring gene activity while preserving where it sits in a tissue) will let researchers look at very large tissue, "a whole slide," rather than very small areas "which you can't really do biology on."
- It is still in early access. Thaysen said Illumina was "overwhelmed with the interest" and expects to bring it out "during the next 6 months."
- Pricing intent: Illumina will price it to open up new applications. He said the conference claim was at least 4x cheaper from an insight perspective and "I think we can do even better than that."

### Competitors at AGBT
- Thaysen said most competitors were excited about new instrument launches. Illumina's signal was "it's not about only sequencing anymore, it's about the highest quality with the lowest end to end."

## 2. Roche and the competitive landscape

### Initial take on Roche
- Thaysen said Illumina has a good relationship with Roche people but will compete with them. [Thaysen, TD Cowen, 2026-03-03]
- His characterization: over the previous 12 months Roche presented "all the good things and all the promises," and now Illumina is "starting to get more of the details about all the consequences of that technology."
- Per Thaysen, the Roche technology "appears to be a very, very different workflow" and "appears to have some challenges in how you actually have to do library prep." Its pricing model "requires you to do high throughput," which limits the applications Illumina sees for now.
- He expects more information from Roche later but said Illumina feels "very good about how we're going to compete against Roche both short term and long term."

### Where AXELIOS 1 would and would not compete (Brennan's question)
- Brennan noted clinical is entrenched and takes time to switch, and asked where Roche's AXELIOS 1 would start and where it would not compete.
- Commoditization: Thaysen rejected the notion that sequencing is becoming a commodity: "not true at all." He said that in his long life-science career, commoditization in tools is rare, and sequencing is "definitely far way from that."
- Workflow complexity: Roche's system has one flow cell or chip with one lane, so users must think about how to mix and match applications. He also cited challenges with panels (targeted gene tests), which are especially important in clinical.
- Where Roche is strong: the "counting applications" (single-cell, bulk RNA and affinity-protein type work where sequencing is used to count molecules). Roche's simplex read can address this segment. Thaysen estimated that segment is "probably less than USD 100 million of the space that we participate in," and said Illumina feels good about that.
- Elasticity argument: in counting applications, library prep costs far more than sequencing. If sequencing price halves, researchers cannot run experiments twice as large; "You might be able to do 20% more." So cutting only the sequencing cost does not drive elasticity (more volume from lower prices).
- Researchers want to scale experiments from 10,000 to 100,000 to 1 million single cells. Thaysen said this is why Illumina acquired Fluent and SomaLogic and other pieces, to cut the cost of the whole workflow. He said "we feel good about our position in that space, even though the headline is a very cost per million read."

### Impact of Roche on the research guide and market behavior
- Brennan asked whether the guide includes any market share loss from Roche, even though Roche is not doing panels (it is excited about whole genome).
- Thaysen said Illumina takes insight about the competitive space into its expectations. He said even in a highly competitive space Illumina "should easily be able to grow with high single-digit growth over the next period of time as we have said in... our goal is in '27."
- He sees no market freeze: many NovaSeq X placements go into clinical customers who "have made their decisions" and will expand their fleets. Individual core labs may test other technology, but that is "the usual business."
- He said "I'm not seeing any freezing of the market at this point." This is also why Illumina is not discussing future instruments in its roadmap: "we don't want to freeze the market."

### Whole genome head-to-head (Brennan's closing Roche question)
- Brennan asked how large whole genome is for Illumina today in research and whether Roche's product looks viable. Thaysen did not give a size.
- He said Illumina feels "really good," especially with TruPath, where customers get short-read information plus "structural long readout." He argued "no other technology out there can actually provide the insight that TruPath can," with workflow simplicity.
- He sees Illumina as well positioned in genomes, in research first and then clinical.
- For short-read genomes, Illumina can still serve the market well. The value is "not only the price of sequencing, it is really the insight." He cited the Dragon pipeline (Illumina's analysis software), the Tertiary Analysis Platform with Amgen for rare diseases, and an ICI-linked platform for oncology as what turns a genome into insight. Customers "are not just excited about the price of the genome."

### Ultima and Element
- Brennan said Ultima and Element boxes had not had the traction some expected, though with some success, and asked about new price points or worries.
- Thaysen said there was nothing new. "Everybody is putting the best foot forward," he spoke with all of them, and the environment is "collegial," but "we fight every day on the streets, which we should." He said they are "trying to do what they need to," while Illumina continues to feel it has "a very, very strong position."

## 3. 2026 guidance and financial framework

### Guidance level and conservatism
- Brennan asked what levers could move results outside the 1% to 3% organic growth range excluding China, and whether the range is fair, conservative or aggressive.
- Thaysen said the conversation has changed. A year earlier it centered on NIH funding, China channels and tariffs. Now people ask if Illumina is conservative, "a much better conversation to have." [Thaysen, 2026-03-03]
- Reference base: 2025 had a challenging first half (macro hits) and momentum in the second half. Illumina treated the second half of 2025 as "probably a good proxy" for 2026: momentum in clinical, continuing challenges in research.
- Guidance structure as he described it: rest of world outside China 2% to 4%; including China 1% to 3%. He said Illumina is "always guiding for the midpoint."
- Upside levers: clinical growing faster than planned ("we don't see any reason why clinical shouldn't continue to be in a very strong trajectory") and research getting better than anticipated. Both would be "definitely upside to our guide."
- Caution: he cited the macro environment and said "Just last week, we had another big macro event," as a reason to keep "prudency" in the guide.

### Operating margin and EPS
- Operating margin (profit from operations as a share of revenue) on a core basis, before the SomaLogic acquisition, was guided to roughly 150 basis points of improvement (1.5 percentage points). Thaysen said another 150 bps the following year would put Illumina at a 26% operating margin.
- With SomaLogic included, the company is "within 50 basis points" of that path. He said Illumina will "work a lot" to try to close the gap.
- EPS (earnings per share): guided, before the acquisition, to double-digit growth of about 10%. With the SomaLogic headwind it is "a little bit lower than that." He said Illumina feels good about EPS growing "double-digit to teens going forward."

### Clinical consumables guide
- Brennan noted that clinical consumables grew about 20% in Q4 2025 and that guidance implied roughly 11% to 15% (TD Cowen's estimate, "at least what we have") for 2026. He asked whether this was conservative or biased low given the exit rate.
- Thaysen said the transition is still happening in 2026, so "there's definitely still some pricing headwind." It should dissipate through the year and "shouldn't be worse than '25."
- He said he can predict clinical growth only within about 1 percentage point. Could the number be a bit higher? "Potentially." But it depends on the environment, funding and macro, so the company keeps prudence in the guide.

## 4. Clinical growth drivers

Brennan asked which application is most exciting for clinical NGS consumables over the next couple of years. The exchange was light-hearted (Brennan: "pick your favorite child"). Thaysen did not name a single favorite.
- Sequencing is "far from standard of care" in health care and it is "early stages" of the opportunity. He sees growth in clinical for "many quarters, if not years."
- Drivers: physicians are more comfortable using genomic insights, and reimbursement is "starting to more solidify," improving predictability. As comfort grows, panels expand: from 20 genes to 500 genes and eventually the whole genome.
- Oncology remains the main driver. Most of the business today is therapy selection (choosing a treatment based on a tumor's genetics). Illumina is shifting toward MRD and early cancer detection.
- Timing: for "at least a year or 2" therapy selection will be the main part of the business and the main dollar growth, but in percentage terms MRD and early detection are "starting to kick in."

## 5. New product areas: protein, spatial, single cell

### Protein prep
- Brennan asked about the opportunity for protein prep (preparing protein samples for sequencing readout), noting it seems to be the biggest of the three newer areas (protein, spatial, single cell).
- Thaysen said it is the biggest now because the SomaLogic acquisition brought an existing revenue base, unlike the organically developed areas.
- He sees DNA sequencing for proteins as a big opportunity, with strong interest from pharma. Illumina can expand from the 9,500 proteins it now addresses toward the whole proteome of about 20,000 proteins, which he said "no other technologies will be able to do."
- Cost of the workflow: library prep dominates cost, so lowering end-to-end cost could create elasticity.
- Multiomics: early cancer detection data from a few weeks earlier suggested one dimension may not be enough. Combining genome, methylome (DNA chemical marks) and proteome could be needed, which suggests opportunities for smaller panels.
- Spatial proteomics: spatial today measures the transcriptome; later the proteome will be added, and aptamer technology (SomaLogic's protein-binding approach) is "very well suited" for that.
- He said Illumina and its new team in Boulder (the former SomaLogic team) are excited.

### Installed base and workflow maturity
- Asked whether Illumina has sized how much of its installed base could adopt protein prep, Thaysen said "not of this." The workflow is "a little too complicated" for the huge opportunity, so Illumina will work on it. Proteomics will first suit bigger experiments with many samples, where the cost of automation is absorbed. Over time Illumina wants a much smaller capital equipment investment to enter proteomics.

### Reporting
- Protein prep currently falls into library prep revenue. Thaysen said Illumina is "still considering how we're going to make sure that all of you understand how great this is going to go," implying possible future disclosure.

### Spatial pricing and the "cost of insight"
- Brennan asked whether "4x cheaper" meant a lower per-sample price at larger scale, or a flow-cell price cut, comparing to a ~$7,000 panel at 10x Genomics, with a ~$4,000-ish alternative, as his illustration.
- Thaysen did not give a price. He said Illumina prefers to talk about the "cost of insight" rather than the cost of sequencing: per cell for single cell, per sample for proteomics, per square millimeter or per cell for spatial. The aim is "aligning our pricing model with the insight they're trying to achieve."
- To do this Illumina needs to own the end-to-end workflow. He added Illumina "will still keep our ecosystem open": customers can use third-party single-cell or proteomic assays and library preps on Illumina systems, while Illumina offers a "very, very competitive end-to-end solution."
- Ambition: not one or two tissues but "hundreds, if not thousands," doing for spatial and proteomics what Illumina did for sequencing.

### Spatial versus single cell
- Asked if spatial is bigger than single cell, Thaysen did not rank them. He said Illumina is excited about high-volume single-cell (millions of cells) and big spatial experiments because they drive deep insight and a lot of sequencing.

## 6. BioInsight, AI and software

Brennan asked about BioInsight (Illumina's data and AI business, tied to a SaaS model and foundation models) and how important it is.

- Thaysen's argument: large language models only became possible thanks to huge internet data, and a scaling law holds (more data, bigger models, better insight). Biology lacks an "Internet of biology": data is dispersed, not standardized, and experimental details are unclear, so input quality is poor. BioInsight exists to create those datasets for building AI foundation models that predict biology.
- Why Illumina is positioned: scale; technology to sequence billions of cells (needed for "billions of insights"); compute via Dragon; in-house AI capabilities; and a genome-wide optimized CRISPR library (to knock genes in and out across the genome and pathways). (One program name was indecipherable in the transcript: "[indiscernible]".)
- Business model: pharma wants access to these datasets and will pay. Illumina will create datasets and sell them to pharma on a subscription basis, earning money while creating them. The accumulated data also helps build foundation models for individual pharma companies' drug discovery and deeper insight into biology.
- Customer benefit: researchers in Illumina's ecosystem get access to the data and models and can build their own; clinical customers get better interpretation. He envisioned a future where customers buy "insights," not just sequencing, and said Illumina can be "a very substantial player."

## 7. Pricing and other items noted

- 35B flow cell: Brennan asked whether the 35B flow cell announced at AGBT was priced. Thaysen said no. Illumina will not price it before it is ready to launch, and it has additional flow cells beyond those on the roadmap "if we need to."
- Consumables pricing headwind in clinical continues in 2026 but should fade during the year and not exceed 2025's (see Section 3).

## 8. Closing message

Thaysen's closing message to investors was that Illumina will grow "very strongly going forward, at least high single-digit growth even in a very competitive space." He said teams compete, that Illumina "has the best team, both in our R&D, but... also very importantly, in our commercial organization," and that he is "very proud of being a part of a company that is changing the world."

## 9. Points worth flagging

- Mix of facts and claims: the Roche critiques (workflow, library prep challenges, high-throughput pricing model, sub-$100 million addressable overlap) are Thaysen's characterizations, not independently verified in this document.
- The "4x cheaper insight" spatial claim and the $395 TruPath price are management statements at AGBT 2026; spatial pricing and the 35B flow cell price were not given.
- Guidance figures appear as stated by Thaysen; Brennan's 11%-15% clinical consumables range is TD Cowen's own estimate, not a company-stated number.
- The Q70, multiplexing (96 samples), and spatial launch timings are loose windows (18 months; next 6 months), not firm dates.
- Thaysen's 2027 reference is both a "high single-digit growth" goal and an implied margin trajectory (26% operating margin if another 150 bps is achieved the following year), with SomaLogic putting Illumina within 50 bps of that.
