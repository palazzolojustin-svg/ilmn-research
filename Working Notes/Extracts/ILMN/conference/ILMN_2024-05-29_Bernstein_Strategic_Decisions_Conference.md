# Illumina at the Bernstein Strategic Decisions Conference, May 29, 2024: new CFO Ankur Dhingra on strategy, GRAIL separation, end-markets, multiomics and capital allocation

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/conference/ILMN_2024-05-29_Bernstein_Strategic_Decisions_Conference.md` · Date: 2024-05-29 · Seeking Alpha transcript of the Bernstein Strategic Decisions Conference; host analyst Eve Burstein (Bernstein Research); Illumina speakers Ankur Dhingra (CFO) and Salli Schwartz (VP Investor Relations) · conference*

**Summary.** Newly arrived Illumina CFO Ankur Dhingra (about six weeks into the job) laid out three near-term priorities: return to revenue growth, operational excellence and higher profitability, and a fast resolution of GRAIL (spin-off or sale, both being run in parallel, with a decision targeted by end of June 2024). He previewed a strategy to be presented in the fall (September) focused on the sequencing "ecosystem", multiomics and sample-to-answer, with return-on-investment metrics and a profitability path from a roughly 20% core operating margin. He also discussed academic funding stability, clinical and research elasticity of demand, the FDA LDT rule, competition and the "free rider" problem, and a conservative capital allocation stance (investment grade, tuck-in M&A only).

# Illumina at the Bernstein Strategic Decisions Conference (May 29, 2024)

**Format and participants.** This is a fireside chat (a moderated one-on-one Q&A) hosted by Eve Burstein, who covers US life science tools and diagnostics at Bernstein Research. Illumina was represented by Ankur Dhingra, Chief Financial Officer, and Salli Schwartz, Vice President of Investor Relations. Burstein introduced Dhingra as having joined Illumina as CFO "just a couple weeks ago" (he later says he had been there about six weeks, "a month and a half"). Before Illumina he was CFO at Summit Therapeutics and earlier CFO at CareDx (a transplant diagnostics company), and spent a number of years at Agilent (a life science tools company). Schwartz has been VP of Investor Relations at Illumina for almost 2.5 years, and was before that head of Investor Relations and Treasurer at MSCI and spent years at Moody's. Audience questions were taken through the "Pigeonhole" app. The tone from management was confident, measured and deliberately non-committal on specifics, since Dhingra repeatedly said detail would wait for the strategy presentation in the fall.

## Opening remarks: the CFO's three priorities [Bernstein, 2024-05-29, Dhingra]

Dhingra said he and CEO Jacob Thaysen (referred to as "Jacob") had agreed on three near-term priorities:

1. **Getting back to growth.** Returning Illumina to revenue growth "remains priority number one."
2. **Operational excellence and profitable growth.** His focus as CFO is profitable growth. The company is "in the process of setting up what does baseline Core Illumina look like" ("Core Illumina" means Illumina excluding GRAIL, its cancer-detection subsidiary). Ahead of a strategy to be laid out "in the latter part of this year," the aim is both growth and "much higher levels of profitability, similar to what Illumina may have seen in the past."
3. **GRAIL (the most short-term item).** Everyone, "including us," wants a quick resolution so the company can focus on the core business.

### GRAIL: parallel spin and sale tracks [Dhingra, opening and Q&A]

- Illumina is running both scenarios in parallel: a spin-off (distributing GRAIL shares to Illumina shareholders so GRAIL becomes a separate public company) and a sale of GRAIL. The Board is considering both and "has given us the pathway to continue to run those."
- Investors may see filings needed to run both together, including an amended Form 10 (the SEC registration document used for a spin-off) and seeking approval from NASDAQ or the SEC. In parallel Illumina "may also be talking to companies about the alternative scenario for sale."
- The stated intention is to reach resolution "within this quarter," which Dhingra clarified means "end of June" 2024. Both tracks will run until it is "a very clear decision that there is only one of them that is viable," after which Illumina will communicate promptly and execute promptly.
- Reason for dual tracks: both paths require various regulatory approvals and "it's not a really short process." Illumina did not want to pursue a single path, find it not viable, and only then start the second. He said "as of now" the company feels confident of reaching a resolution this quarter.

## Strategy preview [Burstein Q&A, Dhingra]

**What the strategy will contain.** Dhingra declined to give the strategy itself. He said the company is stepping back to think about the genomics "ecosystem" Illumina has built over "two, two-and-a-half decades," which has made access to genomics "much more broad as well as efficient." He cited that the company is "realistically in a production environment at $200" (the $200 genome, a whole-genome sequencing cost target enabled by the NovaSeq X, being run in labs now), calling it "a great feat of both technology, as well as the science."

Key strategic themes he named:
- Thinking of the sequencer "potentially as a detector" (a view he brings from the life science tools side), with much more happening around the sequencer than the sequencing itself.
- Asking what role Illumina can play in making other parts of the sequencing workflow "scientifically more progressive, as well as cost wise more efficient," including **sample-to-answer** (taking a workflow from raw sample through to the final result), **multiomics** (measuring multiple biological layers, such as DNA, RNA and proteins, together) and **elasticity** (the idea that as sequencing gets cheaper, customers use far more of it, enabling higher-resolution applications that were previously unaffordable).
- The second part is financial: **returns**. Illumina has invested heavily across the ecosystem, and the plan is to say what returns are expected from these investments, how they are measured, and what "Core Illumina, excluding GRAIL, could potentially look like" over the next several years.

**Long-term financial targets.** Asked whether Illumina would offer long-term revenue or EPS guidance, Dhingra said "in some form," without committing to EPS specifically. He intends to set expectations on where Illumina sees leverage in its P&L (profit and loss statement), "likely going to be across all lines of the P&L." Noting the current "20-ish percent core operating margin," he said he would provide "some sort of algorithm" for the profitable-growth path over the next couple of years, while saying "I don't know the answer exactly what it would look like."

**Product announcements.** New product announcements at the strategy event are "at lower probability." He framed it as "more like an Investor Day less an R&D day type of discussion," a strategy conversation about where to take Illumina rather than a specific product discussion, adding "don't hold me, I may change that later on."

**Timing.** In a later answer, he said "we'll talk about it in September," and that the back half of 2024 will give "a very clear view of what Core Illumina baseline is," including earnings power and cash generation power, beyond the operating-margin P&L already disclosed.

## Investment intensity versus growth, and the "free rider" problem [Burstein, Dhingra]

**Are heavy investment and cost-cutting at odds?** Burstein, quoting Michael Porter on strategy being about what you will *not* do, asked whether investing at historical high levels to restore historical high-single to low-double-digit growth conflicts with acknowledging that may not be possible and cutting costs. Dhingra's answer:
- It depends on where a technology is on its "S curve" (the adoption curve). Outsized investment is needed early, when you are "market making." At Illumina's current stage he does not see the two as much at odds.
- Illumina has built an ecosystem and "people really know the value of what the sequencing is." Most changes now are about pushing the envelope of the science, where customers can clearly say how it helps them (new tests, or running tests more efficiently). Return expectations on R&D and marketing differ from "what Illumina was 15 years ago."
- Internally, the team is "getting there." One of his most positive observations from the leadership team is the understanding that "we have a leading science position, but the size of investments that we need to make going forward... may not be that outsized as they were in the past as a percentage of revenue."
- With the right return expectations and prioritization (which "by connotation means the areas that we will not work on"), "I think we can drive a good revenue growth in this market." He described the market as "not in early stages of technology development, but still early stages of adoption."

**Market-making spend and free riders.** Burstein noted Illumina discussed strategic partnerships on its Q4 call (the earlier quarterly earnings call), which brought revenue but lower margin, and asked when the burden of building the market goes away, and how Illumina thinks about "credible competitors" benefiting from its investments. Dhingra said:
- Every new market goes through this cycle: the first entrant invests far more than second and third followers, who benefit. But followers also help expand the market by making the technology more available and acceptable.
- He sees the size of market-making investment potentially going down. The transcript reads "within 2024. So I can say within this decade where I'm not going to say 2025, but certainly within the time horizon that we're talking about." (The wording is garbled; he appears to say it will not necessarily happen in 2025 but within the strategy's time horizon.) Most customers "really know now what they're getting into."
- Customer conversations now concern what cheaper sequencing enables (whole genome, whole exome, or more sequencing-intensive tests such as MRD, i.e. minimal residual disease testing, which looks for tiny traces of remaining cancer), not adoption of the technology itself. "That's the power of this ecosystem where we make one step change and what does it enable in the entire ecosystem."
- Do followers get a free ride? "Yes, they do." What they lack, he said: "a 25,000 instrument base in the world," and a "stable, high quality, highly reliable instrument," which is also getting technology upgrades in hardware and via analytics ("Kraken" in the transcript, appearing to refer to Illumina's analysis software, either in the instrument or available in the cloud). New assays are enabled at the front end, and many are backwards compatible with one or two generations of older instruments. The ecosystem took "couple decades to build" and "we don't intend to just back away from it." He thinks investors under-appreciate this.

**Is pure sequencing commoditizing?** Burstein asked whether platform sequencing is commoditizing and value now lies in the ecosystem. Dhingra:
- Sequencing becomes a commodity only if many companies can replicate the same quality, capability and experience. "I don't think we're anywhere near commoditized."
- However, sequencing cost is "not the largest part" of end-to-end cost in either research or clinical settings. Drawing on his CareDx experience, he said more time, labor and money go into collecting samples, bringing them to the lab, staff running the test, library preparation (preparing DNA for the sequencer), analysis and delivering results.
- Illumina will not play in every one of those steps, but sees areas where it can scientifically make a difference. Example: whole-genome sequencing, where Illumina could make that part of an assay easily available so customers can build diagnostics on top. A whole-genome run "in a 15 hour run at a low price point" lets the ecosystem think about tests differently.

## Academic and government market [Burstein, Dhingra, Schwartz]

**Funding risk.** Burstein argued NIH (US National Institutes of Health) funding is at an all-time high in inflation-adjusted terms and faces political risk this year, so downside outweighs upside. Responses:
- Dhingra: even at Agilent, predicting government funding was "highly questionable." But "so far what we've seen, is that the funding has been stable to slight growth this year as well," based on academic partners and the Street. He sees "good bipartisan support for the science at least so far" and has heard no discussion from academic partners of cuts or program questioning. The risk of a change "will always remain," but he sees no signs of it now.
- Schwartz added two points: (1) Illumina tracks many grants through customers, and the number and dollar amounts of the grants tracked "have persisted"; (2) the genomics-relevant portion of funding "has been holding up better and growing a little faster than the overall NIH budget."
- Dhingra: even single-cell analysis ends with a sequencer in the back end, so Illumina enables a portion of the research.

**Strategic angle: KOLs and pharma partners.** Dhingra said the strategy will give detail on Illumina's relationships with KOLs (key opinion leaders, i.e. leading researchers/clinicians) and pharma partners. Illumina wants its largest customers to have visibility into its pipeline and to be "participative in where Illumina is going." He cited the MRD partnership with BMS (Bristol Myers Squibb) and J&J (Johnson & Johnson). Illumina has been working with leading KOLs on their next programs and "we're seeing excitement around that." He also said KOLs with large programs needing "a different level of measurement" may access funding if Illumina's pipeline enables the work, so the relationship runs "both ways": funding available, and enabled science pulling funding in.

**Share loss to single cell and spatial add-ons?** Burstein asked whether Illumina loses share of an academic grant (for example a $2 million grant) when researchers also pay for single-cell or spatial instruments and consumables. Dhingra:
- Single cell and spatial runs, despite their own front-end costs, drive much more sequencing than traditional samples, so consumable consumption is "much, much higher." There is "a transition period," referencing "the valley" discussed previously (a temporary dip as customers move to newer technologies).
- Illumina measures this: actual gigabase (Gb, a unit of sequencing output) is much higher. Asked "higher than what," he said a core genomics workflow on an S4 flow cell (a high-capacity NovaSeq consumable) moving to single-cell analysis could require "30%, 40%, 50% higher" core sequencing output, and single-cell by design requires more flow cell consumption. Without the lower cost of sequencing, such tests would not be economically viable. "And so we still get a fair share there."

## Clinical market and elasticity [Burstein, Dhingra, Schwartz]

**Burstein's framing of Illumina's view:** people will do more (bigger panels, more coverage) and clinical applications remain under-penetrated, which drives growth.

**Dhingra's additions:**
- The clinical market is a "longer cycle market": getting a test approved and into clinic, and moving to newer technology, takes much longer because of the cost of regulation and validating a test on a new technology. This is true of sequencing and other diagnostic modalities.

**FDA LDT rule.** Burstein asked whether the FDA's new final rule on LDTs (laboratory-developed tests, tests designed and run within a single lab, which the FDA is now regulating more strictly) slows clinical players' ability to add depth (she cited Guardant, which has a CGP test, a CDx version and a bigger LDT version), delaying elasticity. Dhingra:
- "Well, if they don't, somebody else will." If the science is enabled and cost-effective, someone will develop the test.
- Clinical elasticity appears in two steps. First, test development: companies building new tests (for example MRD) will use the cost and capability of the NovaSeq X (called "X") to develop tests that are viable scientifically and economically. He believes "roughly about a third of our Xs got sold to the clinical customers" and his thesis is that this "is largely still going into their research efforts." Second, taking tests into the clinic through approval, where the final LDT rule could mean a transition for "grandfather" tests. It may take "a year longer or whatever? We'll see where it eventually lands."
- Medium to long term, "somebody will go develop that test," and Illumina "is positioned to benefit either ways irrespective of which player takes that position" (specialty lab, core lab or new lab).
- Schwartz added that Illumina can differentiate through its infrastructure in clinical affairs, medical affairs and quality, having "run the gauntlet globally through multiple different regulatory systems," and can help customers with clinical validation paths as they adapt to new requirements: "it's an opportunity."

**Clinical customers are mostly research.** Burstein noted Illumina has said about half of revenue is from clinical customers, but that bucket includes clinical research, so elasticity may look like academia (a fixed budget stretches to more samples as cost drops). Dhingra agreed that is one aspect, but said the more important one is enabling tests that "I did not believe were viable in the past" or that were "shelved." That is "the most enticing part" of research-setting elasticity, discussed with KOLs and clinical customers looking at the X and Illumina's assay work. "Will there be people who will take advantage of the lower cost? Yes, everyone will."

**Truly clinical revenue (diagnostic or therapeutic).** Asked what share of revenue this is and whether it has any elasticity, Dhingra said he did not think Illumina has disclosed the mix that precisely and it was not top of mind. Customers validating a test would get some benefit from moving to the new flow cell and newer technology, but for most, the discussion is as much about **menu expansion** (adding more tests). He said Illumina wants to get closer to customers to show what is coming over the next two to three years and align R&D, with menu expansion being what "gives us a higher share as well."

## Multiomics and the sample-to-answer vision [audience question via Burstein, Dhingra]

**Question.** Burstein said multiomics has been a "real buzzword" on Illumina's last call, noted papers about "hacking" Illumina machines to do it, and asked whether expanding into more of the workflow would mean competing with today's single-cell and spatial partners, who currently drive sequencing volume for Illumina.

**Dhingra's answer:**
- Illumina sees opportunity in the front end of the sequencer, where preparation happens, in end-to-end workflows. Even though sequencing is intense, much money and science sits in front-end steps and "we think, we can develop some parts of that ecosystem as well." Technology could be integrated in front of the sequencer to enable more science.
- He has done, in six weeks, "a full pipeline review of what's going on within our R&D programs" and was "almost blown away" by what is happening, including how R&D spend splits between the instrument ("box") and assays, and the front-end work is "phenomenal." It needs to "go through the process that it needs to go through before it shows up in front of the market."
- Example: the SomaLogic partnership on proteomics (measuring proteins). The team is working to simplify that workflow, integrate it tightly at the front end with fewer manual steps, and improve the science, and to integrate back-end software ("Kraken" or "ParTech" in the transcript; spellings uncertain), making workflows tighter and data easier to generate and use. He sees similar opportunities in other omics, not just proteomics.
- The vision: the sequencer as "the core instrument around all of the omics." This is happening in the ecosystem today, but "we really haven't built it in a tightly managed way." Some existing players will have to consider their plans as the science evolves, but Illumina believes it has "both the right as well as the opportunity to go improve the science in those spaces."

**Follow-up: displacing partners or manual processes?** Burstein summarized that the aim sounds like displacing manual or inefficient processes (the "lab tech") rather than partners. Dhingra: "We'll see where... the share... actually happens." Because adoption is still early, the market is expanding enough that Illumina acting as a market enabler "will likely create more market opportunities than thinking about it as a fixed pie out of which Illumina is actually taking share." He added that the economics of removing manual steps matter, "but in the end, it's still about the science... it has to do more than what's available today."

## GRAIL timing and execution [Burstein, Dhingra]

- Asked whether the end-of-June finalization is still on track, Dhingra said yes: "to get to a decision clearly articulated, communicated in the next little over a month."
- Both tracks have different timeframes from the decision, but either needs approval from the EC (European Commission, the EU regulator that had required Illumina to separate GRAIL). A spin requires working with NASDAQ and the SEC and ensuring the EC is comfortable. Illumina has already taken steps with the EC, with "a framework that they've already agreed to."
- For a sale, Illumina must return to the EC to confirm the transaction fits the framework and that the EC sees the buyers as viable players. Dhingra expects a sale "may have a slightly longer" timeline because of the additional EC steps, whereas in a spin, outside the NASDAQ and SEC approvals, "we control the process more." Illumina is prepared for either and intends "to move as expeditiously as we can within that framework."
- Months or years? "As of today, we're still thinking months. I'm still thinking within 24," which appears to mean within 2024.

## Capital allocation and M&A [Burstein, Dhingra]

- Dhingra's aim is for the business (Core Illumina) to generate free cash flow "and a pretty good amount of it," then decide how to deploy it.
- On whether cash goes to reinvestment or shareholder distributions: "Judiciously." He cannot lay it out yet ("We'll talk about it in September"), but the approach "is going to be more prudent... probably on the conservative side and in a more balanced use of cash." On leverage, "I intend to remain investment grade, here in the near-term, establish a good runway for the business." The business has "a lot to do" and he would rather keep it focused on its priorities.
- Illumina has done "few tuck-in M&A" (small acquisitions) and will probably continue to look at tuck-ins for adding new technology capabilities, with remaining cash deployed in some way.
- On large-scale M&A, Burstein noted that a prior executive (named "Joydeep" in the transcript; presumably Illumina's previous CFO) had said tuck-ins were on the table but large M&A probably was not. Dhingra agreed in substance: with the three priorities (top-line growth, core business, higher operating performance "commensurate for the quality of the business"), "it isn't our intent right now to then layer on a really large acquisition." BD (business development) teams have not been asked to look for one and there is nothing on the horizon. "There is a lot within our strategy that we can do, ourselves."

## Closing remarks [Dhingra]

Asked what he expected to be asked beyond how Q2 is progressing, Dhingra said investors often ask "how long will this last" about the near-term growth. He stepped back to say the ecosystem built over two decades is "really, really strong," that individual businesses will continue to be built on it, and that "we really don't have to go out to build those. That capability resides within us at this point in time." The task is to focus on the right things, which he said the teams are doing. He is "excited about what this business can look like within the next few years, both in terms of top-line performance opportunity, as well as where this could be from a margin performance perspective."
