# Illumina CFO Ankur Dhingra at the Wolfe Research 2024 Healthcare Conference (fireside chat with Doug Schenkel)

**Summary.** Illumina's CFO describes seven months of progress on a transformation plan aimed at returning to high-single-digit revenue growth, and addresses post-election concerns (NIH funding, China, tariffs). He explains that the gap between 40% growth in clinical sequencing activity and roughly 5% consumables growth in Q3 2024 was largely price, as customers moved from NovaSeq 6000 to NovaSeq X. # Illumina CFO at Wolfe Research Healthcare Conference, 20 Nov 2024

## Setting and format

This is a roughly half-hour fireside chat (a moderated Q&A on stage) at the Wolfe Research 2024 Healthcare Conference on November 20, 2024. The host is Doug Schenkel, lead analyst for tools, diagnostics and labs at Wolfe Research. The guest is Ankur Dhingra, Illumina's Chief Financial Officer. Salli Schwartz was also welcomed by the host. Schenkel set the agenda: the state of the company, implications of the US election (held about two weeks earlier, which he describes as "six days after or seven days after"), the clinical end market, a Q3 2024 "cleanup" and the 2025 and longer-term outlook. Dhingra gave no formal guidance. Schenkel asked about 2025 and Dhingra answered "Of course not" to the idea that he would guide. Tone throughout: confident and upbeat on execution and cost, candid and cautious on macro conditions.

## Progress on the transformation (seven months in)

Schenkel framed the CFO's mandate as restoring revenue growth, improving operating discipline, and deploying robust free cash flow both above and below the operating line. He noted Dhingra joined about seven months earlier. Dhingra said he started in April 2024 and is "very pleased with the progress." He said "we are in transformation" and cautioned that "transformations take time, improving cultures take time."

### Strategy and the divestiture of GRAIL

- In August 2024 Illumina set out a strategy to return the company toward high-single-digit revenue growth and to improve operating margins and earnings power over the next few years (a three-year horizon) [Dhingra, Nov 2024].
- Illumina has divested GRAIL (its former cancer-screening spinout). Dhingra said it kept "a small portion of it," but GRAIL is "essentially... now completely divested from Illumina."

### Growth plan: three components

1. **Core consumables and instruments back to growth (the largest component).** Consumables are the reagent kits customers must buy for every sequencing run. This depends heavily on the high-throughput business, where Illumina has its largest strength and market position. Dhingra said the transition, as laid out in the Q3 2024 results, "has been going quite well." He expects that by the second half of 2025 "a major part of that transition" of customers and volume onto the X platform (NovaSeq X, the newer high-throughput sequencer) will be complete, with significant progress made in Q3.
2. **Multiomics (measuring more than DNA, such as single-cell and protein data).** Described as an additional layer of growth.
3. **Services and data strategies.** Illumina has several smaller services businesses that were historically managed "more like programs" rather than as formal businesses with defined portfolios and people who know how to scale them. Near-term focus is building management, team and portfolio capability. A leader for this business has been hired and has started building a team, and specific data and AI strategy pieces are being formed. Dhingra said this will make the smallest contribution to the return to high-single-digit growth over the three years.

### Change in how Illumina works with customers

- Starting in August 2024 Illumina opened up a good part of its product pipeline and began giving key customers early access to products.
- Two benefits: Illumina gets feedback well before commercial launch, and clinical customers, who must choose technology well before launching a clinical test, can build on newer capabilities.
- Dhingra said early customer comments are "phenomenal." He has put several customers in their labs and brought core lab directors to Illumina's offices, and many said they had never had a meeting like it with Illumina.

### Multiomics and core technology items named

- Acquisition of Fluent (single-cell technology).
- A partnership on the Protein Prep side. Dhingra said these two are already becoming part of the portfolio.
- Changes to the "backbone genome" technology, which Illumina calls the "constellation map." He pointed to data presented by Neil at ASHG (the American Society of Human Genetics meeting) showing "tremendous potential." He said he and Jacob (apparently CEO Jacob Thaysen, though the transcript gives only the first name) are heavily focused on this.

### Cost structure and capital allocation

- Since joining, there has been an additional push on reprioritizing investments and spending discipline. Some early work began before him.
- Illumina has launched several programs to structurally change its cost base, in manufacturing, G&A (general and administrative overhead) and international capability. Dhingra expects incremental improvement over the next two to three years, delivering the operating margin expansion previously discussed.
- He said Illumina has very strong cash earnings power, and its "cash conversion is significantly stronger than anyone else in the tools" industry.
- Illumina now has a "very clearly defined M&A strategy" with defined targets and has begun executing; Fluent is the example. It is also working through share repurchases and has enough cash for continued tuck-in (small) acquisitions.

## Post-election topics

Schenkel said three election-related topics came up repeatedly for Illumina: NIH funding, China and local competition, and tariffs.

### NIH and US research funding

- Dhingra said genomics has historically enjoyed bipartisan support, and Illumina is connected to both sides of the aisle and watching what actually gets written into policy, "because it is eventually what that matters."
- Historical data point: under the same administration in 2016-era years, NIH funding "actually grew during those three to four years." He added Illumina is not anticipating growth this time.
- Typically, when NIH funding holds flat, genomics grants have held up well, and money going to genomics has typically been growing, in oncology and broader healthcare research.
- Exposure: about half of Illumina's business is in the US and about half is in research, so roughly 25% of the business is "in some ways exposed to government funding." The portion directly from NIH or the government is "really small"; the exposure is mostly through grants customers receive. Dhingra said Illumina lacks visibility into all of it.
- Schenkel clarified the 25% is total US research, not just academic and not just NIH. Wolfe's own work suggests government-funded research (NIH, NSF, Department of Energy) is about half of that and direct NIH exposure is low single digits. Dhingra said "You're probably not too far off." (Schenkel stressed he was not asking Dhingra to bless the numbers.)
- Schenkel added that NIH has not seen massive cuts in decades, including in the last Trump administration when it grew above average, and that during slower NIH growth, endowment and industry funding tends to stabilize things. Dhingra agreed: grants from private foundations and endowments "[have] remained pretty robust overall."
- Mitigation levers: Illumina helps customers write grant applications. Research dollars have been going to single-cell and protein analysis, and simplifying workflows and lowering their cost helps customers win funding. Dhingra conceded that "if NIH dollars get cut, there will be an impact to our business, hard for us to quantify."

### China

- China is where Illumina has historically faced the most competition and has the lowest market share, with competitors BGI and MGI plus newer, smaller entrants.
- Illumina's China business has shrunk quite a bit over two to three years but has stabilized for two to three quarters at about $75 million per quarter, roughly $300 million a year. Dhingra said it is too early to call this a trend or a return to growth.
- Schenkel's framing, which Dhingra confirmed ("That's right"): China is about 7% of sales, down about 25% year over year so far, but roughly flat in dollars at about $75 million a quarter.
- Competition: local competition is intense, but much of it is among Chinese companies. Illumina's customers remain customers, and it sees less competition from smaller players directly, while MGI and BGI face rising competition from others. Competitive intensity "remains very strong as it has always been."
- Access improving: a new China leader (Jenny, in the role about five to six months) has restructured the commercial team and regulator relationships. Over the last two to three months, regulatory inputs have "improved slightly." Illumina is allowed into more hospital tenders (public bidding processes) and to sell directly to hospitals, and there are discussions about operating from certain free trade zones previously off-limits. Dhingra called these "small incremental steps," positive relative to the broader macro commentary, but said real business impact takes time.
- Plan: sell more directly into opening channels and deepen regulatory and hospital relationships. China remains a leading nation in clinical genomics, wants leading technologies, and Illumina "intend[s] to remain invested."

### Tariffs

- Manufacturing is fairly global. A sizable share of instrument manufacturing and part of consumables manufacturing is in the US, with three US factories. The second-largest manufacturing base is Singapore. Non-US supply-chain exposure is largest in the Singapore and Taiwan area.
- Only a very small portion of manufacturing is in China, and what is there is for China.
- Illumina is doing a "layer 2, layer 3" deep dive of its supply chain to find components and subcomponents from Chinese suppliers, and is working to qualify alternative non-China suppliers.
- Dhingra's conclusion: "in aggregate, it's not a very large exposure," and with US and Singapore manufacturing Illumina may have levers to shift production depending on what policy emerges.

## Q3 2024 clinical end market: volume versus revenue

### The 40% versus 5% question

- Schenkel recalled that on the Q3 2024 call Illumina said high- and mid-throughput sequencing activity grew 40% year over year. Wolfe's model has high- and mid-throughput consumable revenue up about 5% in Q3 (high throughput above mid), and even at 10% there is a large gap. Dhingra confirmed the 40% and said the gap is "Largely price."
- He noted the price decline in the quarter was at the higher end of the range Illumina provided, which gets to "about, say, 25% decline on the price." (Schenkel referred to a "40% or 30%-ish pricing hit.")
- Why price falls: customers moving from the NovaSeq 6000 to the NovaSeq X pay much less per unit of data.

### Dhingra's read: "I was pleased"

- Asked whether the numbers were worrying, he said he was pleased. It was a quarter of accelerated transition, with "a little over 10% of our volume" in high throughput moving from the 6000 to X, and consumables still grew at the reported pace.
- That is "a proof point" and gives confidence that even with a steep move in a quarter, "till the time the overall sequencing activity holds, we will see a good flow-through to our revenue."
- Timeline: by the second half of 2025, if more than 75% of volume has moved to X, the price transition will be "largely annualized" (the price decline will already be in the year-ago comparison), so more volume growth should flow to revenue, bringing Illumina closer to the high-single-digit growth model over three years.

### Clinical gigabases and customer visibility

- Schenkel noted Illumina said 40% of clinical gigabases (units of sequenced data) were generated on the X. Dhingra called it "a good spot from a transition perspective."
- Customers share transition plans while validating (they need Illumina's help optimizing software pipelines). Many plan to move one test in Q1, a second in Q2, staging over several quarters rather than all at once.
- Illumina has the best visibility into its largest customers, which it "hand hold[s]" through the transition; smaller customers sometimes buy an X and Illumina learns afterward. Dhingra said this visibility gives confidence the transition is going well.
- Conversations with large customers about next tests include how the constellation map and DRAGEN (Illumina's analysis software) could add value and potentially give higher specificity. Dhingra: "the elasticity conversation is almost always part and parcel of the transition discussion." (Elasticity here means lower per-unit cost leading customers to run more or deeper sequencing.)

### Elasticity and who will not move

- Schenkel summarized that data volume up 40% against 5-10% revenue growth shows customers sequencing "deeper and wider," so elasticity is not dead. Dhingra agreed ("Absolutely").
- He said some customers will, in his personal belief, never move existing tests off the 6000, preferring to put resources into developing new tests with higher revenue potential rather than revalidating existing ones. Some clinical customers still use MiSeq and have never moved to NextSeq or the 6000.
- The bigger discussion is applying a whole-genome sequencing base to additional tests in oncology and other diseases. Illumina has enriched its DRAGEN pipelines and AI models so that, with library prep and improved software, it can "dramatically reduce the overall cost of the workflow." Dhingra said this gives line of sight to new tests under development, which customers do not want disclosed.

## Mid-throughput pressure

Schenkel said he feels better about high throughput but mid-throughput improvement has taken longer than expected, noting Illumina is over-indexed to mid throughput in China and faces more competition in the West. He asked whether to assume continued pressure for the next few quarters.

- Dhingra: "We are not assuming any macro to improve."
- There has always been competition in mid throughput. Sometimes Illumina has had to go back to an IP-based (intellectual property) discussion with competitors; in other cases the argument is Illumina's value to the ecosystem.
- Driver one, "by far" the largest: because of macro constraints, customers have shifted sample volume to high throughput, and mid-throughput customers are sending samples to core labs or other sequencing providers that benefit from lower high-throughput pricing. Dhingra drew on his prior experience as CFO of a pharma company running a genomic biomarker-based drug trial that relied on a CRO (contract research organization) or send-out testing.
- He believes that when funding conditions improve, pharma and biotech companies focused on genomic biomarker-based drugs will bring lab operations back in-house, even at the higher mid-throughput cost, because of the speed of responding to trial results and new indications.
- Two new Illumina products may squeeze mid throughput from both sides: a lower-throughput NovaSeq X with a single flow cell (a lower-capacity version) priced "well below 1 million" (dollars), and the higher end of the newly launched MiSeq i100, which can address some mid-throughput needs.

## 2025 framing

Schenkel noted consensus around 4% revenue growth in 2025 and about $4.40 of earnings per share ("lots of 4s"; the EPS figure is marked uncertain in the transcript). He said Wolfe has confidence in Illumina's self-help (cost and capital) levers, and that the open question is revenue.

- Schenkel's list of factors: continued high-throughput improvement as pricing works through; continued mid-throughput pressure until the initiatives take hold; potential upside from SomaLogic/SOMAmer (protein technology), the new MiSeq, and Fluent; and a risk of customers pausing instrument purchases amid NIH uncertainty and a difficult environment.
- Dhingra said "That's good framing" and "Directionally, based on things that we've laid out, that's exactly how it is." He expects continued transition to X, with a larger part of volume transitioned by the second half of 2025. On macro: "we have no reason to assume that it is going to improve... We'll call it when it actually happens." He said the launches Schenkel outlined are ones Illumina is "super excited about."
