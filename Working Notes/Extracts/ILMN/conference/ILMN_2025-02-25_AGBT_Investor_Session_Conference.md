# Illumina AGBT Investor Session, February 25, 2025

## Format and tone

This was an investor session held at AGBT (Advances in Genome Biology and Technology, an annual genomics technology conference), run by Salli Schwartz, Illumina's Head of Investor Relations. It was entirely Q&A, with no prepared remarks, and was webcast as well as held in a room. Schwartz asked analysts to limit themselves to one question because the speakers could not write questions down. She asked media to direct questions to the PR team.

Management's tone was confident and combative toward competitors. Thaysen repeatedly framed the competition as a contest between sequencer makers and end-to-end workflow providers. Barnard was pointed about competitors' technical claims. No financial guidance or earnings figures were given. The only financial data points were the NovaSeq pull-through figure and Dhingra's mid-quarter instrument comment, both noted below.

## Opening question: the one thing each executive wants investors to focus on

Schwartz asked each executive to pick the product they were most excited about.

**Thaysen (CEO) chose Constellation.** [AGBT, 2025-02-25] He called it "an unbelievable cool technology that completely sets us apart" on workflow simplicity and on the insight it gives into the genome. He said he expects it to become the standard for rare diseases and for germline sequencing (inherited DNA, as opposed to tumor DNA). He said nobody else can provide the same insights.
- His evidence was a session the previous day in which Dr. Stephen Kingsmore described seeing hemophilia A (a genetic bleeding disorder) variants. Thaysen said this had not been possible before, and that "even other long reach techniques have difficulties" with it.

**Barnard (CTO) chose the spatial offering.** [AGBT, 2025-02-25] Spatial means measuring gene activity while preserving where in a tissue it occurs. He said Illumina now has "a complete portfolio… across the omes, from proteomics down to genomics" and across bulk, single-cell and spatial modes. He said this is being completed this year and is "a huge inflection point for the community and for Illumina."

**Dhingra (CFO) chose Perturb-Seq in single-cell.** [AGBT, 2025-02-25] Perturb-Seq is an experiment type that deliberately disrupts genes in cells and reads out the effects.
- Illumina just launched a 5 billion cell atlas program, to be built over "the next two or three years at the most." Thaysen later said this is with the Broad Institute.
- Dhingra said it would let scientists manage perturbations across entire cell lines rather than a small set of segments.
- He said it lays the foundation for a pharma strategy. This ties to the third pillar of Illumina's growth strategy, the "rightmost column" on software and services.
- He called these "new pieces of hyperscale technology" and said Perturb-Seq could materially improve the speed of drug discovery. Layering multiomics (genetics plus proteomics) on top could support meaningful services.

## Constellation versus long-read competitors (Subbu Nambi, Guggenheim)

Nambi asked whether Constellation can compete head-to-head with existing long-read players on multiplexing (running many samples together) when using an entire flow cell.

**Thaysen** said Constellation is not only aimed at the long-read market, though that is a big opportunity. It also creates deeper insight in markets Illumina already serves, such as rare disease, at a cost level consistent with Illumina's usual pricing. [AGBT, 2025-02-25]

**Barnard** set out the numbers. [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Illumina claims 16 samples in 24 hours on a 10B flow cell, using eight lanes. Each is a complete genome with phasing (which copy of a chromosome a variant sits on), structural variants (large DNA rearrangements) and all the usual variants.
- PacBio, in his words, gets "maybe three genomes incomplete" with a two-day library prep (versus 10 minutes for Constellation) and over 24 hours of sequencing.
- Oxford Nanopore (ONT) takes about three days for one sample, and "you're not going to get the variants at the levels you need for diagnostics."
- His conclusion: "There is no comparison across the market today."

## Roche's new sequencing technology (Dan Brennan, TD Cowen)

Brennan asked for Illumina's high-level view of the specs Roche introduced and how Roche's entry would affect Illumina's share and strategy. Roche was to present later that day.

**Thaysen** said: [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Illumina has been competing in this space for a while and it is competitive.
- Customers are no longer buying just a product. They "are buying into a whole ecosystem" of applications, workflows and informatics.
- Illumina already offers an end-to-end workflow. He pointed to the MiSeq and an automated sample-prep system on the show floor, where samples go directly into the sequencer.
- Roche has shown a "technology overview," not a product launch. He said he could not speculate on details that have not been shared.

**Barnard** described two lines of competition. [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
1. *Whole-genome diagnostics.* Roche's technology is short-read, "may be a little bit longer, but it's still classically short read." That puts it in the same group as traditional short-read data. Constellation moves Illumina to a "new dimension" with variants, phasing and structural variants, plus 10-minute sample prep. "No short read technology has that."
2. *Counting applications* (RNA, single cell, spatial). The majority of the cost lies in library prep (preparing samples for sequencing), not sequencing.
   - Spatial library prep is classically a two-day process and the kits are very expensive. That is why there has been no "really big science in spatial."
   - Sequencing is typically 10% to 20% of total cost for these experiments, so cutting sequencing cost does not change science. "You're focusing on the wrong Pareto."
   - Illumina's acquisition of Fluent (scalable library prep that needs no instrument) and its new spatial release, which gives flexible choice of tissue area and sequencing depth, address this.
   - He questioned whether Roche really understands the NGS (next-generation sequencing) marketplace and what customers need.

## Cost of insight: Constellation, single cell and spatial pricing (Dan Arias, Stifel)

Arias asked for numbers on "cost for insight," for example whether Constellation at 16 samples on a 10B flow cell equates to $1,000 per genome, and what a single-cell experiment would cost.

**Thaysen's answer:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- *Constellation:* It is in early access, currently one genome per lane. Pricing has not been fully disclosed, but Illumina has said "it's in the hundreds of dollars, it's not in the thousands of dollars."
  - Multiplexing (several genomes per lane) is coming and will lower cost. Illumina showed one genome per lane to get people working on the technology.
  - Illumina will be clear on application-specific pricing and thinks Constellation can be priced at a premium.
  - Do not take the cost of a 10B flow cell and divide by eight. "We want to make this competitive. We want to open up markets with it."
- *Single cell:* For big programs the metric is cost per cell. For small projects it is cost per experiment. Because Illumina owns sample prep, sequencing and informatics, it can drive "a cost per cell that is at least two times less than anybody else out there."
- *Spatial:* Priced very differently from competitors.
  - Competitors' surfaces take only very small tissue. Illumina can sequence whole slides. Thaysen, citing his pathology background, said pathologists want bigger areas, and human tissue needs "centimeters of insights," not millimeters.
  - Pricing is intended to scale from one or five experiments up to hundreds or thousands.

## Roche in diagnostics (Puneet Souda, SVB Securities)

Souda asked how Illumina views Roche as a diagnostics competitor, noting Roche's products also compete with some Illumina customers, and noted that Thaysen competed against Roche before. He added a second question on nanopore data seen in the spatial slides.

**Thaysen on Roche:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- He said he knows Roche well. At his prior company (named in the transcript as "DARCO", likely a transcription of Dako), he competed against Ventana (Roche's tissue diagnostics unit) in both commercial roles and R&D, "very successful actually."
- Diagnostics requires companion diagnostics, tests developed with pharma companies to go with a specific drug. Pharma prefers to work with independent firms. Whoever has the most companion diagnostics will succeed.
- Illumina's TSO 500 (TruSight Oncology 500, a tumor profiling test) is now an FDA-approved kit with companion diagnostic claims, and Illumina is adding more to its labeling.
- Clinical customers "do not care about technology, they care about insight." Putting a new technology into the IVD (in vitro diagnostics) market "takes a long time" and cannot be done fast and cheap in two or three years. It must perform repeatedly in decentralized, production-environment labs, not just in the hands of a key opinion leader (KOL).
- The clinic needs a full workflow, sample in to result out, including automated sample prep, sequencing and informatics. Illumina has all of this and expects "a very strong presence."
- A brand name alone does not establish a new technology: "it remains to be seen."
- On vertical integration: Roche now combines Foundation Medicine, Roche diagnostics and sequencing. Thaysen said many customers who might otherwise buy from Roche "might consider twice," since they would then be dealing with a vertically integrated competitor. Many customers, and pharma, want an independent supplier.

**Barnard on nanopore:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Illumina has built "a very nice IP portfolio in nanopores," but it is one research direction. Illumina's R&D approach is to provide total solutions, with optical sensing and electronic sensing each having a place. It will pick the right modality and chemistry.
- Illumina will not release a product unless it meets future quality expectations, "essentially Q30 now." (Q score is a measure of base-calling accuracy; Q30 is about 1 error in 1,000 bases.)
- A technology launching at Q20 has to make up the gap in library prep, "exotic, time consuming" prep. He said this will be seen in Roche's offering, which he described as "three boxes," unlike Constellation, which "the sea level" (likely "C-level") can run. Thaysen said "I've done it."
- By the time these products reach the clinical market in two to five years, expectations will be above Q50, driven by "needle in a haystack" applications such as MRD (minimal residual disease, detecting tiny amounts of leftover cancer DNA). If the starting point is too low, library prep cannot fully compensate. The start has to be Q30, then library prep can reach Q40, Q50 and possibly Q60.
- Roche's positioning as "a clinical company" is "a big question mark."

**Thaysen added:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Duplex sequencing (reading both strands of DNA to improve accuracy) requires sequencing substantially more, "so it comes with a penalty," among other undisclosed penalties.
- Native Q score must be above at least Q30 to be a relevant product.
- The MiSeq i100 has a CMOS readout (a chip-based detector) and can sequence in four hours today. That is a direction available if speed matters, and Illumina knows how to make chips and could make them bigger and more powerful. SBS chemistry (sequencing by synthesis) offers many more options.
- Illumina has historically built tools that serve many applications, but could optimize for individual applications if it chose.

## Pricing strategy and adjacencies (Doug Schenkel, Wolfe Research)

Schenkel asked whether the new products (Constellation, single cell, spatial, proteomics) are not just new revenue but levers to protect sequencing spend per assay. The idea is to price adjacencies aggressively so customers get the lowest total cost even if rivals have cheaper sequencing.

**Thaysen** answered: [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- The thinking is "highest quality insight for the lowest end-to-end" cost, so customers optimize on the true cost of an experiment rather than one piece.
- Historically sequencing was the largest cost, so Illumina worked on that. In single cell, spatial and proteomics, sequencing is a smaller part, so lowering its price does not much lower total experiment cost.
- Owning the whole workflow lets Illumina cut sample prep cost. In spatial, prep is "so expensive that it's prohibitive" for large experiments. Customers would then run bigger experiments and sequence more, to Illumina's benefit.
- This is the logic behind moving "from cost per gigabase to end-to-end workflows." Few companies can do this because it requires investment across all parts of the workflow. "Just coming out with a sequencer" will not be the success criterion.

## Ultima, reagent rental, and partners in spatial (Tycho Peterson, Jefferies)

Peterson asked three things: how Illumina responds to Ultima, which recently won business from Chan Zuckerberg and UK Biobank and says it earns good margins; whether Roche might use reagent rental (a model where the instrument is provided and revenue comes from per-test chemistry, as Roche does in immunoassays); and how Illumina manages relationships with partners such as 10x Genomics now that it competes directly in spatial.

**Thaysen on reagent rental:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Instruments are a relatively small part of Illumina's revenue. Illumina already has reagent rental agreements.
- It does not work well in academia, which is grant-based, but in the clinic, where volume is known, a reagent rental or price-per-test model is often right. Customers like it because the price paid ties 100% to the reimbursement level. "We know how to play that, we will go there also in the clinical market."
- Owning the whole workflow lets Illumina adapt pricing to a customer's budget, whether CapEx (capital) or OpEx (consumables).
- A NovaSeq buyer generates pull-through (recurring consumables revenue) of "1.3 million per year," and Illumina knows they will sequence for the next five to six years, so it can build models around customer lifetime value.
- He did not directly address Ultima's wins in the transcript.

**Thaysen on partners:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Illumina is not competing with its customers; it is a life science tools company. It will continue to collaborate with partners even where technology overlaps.
- His stated commitment: "we will always make sure that they're successful on our platform." Partners also work with competitors, which is fine. Illumina does not try to lock customers in.
- On competitors: "some companies like to compete in press releases." He said the $5 billion cell project with Broad "is real," will be delivered over the next three years, and Illumina will do more than 5 billion cells. (The transcript says "$5 billion cell," which from context means 5 billion cells.)

## NIH and academic customer conversations (Patrick Donnelly, Citi)

Donnelly noted tight academic budgets, scrutiny of instrument purchases, and that one AGBT chair had travel canceled because of NIH (National Institutes of Health, the main US research funder) turmoil. He asked what Thaysen is hearing and when conditions might loosen.

**Thaysen:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Customer conversations at the meeting have been "very positive." Everyone, especially US academics, is concerned about the NIH situation.
- Many customers say budgets are still there and they can spend. Some see volume still flowing through core labs (shared facilities). Others worry more about future grants than current ones.
- It is a period of "more concern, more risk." But everyone sees sequencing and multiomics as the future of where grant money will go "when things are starting to operate normally again. And they will."

**Barnard:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Under NIH headwinds, customers want reliability and vendors with a long history of delivering on time and at quality. They are afraid of taking risks. This has deepened relationships with core labs, big institutes and KOLs, especially around emerging technologies.

**Thaysen:** "No CEOs ever get fired by hiring McKinsey… Nobody is going to be blamed with going with Illumina." He called Illumina both the safe choice and the most innovative company. [AGBT, 2025-02-25]

**Dhingra (new data point):** Of the instruments (the transcript says "excess," likely a transcription error for "boxes") placed so far in the current quarter, measured to mid-quarter, "half of them have gone to research." People are still buying and taking delivery. [AGBT, 2025-02-25]

## Accuracy and "future-proofing" the genome (Dan Brennan, TD Cowen)

Brennan referred to a Q55 accuracy slide shown the previous day. He asked whether Illumina expects trade-offs by instrument or will match every metric competitors cite, and whether in a year or two it could deliver a genome better on all facets, so that the roughly $8 billion Illumina spent on R&D over the last 10 years is "future proofing the business."

**Barnard:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Constellation delivers expanded information on medically relevant genes that no one could unlock before. The Kingsmore result is an example, and it "is not just coming from Illumina."
- Illumina will layer Q55 on top, to serve needle-in-a-haystack applications such as MRD, which is gaining acceptance in diagnostics. Higher Q scores mean more confidence calling a signal above background. It is in the R&D pipeline.
- Illumina is "redefining what genome quality is" across cost and throughput, quality, and clinical interpretation.
- The debate has been Q scores versus callability. "If you ask a physician, he could care less about the Q scores." What matters is whether a variant can be called in the genes or variants of interest.

**Thaysen:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Some competing technologies cannot call parts of the genome. You might call 95% of the genome, but if the missing 5% holds many relevant variants, it does not matter. "If you can't measure BRCA" (a key breast cancer gene), comprehensiveness fails.
- The industry should move past standard terminology and focus on what customers need.

**Dhingra** added that the comprehensiveness of what Illumina brings in 2026 or 2027 is the point, and that the Constellation map "paves the path for our most medically relevant genome going forward." [AGBT, 2025-02-25]

## Spatial roadmap: FFPE and protein (Tejas Savant, Morgan Stanley)

Savant asked whether FFPE compatibility (FFPE: formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded, the standard way clinical tissue samples are preserved) could be available on spatial by commercial launch next year, and whether the protein prep work could add protein detection to spatial.

**Barnard:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- FFPE is on the roadmap but "in research." Work is starting now. A release is probably in 2026, "probably second half of someplace." More progress will likely be revealed at the next big conference.
- Protein: "absolutely." The next step for both single cell and spatial is to layer in protein, also in research. The roadmap also layers epigenetic state through the methylation "five base" genome. That technology can apply across bulk, single cell and spatial.

## China and the unreliable entity list (Jack Meehan, Nephron Research)

Meehan asked for any update on the unreliable entity list (a Chinese government list that can restrict foreign companies) and on competing in China.

**Thaysen:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Illumina is "still operating completely in China," selling instruments and consumables and serving customers daily with a great local team.
- Chinese customers have told Illumina they want it to stay. It is important for clinical studies and clinical use, and seen as the main innovation leader.
- At the last check-in, Illumina had known of the listing for only 48 hours. Since then there have been "multiple conversations with the right parties in China" and the dialogue continues.
- "For the time being we are operating completely as we have done and no change."

## Spatial technical challenges and the informatics stack (Kyle Mikson, Canaccord Genuity)

Mikson asked about difficulties getting to FFPE or protein detection given the chemistry, any IP (intellectual property) concerns given other technologies, and how much of a differentiator the multiomics software is.

**Barnard:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Those difficulties are "why it's in research right now." Illumina is working on unique chemistry and doing freedom-to-operate (patent clearance) work, which is standard in research. He raised no specific alarming IP issue.
- Software: a single-cell experiment may take three days to run but three weeks to analyze with heavy compute cost. Illumina Connected Multiomics (ICM) will cut three weeks to hours and provide state-of-the-art visualization across data types.
- This is driven by the Partek acquisition, "still sort of the leading visualization software." The goal is for customers to focus on biology rather than building technology. He expects ICM to be a major differentiator as products become de facto standards.
- Three durable differentiators: (1) DRAGEN (Illumina's hardware-accelerated analysis, using FPGA and CPU acceleration) saves money and cuts analysis cost; (2) the AI and machine learning team, which has "more access to data than anyone in the world," with results fed back into ICM; (3) multiomics layered on top for a complete picture of biology.

**Thaysen:** [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- The informatics stack gets too little attention and is "really resonating." Many research customers now use Illumina pipelines.
- Customers do not want to stitch tools together, including "an Excel sheet to change my format." They want end-to-end plus flexibility. Once they use Illumina's tools "you're not going to move over and make your own home brew anymore," which is a reason they will stay with Illumina for a long time.
- Barnard's team has a large AI capability for analyzing big data cohorts and building foundational models (large AI models trained on biological data) to help pharma and academia interpret biology. Thaysen said a few pharma companies have this and Illumina is one of the few others. He tied this to Perturb-Seq and helping pharma find targets and accelerate drug discovery.

## Is R&D money just shifting between baskets? (Unidentified analyst)

The analyst said genomics R&D budgets have not grown as in the past. New entrants (Element in mid-throughput, Ultima in high-throughput) and new technologies (spatial versus single cell) appear to move money from bucket to bucket, with more cannibalization recently. He asked whether Illumina's new areas could hurt its traditional bulk sequencing business.

**Thaysen** disagreed: [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- There is "a huge opportunity still in front of us." The industry has focused on driving down one parameter instead of the customer's end-to-end problem.
- Academic customers, including at Broad, say they run five to ten spatial samples because hundreds are cost prohibitive. Science has not been established in these areas because many small companies each offer bits of the workflow.
- Illumina provides the whole workflow and can enable much bigger programs, which will draw more money. "Pharma is going to be all over this," as shown by the number of pharma companies at the meeting.
- The pharma opportunity extends beyond sequencing to insights, a "completely different business model" and a big market.
- "I don't agree with the notion that we're just shifting around money." If a rival wants only to sell a sequencer, "you're going to be unsuccessful, because it's going to be an end-to-end game."

**Barnard** gave historical precedent: [AGBT, 2025-02-25]
- Around 2000 Illumina offered an end-to-end workflow and array technology for the HapMap project (the transcript spells it "HAM"), a global project, and took roughly 70% of it. NGS later came in and the major large sequencing initiatives used Illumina technology.
- The UK's Genomics England 100,000 Genomes project (the transcript says "the GEL project") is another example of end-to-end total solutions at large science projects. The same philosophy is being applied to proteomics, spatial and single cell, and the Broad announcement to fund the 5 billion cell effort is another example. "We have a history of executing on this and Illumina being the de facto standard."

## Points of disagreement and caveats

- Management repeatedly dismissed Roche's technology as unproven or unlaunched while also arguing it is inferior (short-read, lower starting Q score). The Roche claims are management's characterizations. Thaysen acknowledged only a technology overview had been seen, so these remarks are partly speculation by his own admission.
- The comparisons to PacBio and Oxford Nanopore were made by Illumina's CTO and were not challenged by the analysts in this session.
- Thaysen did not directly answer the portion of Peterson's question about Ultima's wins.
- Dhingra's "excess" in the instrument comment appears to be a transcription error, and its interpretation as instruments placed is an inference from context.
- Some names in the transcript appear garbled by the transcription (for example "DARCO," "HAM," "GEL," "sea level," "$5 billion cell"). Interpretations given above are inferred from context.
