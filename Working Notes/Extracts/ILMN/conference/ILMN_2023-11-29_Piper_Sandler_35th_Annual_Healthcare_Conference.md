# Illumina at the Piper Sandler 35th Annual Healthcare Conference

**Event:** Fireside chat, Nov 29, 2023, 11:30 AM ET. Host: David Westenberg, Piper Sandler. Speaker: Jacob Thaysen, Illumina's Chief Executive Officer (CEO). Westenberg introduced him as Illumina's "brand-new CEO." The document is a Seeking Alpha transcript. It contains no prepared remarks, no financial guidance, no estimate changes, no ratings and no price targets. Everything below is management opinion or claim unless stated otherwise. Tone: upbeat, confident and conciliatory, with emphasis on customers, focus and discipline. He did not criticize predecessors.

## Opening: name and why Thaysen joined

Westenberg asked whether to say "Jacob" or "Jakob." Thaysen said his mother says "Jakob" in Danish, he says "Jacob" in English, either is fine, and he prefers "Jacob" [Piper, 2023-11-29].

Asked what drew him to Illumina and what the Board wanted, he said:
- He has followed Illumina for a long time, and says it has had a "huge impact" on the industry and, more importantly, on human health.
- He has a "very strong passion for oncology and genomics" and calls Illumina the pioneer of the space, expecting it to pioneer the future too.
- The main draw was the talent. At the San Diego headquarters, people approach him in the cafeteria to talk and show R&D work. He cited this energy as fuel for his own passion and ties it to his R&D background.
- On what the Board wanted, he said that is a question for the Board, but he thinks they sought someone from within the industry with global experience, a track record of understanding and commercializing innovation, and experience running a business of similar size. He said his profile "pretty much" meets all of these.

## Most underrated parts of the Illumina portfolio

Thaysen listed several things he sees as underrated [Piper, 2023-11-29]:
- **Product road map and pipeline.** Plans for the coming years include sequencing itself and automation to help customers succeed in their labs.
- **Informatics (the software and data analysis layer).** He called it "highly undervalued." He said most of the time in sequencing is spent analyzing data. Informatics investments, especially on the NovaSeq X (Illumina's newest high-throughput sequencer), make processing much faster. Data can be compressed by a factor of five, so storage is much cheaper. Automated pipelines are built in, which many customers find simpler and faster than anything else on the market.
- **Global presence and service.** He said there is "not a lot of fun" buying a new tool if you cannot get it running or reach someone within 24 hours when problems arise, and that Illumina is "the only company that can really do that."

## Culture and the "brain drain" question

Westenberg noted talk of a long-running Illumina "brain drain" and asked how to make Illumina a cool place to work again. Thaysen's answer:
- He thinks it is already a "pretty cool place," which is why he joined. Few companies let people take part in a big change in human health from a tools perspective.
- He cited investments in DNA sequencing and beyond: multiomics (measuring DNA, RNA and other molecular layers together), single-cell, and potential in proteomics (proteins) and spatial (where molecules sit in tissue).
- He said he feels great passion and that colleagues share it.
- On branding he said "I don't know about that," but he sees many smart, passionate people and does not "believe that brain drain comment."

## Startup culture versus a mature P&L

Westenberg noted roughly $4 billion of revenue and a "mature P&L" (profit and loss statement). Thaysen said:
- He does not consider the market mature. There is "still a lot of opportunity to grow" in sequencing and the company is "just scratching the surface," which supports staying fast-paced and innovative.
- The unique opportunity is to also run the rest of the P&L with more "rigor" and "discipline," organizing to allow innovation alongside operational excellence. He said few companies in the industry have this combination of fast growth and scale.
- Quote: "I don't want to consider us mature. I will do a lot to keep us agile, but at the same time, drive everything else to go back to be a very high profitable company."

## Value beyond cost per gigabase; competition

Westenberg noted that Illumina set the "lowest cost per G" (cost per gigabase, a billion DNA letters read) conversation, and that Element, Ultima and BGI are matching on that metric. He asked how Illumina shifts the discussion to value per project.

Thaysen's answer [Piper, 2023-11-29]:
- Illumina's focus is customers: "We want to make sure that the customers are the heroes here." He acknowledged competition, saying Illumina has always lived with it.
- Illumina invented cost per gigabase, but it is a proxy for the cost of the whole workflow. With sequencing being a significant part of that cost, the conversation is simplified to it.
- The industry is now entering a regime where the goal is to drive down the cost of running an entire sequencing lab. That makes the front end (sample preparation), informatics and the right applications important too.
- Commercially, workflows are still highly sophisticated. Buying a sequencer and then facing a complicated workflow, or a week or two of downtime waiting for a service technician, is a bad experience. Illumina offers fast service and support, and he said customers are excited to keep working with Illumina because it keeps them "up and running."
- He did not name competitors specifically or give pricing figures.

## Mistakes and priorities

Asked about mistakes of the last couple of years, which are quickly fixable and which will take longer, Thaysen declined to be a "Monday morning" quarterback. He said everyone makes decisions they would do differently and he wants to look forward.
- His focus is honing in on the right strategic priorities: what drives the top line, customer success and the full P&L. He is working with his team on this and says the company already does "a lot of good stuff" but can gain "clarity and focus."
- He wants "rigor and operational excellence" that will improve the P&L and also "unleash" opportunity. Simplifying decision-making processes allows quicker decisions, more innovation and better customer service.
- Larger companies accumulate complex processes that do not always serve customers. Simplifying helps efficiency and customer service. He says he is already working on this.

## Is he an operations person rather than an innovator?

Westenberg relayed investor concern that Thaysen's background (Dako, Agilent) suggests an operations leader rather than an innovator. Thaysen responded [Piper, 2023-11-29]:
- He has a PhD in physics and founded a high-tech company in MEMS (microelectromechanical systems, tiny semiconductor-style devices), where he was the innovator with many patents.
- At Dako he ran R&D, then shifted roles. He said he is not "necessarily the sharpest person in the room" to make the best innovations and no longer aspires to that.
- His strength is nurturing a culture of innovation: helping decisions get made, building the right organizational structures, and creating an environment of collaboration, since innovation "doesn't come out of one individual."
- His resume shows he has driven top-line growth through innovation, not only the bottom line. He stressed that innovation includes business and process innovation, not just product innovation, and he sees much opportunity there.

## M&A and the EU case

Westenberg said winning the EU case (the European Commission challenge over Illumina's acquisition of Grail, a cancer-screening company; Thaysen was not asked about details) matters for keeping future M&A on the table, and asked what Illumina would need to buy.

Thaysen said:
- Illumina "right now" has everything it needs to succeed and invests substantially in R&D, to pioneer in DNA sequencing and in using the sequencing platform as the readout and ecosystem for multiomics, which he is "pretty excited" about.
- It will keep looking at bolt-on (small add-on) acquisitions. He sees opportunities in informatics.
- "For sure, we're not looking at any larger acquisition at this point. We have a lot on our plates and we will focus on executing on that."

## Technology: limits of SBS chemistry

Westenberg asked whether Illumina is near the limits of SBS (sequencing by synthesis, Illumina's core chemistry that reads DNA by building complementary strands).

Thaysen's answer [Piper, 2023-11-29]:
- Illumina just launched XLEAP chemistry, a "reinvented" SBS chemistry. The team also examined other chemistries and ways to accelerate sequencing, but he said the reinvented SBS remains "the best chemistry out there" and continues to move performance forward.
- He cited recent papers showing more than 80% of reads (the transcript renders "reads" as "REITs") are at Q40 level. Q40 is a quality score meaning about one error in 10,000 bases. He called it a "quantum leap" in quality. These are papers, not Illumina-reported figures.
- He sees a "tremendous opportunity for short reads" (short DNA fragments), and says this chemistry is well suited. Most solid tumors and liquid biopsies (blood-based cancer tests) use short reads. This will be the prime focus, and Illumina can keep lowering sequencing cost with this chemistry.
- Customers using the NovaSeq X are "raving" about it and XLEAP. It is familiar, so they do not have to start over. Many customers built whole ecosystems on Illumina systems and chemistry, and switching ecosystems is "simply too costly."

## Informatics and data analysis

Westenberg asked about scale in analysis, file compression and ease of use. Thaysen said (with some garbled transcription, e.g. "25B" and "Dragen"):
- Data analysis needed in sequencing is "massive," and will grow in single-cell and other areas.
- Investments in DRAGEN (Illumina's hardware-accelerated analysis software) and informatics will be a "huge amplif[ier]." He said Illumina is "just scratching the surface" and that data analysis, and being able to do it on the platform, will matter for the next decade. Illumina will put a lot of effort here.
- Visualization of data is another area where Illumina can do more, especially with multiomics, where different data types are combined.

## China

Westenberg noted that China was called a very important strategic market in the last earnings call, is down significantly, and has an entrenched competitor (BGI/MGI, implied) that could be a substitute.

Thaysen said [Piper, 2023-11-29]:
- China is "around 10% of our revenue" and will remain an important region.
- He had a deep dive with the China team a few weeks earlier. The team is "still very bullish" on the future.
- Operating in China has unique aspects. Illumina must think about how it acts as, and is perceived as, a local player. He has experience doing this from his previous job and sees ways to "amplify our position."
- Chinese customers, especially in clinical, "really love working with Illumina" because clinical decisions affect patients, and they want certainty in therapeutic decisions based on genomic data. They get "the highest quality."
- He gave no figures on the size or timing of any recovery.

## Clinical demand and elasticity

Westenberg said over 50% of Illumina revenue is now clinical (his statement, "I believe") and asked how to answer investors who say clinical demand is not elastic (i.e., does not rise when prices fall).

Thaysen's answer [Piper, 2023-11-29]:
- "Just look at the facts": fewer than 50% of US cancer patients get DNA sequencing at diagnosis, even though reimbursement exists.
- In cancer monitoring, penetration is only "low percentage points," about 1 or 2 percentage points.
- The information given to clinicians could be much richer, with bigger panels, which would also drive "substantial demand." He said it is "just a matter of time."
- Because over 50% of customers are now clinical (his figure), and quality is paramount, they must validate new platforms to a high degree. Moving from one platform to another therefore takes time. Everyone sees the cost benefit of moving to the NovaSeq X, but clinical labs insist the X meets the highest validation standards.
- Illumina is working with customers to transition as fast as possible, but patients depend on the results. Quote: "even though we're all frustrated it takes time, it will come, and it will come strong back."

## Five-year vision

Westenberg asked him to imagine a look back from the Piper conference in 2028. Thaysen said (he mistakenly said "2025" at one point):
- He expects "five tremendous years" with Illumina pioneering what sequencing can achieve in clinical care.
- His hope: "at least 90%" of cancer patients get their full sequencing profile, versus under 50% of US patients today (his earlier statement).
- Illumina will be "the central part of the ecosystem" in the multiomics revolution.
- Illumina will be a cool company for ambitious people, helping catalyze clinical decision-making, genomics, and academic research insights that open "completely new therapeutics and other things that we couldn't even imagine today."

## Takeaways for tracking

- **New and notable:** This is one of Thaysen's first investor appearances as CEO. Key signals: priorities on clarity, focus and operational rigor; no large acquisitions, only bolt-ons (informatics a possible area); China stated at about 10% of revenue; clinical NovaSeq X transition slowed by validation, with a "strong" return expected but no timing.
- **Not covered:** No revenue, margin or guidance numbers, no quantification of China decline or X adoption, no specific details on the EU case or Grail, and no competitor-specific rebuttals.
- **Source caveats:** Transcript has transcription errors ("REITs" for reads, "25B," "Dako" context, "invalidity" likely "inclusivity/validity"), and several of his claims (Q40 papers, 24-hour service, data compression of 5x) are management statements not independently verified here.
