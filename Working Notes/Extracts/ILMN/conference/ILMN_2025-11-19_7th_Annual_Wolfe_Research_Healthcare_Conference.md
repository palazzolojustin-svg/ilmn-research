# Illumina CEO Jacob Thaysen at the 7th Annual Wolfe Research Healthcare Conference (fireside chat with Doug Schenkel)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/conference/ILMN_2025-11-19_7th_Annual_Wolfe_Research_Healthcare_Conference.md` · Date: 2025-11-19 · Seeking Alpha transcript of Wolfe Research 7th Annual Healthcare Conference; host Douglas Schenkel (Wolfe Research), speaker Jacob Thaysen (CEO, Illumina); Illumina's Ankur and Conor attended but did not speak · conference*

**Summary.** Illumina CEO Jacob Thaysen says 2025 was a year of hiccups (China, NIH funding, tariffs, new competition) but the company is now in execution mode, and the 2027 targets of high single-digit revenue growth and about 26% operating margin remain intact, with growth now defined excluding China. He explained how clinical customers moving from the NovaSeq 6000 to the NovaSeq X see roughly flat growth in the first year and then volume-driven growth, and said less than 50% of clinical revenue has moved to the X. He also covered Roche's entry (no change in customer behavior seen), the partial easing of China sanctions, and enthusiasm for new products (5-base, Constellation, multiomics).

## Overview and format

This is a fireside chat (a one-on-one interview rather than a prepared presentation) held on November 19, 2025 at Wolfe Research's 7th Annual Healthcare Conference. The host was Doug Schenkel, who leads Wolfe Research's Life Science Tools and Diagnostics group. The only Illumina speaker was Jacob Thaysen, CEO and a director. Two other Illumina executives, named only as Ankur and Conor, were in the room but did not speak.

Schenkel set out four themes: the state of the company under Thaysen; unpacking new clinical disclosures from the Q3 2025 earnings call; a mix of recent developments (policy, China, competition); and, if time allowed, 2026 and beyond.

Tone: Thaysen was confident and upbeat. He was cautious on China ("there's more to it than what we can control") and on academic funding, where he assumed no improvement.

## State of the company under Thaysen

**Tenure and background.** Thaysen became CEO in September 2023, "a little more than 2 years" before this event [Wolfe, 2025-11-19]. Schenkel noted Thaysen's earlier roles at Dako and Agilent, where Schenkel said Thaysen consistently exceeded plan. This is Thaysen's first CEO job. Schenkel joked that Thaysen "picked a doozy" because of repeated policy shocks, which he likened to whack-a-mole.

**Thaysen's view of the past two years** [Wolfe, 2025-11-19]:
- He still enjoys the job. He knew it "would not be an easy ride", but the thesis he joined on has not changed: what Illumina stands for and the opportunity ahead are still significant.
- First change: making sure GRAIL (the cancer-test company formerly owned by Illumina) could be separated. He said GRAIL is now "doing a great job" as an independent company.
- Second change: clarifying strategy. Illumina now focuses on an end-to-end workflow (from sample preparation through sequencing to analysis) delivering the highest-quality insights at the lowest end-to-end cost.
- Third: executing on that strategy. 2025 was meant to be an execution year rather than a strategy year, but it was hit by "a few hiccups" early on: China, NIH (US National Institutes of Health) funding, tariffs, and new competition.
- He now feels, "knock on wood," that the company has been through the challenging year and is "fully in execution."
- The opportunity is "still massive," because sequencing is still not the standard of care in health care.

## 2027 financial targets

Schenkel summarized the targets as high single-digit revenue growth by 2027, more than 500 basis points (5 percentage points) of operating margin expansion, and double-digit earnings growth. Thaysen confirmed them ("Correct"). Thaysen separately stated the ambition as getting back to high single-digit growth in 2027 and improving margins by about 500 basis points, "up to 26% operating margin in '27."

**What has changed:** Thaysen said the high single-digit growth target is now stated excluding China. In his words, "China is still something we need to work through. So the high single-digit growth is ex China at this point."

Schenkel observed that China is on track to fall to roughly 2% to 3% of sales by the end of 2025, so in any scenario growth should accelerate a year from now. Thaysen agreed ("That we would expect for sure"), said China is "starting to be very small," and that Illumina would love to grow China again but still needs to work through the situation.

## Drivers, accelerators and risks to the targets

Thaysen's answers [Wolfe, 2025-11-19]:
- **Main driver: the NovaSeq X transition.** The NovaSeq X is Illumina's newer high-throughput sequencer, replacing the NovaSeq 6000. The faster the transition is completed, the faster the pricing headwind (price per unit of sequencing falling as customers move to the X) is behind the company.
- **Price elasticity still works.** When he joined there were questions about whether lower prices would produce more volume. He said it does: "We continue to grow volume very, very rapidly, and I expect that to continue to do so."
- **Multiomics contribution.** New multiomics launches (measuring more than DNA, e.g. proteins, methylation, single-cell and spatial data) are starting to contribute. For 2027's high single-digit growth, he put their contribution at about 1 to 2 percentage points, and expects it to be "even better" beyond that.
- **Upside lever: academic research.** If academic research funding turns positive, growth would accelerate. He is not expecting that; he expects the current environment to continue, though some uptick could help.

## Schenkel's growth framework and Thaysen's response

Schenkel's own framework (he stressed the wording is his, not Thaysen's):
1. In a "normal academic environment," academic research is a low single-digit growth market.
2. Most growth comes from moving past the pricing dynamics and X transition, and from being an "arms dealer" (supplier to all participants) to high-volume clinical growth.
3. On top of that, Illumina layers products that win more "share of wallet" from customers, such as multiomics and sample prep.

Thaysen called it "a good framework" and added:
- **Workflow breadth and partnering.** Customers are looking for a vendor that supplies most workflow steps. Illumina will keep working with the whole ecosystem, competing in some areas and collaborating in others. Even in product lines where Illumina sells its own, "if a customer wants to have a competing Sample Prep, I have no issue in collaborating with a partner." But he sees a "massive advantage" for customers in getting a single combined workflow.
- **Pharma as a distinct growth segment.** Historically Illumina thought of clinical and academic/research only; pharma "has never been separated out as a segment" but is "starting to become a real contributor."
- **How pharma buys.** Not just a sequencer purchase, but sequencing of very large sample sets: "hundreds of thousands, not millions of genomes," or, for Perturb-Seq (an experiment type that perturbs genes and reads out the effect on single cells), "billions of cells," which is "extremely expensive right now."
- **BioInsight.** Illumina recently created a business called BioInsight to offer such services to multiple pharma partners with a shared goal, which can accelerate the work. He expects this to become meaningful over time.

## Clinical transition: Q3 2025 disclosures

Schenkel recapped that on the Q3 2025 call Illumina presented new slides splitting research versus clinical (excluding China) and a case study of the first 40 clinical customers who began converting to the NovaSeq X and have now fully converted. Schenkel clarified, and Thaysen confirmed, that these 40 were customers previously on the NovaSeq 6000, not customers new to high-throughput sequencing.

**Shape of growth after conversion.** Schenkel said that by his ruler-measurement of the slide's chart, the first full year after transition shows growth of probably under 5%, then growth accelerates as volume builds. Thaysen said that is an important takeaway [Wolfe, 2025-11-19]:
- Moving from the 6000 to the X gives customers a very strong price reduction.
- The concern had been that customers would simply pocket that saving. Instead, customers compete with each other, so they use the opportunity to offer better, substantially larger assays (tests) that still fit within reimbursement (what insurers pay per test).
- In the first year, volume gain and price decline are "almost in an equilibrium," giving small growth. After the pricing is lapped (the price cut is in the year-ago comparison), volume is the pure driver and growth returns.

**How much has converted.**
- About 50% of revenue from Illumina's high-throughput customers is now on the X.
- Research customers "have more or less completely moved over."
- Clinical customers are therefore at "less than 50%" of revenue on the X, so there is still opportunity there.
- Thaysen agreed with Schenkel that most remaining conversion is clinical, and that most research customers who would move to the X already have.

## Four clinical customer cohorts

Schenkel and Thaysen worked through groups of clinical customers who have not converted, or have converted differently:

1. **Never-convert, low volume.** These customers lack the volume to justify the investment in the X. They have good reimbursement, are likely profitable on their assay, and stay on the 6000. Thaysen described it as a smaller cohort by volume.
2. **Keep old assays on the 6000, put new assays on the X.** They have volume on the 6000, which may grow slightly, while new, larger assays (more data per sample, wider or deeper sequencing) run on the X. Thaysen said these customers grow "out the gate."
3. **Shift from exome to genome.** Customers running exome (sequencing only the protein-coding portion of the genome) on the 6000 who believe the market is bigger with whole genome are moving to genomes on the X, a much bigger assay. Some customers stay on exome, and that business is declining. Revenue from this group improves from the start. Thaysen said cohorts 2 and 3 are "absolutely the largest of our clinical segments."
4. **Price-motivated movers.** Assays that are good enough as they are, for example NIPT (non-invasive prenatal testing), move to the X only for better pricing. Thaysen said this is a segment of "less than 20%," that most have already moved because they saw the price opportunity immediately, and that it is "not going to be a big cohort going forward."

**Flow cells.** Schenkel asked whether not all customers go to the 25B flow cell (the higher-capacity consumable with the biggest price change) right away, given turnaround-time needs. Thaysen said the two middle cohorts (2 and 3), where most volume sits, go to the 25B. The other two cohorts likely stay on the 10B flow cell, which has a different price point and is more like high-volume 6000 pricing. He summarized that clinical sees roughly flattish growth in year one and then volume comes back, consistent with the Q3 slide.

**New-to-sequencing customers.** The case study does not capture brand-new customers buying sequencing for the first time, including newly formed companies. Thaysen confirmed this.

## Competition: Roche

- **Customer behavior.** Asked whether customers have changed behavior since Roche's announced entry into sequencing, Thaysen said "No, we have not" seen any change. There is interest in any new technology.
- **Attitude.** He called Roche's entry good for the industry: "I absolutely enjoy competition and especially when we're winning." He said it forces better customer conversations about their goals. He noted Roche is the newest competitor, that Illumina has "always had competition," and that it has intensified lately, but "we feel really good where we are."
- **Roche–Freenome.** Schenkel noted an announcement (he thought the day before) that Roche made another investment in Freenome (a cancer-screening diagnostics company), which is evaluating developing its assays on Roche's platform (name transcribed as "Axceelios"). Schenkel cautioned there is a long way from announcement to reality.
- **Dako/Ventana analogy.** Schenkel asked how customers reacted when Roche bought Ventana, becoming vertically integrated (owning both instruments and assays), during Thaysen's Dako days. Thaysen said in the companion diagnostics business (tests co-developed with a drug to select patients), many pharma companies preferred Dako, and still prefer Agilent, because it is not vertically integrated. Pharma was wary about how information flows between a Roche diagnostics business and a pharma business.
- **Read-across.** He expects some of the same: clinical customers will think about how they want to work with a potential competitor. He sees "a huge opportunity" over the next 3 to 5 years for decentralization (testing moving to many local labs) and for offering workflows to enable it.
- **Installed base advantage.** From Dako, he learned that installed base matters: pharma wants its assays on the platform with the most instruments so they reach volume quickly. He called this "a huge advantage for our ecosystem going forward" and said Illumina will "play to our advantage."

## China

**Timeline Thaysen gave** [Wolfe, 2025-11-19]:
- **February 2025:** After US tariff announcements, Illumina was put on China's unreliable entity list, initially with no sanctions.
- **About a month later:** After further tariffs (before the April tariffs), sanctions followed: Illumina could not import or export instruments into China.
- **From about March to the time of the event:** Illumina could serve all existing China customers but could not put in new installations.
- **Before/around the Q3 call:** Illumina became able to serve OEM customers (partners who sell Illumina-made instruments under their own brand), including manufacturing instruments in China and white labeling for OEM partners. This is still less than 20% of total China operations, but "a good step forward."
- **After the Q3 call:** The sanctions were lifted, so Illumina can place instruments again in China. Customers still need a permit; Illumina is working through what that means and expects more clarity "likely over the next quarter or so."

**Outlook.**
- Even once placements are allowed, customers must get through budget cycles, which typically takes about 6 months, so it will take time "into '26" before placements really resume.
- It still "creates more certainty of the business in China."
- Thaysen was in China the week before the event and met MOFCOM (China's Ministry of Commerce) and the China team. Illumina wants to get off the unreliable entity list and is working toward it, but much of that is outside its control.
- Schenkel's modeling view: given how far China revenue has fallen since Q1 2025, it would be mathematically hard for China to grow in 2026, but off a small base it could return to growth at some point, certainly by 2027. Thaysen said that is "at least our hope," that the China general manager (referred to as "Jenny") will do her utmost, but details remain unresolved. He said China is "a huge market" worth continuing to invest in.

## Operational efficiency and margins

Schenkel noted that P&L (profit and loss) improvement and free cash flow have been impressive despite the headwinds, and asked whether Illumina has exhausted the cost opportunity. Thaysen said no [Wolfe, 2025-11-19]:
- On joining, he saw a huge opportunity to improve how the company works, from decision-making to project management to operations, while keeping focus on growing the top line.
- Philosophy: "Instead of just adding more people to bad processes, you need to go in and fix the processes." Fixing processes also makes work more enjoyable, though there is a transition to go through.
- "There's plenty of opportunities in front of us. We haven't even started really on AI yet."

## Innovation pipeline

Schenkel, who visited Illumina's Cambridge (UK) facility, said he saw no sign of slowing innovation and named Constellation, Fluent, SomaLogic, and 5-base. Thaysen said he is "really excited," that innovation was one reason he joined, and that Illumina will keep investing "very highly" in it, with many launches coming over the next years.

Products and businesses he cited:
- **5-base.** Brings methylation (chemical tags on DNA that affect gene activity) into standard sequencing. He acknowledged others say they can also do methylation, and that "everybody have been able to do methylation for years," but said Illumina has made it extremely simple in sample prep, in getting data out, and in combining it with standard DNA variant information in the informatics pipeline. He expects methylation to be important for screening and clinical insight.
- **Constellation.** Allows structural variant insights (large rearrangements of DNA) using short reads that were not previously possible with short reads. He called it a huge opportunity.
- **Other areas:** spatial and single-cell products, and the SomaLogic acquisition (which brought a proteomics assay, i.e., protein measurement), and Fluent (as named by Schenkel; Thaysen's remark on "the Fluent -- excuse me, with the SomaLogic acquisitions" is unclear).
- Thaysen said 5-base and Constellation are the two he is most excited about because they speak to where growth will come from: the clinical sector over the coming period.

## Items not covered

No new numeric guidance for 2026, no Q4 2025 figures, no analyst ratings or price targets, and no consensus estimates were given in this session. The planned discussion of 2026 and beyond was only touched on through China and the 2027 targets.
