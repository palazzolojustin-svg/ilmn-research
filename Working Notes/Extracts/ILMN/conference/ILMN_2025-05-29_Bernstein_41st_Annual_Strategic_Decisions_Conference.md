# Illumina CEO Jacob Thaysen at the Bernstein 41st Annual Strategic Decisions Conference (fireside chat with Eve Burstein)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ILMN/conference/ILMN_2025-05-29_Bernstein_41st_Annual_Strategic_Decisions_Conference.md` · Date: 2025-05-28 · Seeking Alpha transcript of Bernstein 41st Annual Strategic Decisions Conference; speaker Jacob Thaysen (CEO, Illumina); host analyst Eve Burstein (Bernstein, U.S. life science tools and diagnostics) · conference*

**Summary.** Illumina CEO Jacob Thaysen reviews his roughly 20 months in the job and says the long-range plan (high-single-digit revenue growth and 500 basis points of operating margin expansion by 2027, excluding Greater China) is intact despite China, NIH funding, tariff and competitive headwinds. He explains the growth math (NovaSeq X transition, volume versus price), sizes Illumina's exposure to U.S. academic funding at roughly 10-12% of revenue, argues clinical customers will not easily leave Illumina, and promotes new products (Constellation, 5-base genome, spatial, multiomics, AI). He also explains his recent open-market share purchase and why he thinks the stock is undervalued.

# Illumina at the Bernstein 41st Annual Strategic Decisions Conference

Event: fireside chat on May 28, 2025 (2:30 PM ET). Speaker: Jacob Thaysen, CEO of Illumina. Host: Eve Burstein, Bernstein, who covers U.S. life science tools and diagnostics. All statements below are by Thaysen unless marked as the host's. Tags are [Bernstein, 2025-05-28].

## Introduction and speaker background

Burstein introduced Thaysen as CEO since 2023. Before that he was President of both the Life Science and Applied Markets Group and the Diagnostics and Genomics Group at Agilent, and earlier Corporate Vice President of R&D at Dako. He spent more than 16 years across the two companies. Thaysen confirmed this. The host noted that a competitor's executive "reports tonight," and Thaysen said he was glad not to be there. No company is named in the transcript.

## What Thaysen is proudest of after about 20 months as CEO

Thaysen said he looked it up and it has been 20 months, which "sometimes... feels like only maybe 20 days." [Bernstein, 2025-05-28] He named four things.

- **Team.** He is proud to be part of a "super talented" and "absolutely the most innovative team" he has worked with. As a scientist, he finds this exciting.
- **Operational excellence.** The team has been willing to challenge how it operates and is organized, and to change itself. He said operational excellence "always sounds like cutting costs," but in his view it is about running a resilient and effective company. His example: when growth was fast, the habit was to add people to fix a problem instead of fixing the process. The mindset has shifted to challenging and optimizing what the company does. His view is that this is the only way to succeed on both the top line (revenue) and the bottom line (profit).
- **Strategy set last summer.** The strategy tells investors what the Illumina model is going forward: the "highest insight with the lowest end-to-end cost." For many years Illumina focused on driving down the cost of sequencing, and each cost decline opened many more applications (the "elasticity" logic, meaning lower prices create more demand). He still believes in elasticity. The proxy was always end-to-end cost, but for a long time sequencing cost was the dominant part of the workflow cost. Now, for many applications, sequencing cost is no longer the main driver of workflow cost, so Illumina must work on the whole workflow. This is changing how the company thinks and talks about itself, internally and externally.
- **Customer intimacy.** He said a successful company with a strong market position can become too "inside-out," focusing on what it is good at and expecting customers to follow. He would not call that complacency, but customer closeness gets lost because "we are the one you come to anyway." Illumina now has deeper day-to-day conversations with customers and invites them in to see what it is working on, rather than surprising them with launches. He says this has produced a different closeness and longer-lasting relationships.

He added that others would remind him of things he forgot, such as the GRAIL separation (the spin-off of the cancer-test company GRAIL from Illumina).

## What went differently in 2025, and how Illumina responded

Burstein asked what he would do differently, given that 2025 has not gone as anyone expected. Thaysen said it is easy to be a "Monday morning quarterback." He knew when he took the job that it was a transformation: the GRAIL decision, returning the company to growth, improving margins, and changing the culture.

- **2024 as setup.** Coming out of 2024, with the strategy in place, Illumina had shown execution and operational excellence. It kept expanding margin even in quarters when revenue did not grow. Management had been clear that the top line still had to grow and that this was part of the transition to the NovaSeq X (Illumina's newest high-throughput sequencer, called "the X").
- **2025 as an execution year.** The plan was for 2025 to be an execution year, but "the deck was stacked a little bit differently." He said, "you have to play the cards that you get."
- **Headwinds in 2025.**
  - Early in the year, Chinese authorities notified Illumina that it had been put on China's unreliable entity list.
  - Competition came out.
  - There were NIH (National Institutes of Health) funding challenges.
  - There was the tariff situation that everyone faces.
- **Response.** He said you can complain, or "control what we can control." Management made tough, unanticipated decisions in recent months. These included cutting approximately $100 million of cost this year "and beyond next year," which he said puts Illumina in a good place from a profit-and-loss (P&L) perspective. The company also shifted some priorities based on what it learned from customers and competition, but the strategy is "completely" unchanged: end-to-end, highest-quality insight at the lowest end-to-end cost. He conceded things may change again "tomorrow."

### Strategy versus priorities

Burstein asked what he meant by shifting priorities. Thaysen's framework is that strategy rests on three things: the opportunities in the market, the company's strengths, and the culture of the team. The strategy is to take genomics to the next level and into multiomics (measuring several kinds of biology, such as DNA, RNA and protein, together), by focusing on the end-to-end workflow and deep insight rather than only on sequencing and sequencing consumables (the reagent kits needed for each run).

- If a market headwind forces a change of strategy, "you have the wrong strategy out the gate."
- Priorities, however, should change with new information, whether it is a market headwind, a competitor, or new team members with different strengths.
- Not adjusting priorities means "you're not really listening to your own organization and to the market." This applies at any company size.

## Is the long-range plan (LRP) intact?

Burstein noted that on the Q1 2025 call Illumina said it was still executing toward high-single-digit revenue growth and 500 basis points (5 percentage points) of operating margin expansion by 2027, excluding the Greater China region. She asked whether that holds with all the headwinds. Thaysen: "Exactly, yes." [Bernstein, 2025-05-28]

He explained that the strategy, presented last summer, rests on the belief that there is "a lot of room to grow" and that Illumina will be "a very strong growth company going forward." Management also recognized its growth over the past few years had not been where it wanted, so it chose to commit first to stepping back into growth before committing to more. He described three growth drivers.

1. **Transition to the NovaSeq X.**
   - The X cut the cost and price of sequencing substantially, reducing the price point by two-thirds. At that price cut, volume has to rise 100% (he said "100% more volume") just to make up the revenue.
   - Customers are still transitioning. Volume remains strong, but the transition brings price erosion.
   - Approximately 65% of Illumina's volume now sits on the X, yet the X is still under 50% of revenue. Management expects the X to reach 50% of revenue in the second half of 2025.
   - At that point "growth is going to be easy because the denominator is very different." The transition work is finishing, so he expects 2025 to show stronger growth and 2027 stronger still.
2. **New product introductions (NPIs).** Last summer the company spoke mainly about single cell and proteomics. It has since added spatial, Constellation and the 5-base genome. He said these should add "probably another 1 point" of growth on top of the X transition. The X transition remains the main driver over the next few years, with NPIs adding growth beyond that.
3. **Margin expansion.** Margin gains come along with growth. Because China would hurt margin, management accelerated cost reductions in 2025 (the roughly $100 million noted above). Thaysen said Illumina remains on track for its commitments.

## Volume versus price: does volume growth fade as the discount ends?

Burstein relayed an investor pushback: volume growth has been great only because of the price discount, and it will slow as customers finish transitioning.

Thaysen replied that, as "a numbers guy," he can see in Illumina's own data that customers who have fully transitioned keep growing volume. He added that data does not tell the whole story, and customer conversations, especially in clinical, show continuing opportunity. Many customers raised their own guidance for this year. Multiomics also requires substantially more sequencing.

The model's long-term assumptions, as he described them, are in the table below. The first sentence after the table gives the takeaway.

| Item | Past few years | Long-term model (steady state) |
|---|---|---|
| Sequencing volume growth | 30% to 40% | Mid-20% range |
| Price decline | n/a | Currently modeled in the mid-teens (percent) |

Past volume growth was "elevated over where we expect it to be long term." Combining mid-20% volume growth with mid-teens price declines yields high-single-digit revenue growth.

## End markets: academic and government funding

Burstein noted that research is roughly 50% of revenue (not all academic; some is pharma), and that the long-term plan assumed this market grows about 6% a year. In 2025 Illumina cut its U.S. academic and government end-market guidance by 15%. She asked whether growth ever returns to 6%, and whether that is feasible within four years.

**Exposure sizing (Thaysen).**
- More than 50% of revenue is clinical, and less than 50% (about 45%) is academic and research.
- About half of revenue is from the Americas, which takes the research exposure to under 25%.
- Roughly half of that is exposure to academia, so the exposed portion is about "10%, 12%, maybe... plus/minus" of total revenue. This is where the real challenges are now.

**Funding environment.**
- The new administration is setting a new agenda. A part of the grants in one specific segment is being challenged, but other grants "flow very nicely."
- In genomics, some of the administration's focus areas, such as autism, require a lot of sequencing.
- He said the overhang is less the grants themselves than uncertainty. He hopes uncertainty dissipates "maybe over summer."
- Illumina is not planning for that in its new guidance. It expects the market to be "subdued actually all the way through '25."

**Longer view.** New tools (proteomics, single cell, spatial, Constellation, and the 5-base genome, which opens the epigenetic market) should create growth opportunities even within constrained grant money, because priorities will shift. He cannot predict whether it will be 2026 or 2027, but expects significant investment, globally and not only in the U.S., in multiomics.

**Pharma.** There is uncertainty, especially in the U.S. But products like Perturb-seq (a technique that tests the effect of gene perturbations on cells at scale) have strong pharma focus because they can change drug discovery and cut time to market. Big cohort programs should get a fair share of funding even if pharma overall is challenged.

## China

Burstein, apologizing for asking, said China is only about 5% of revenue. She noted she was surprised that on the Q1 call Illumina said it was still working with the Chinese government on a resolution short of an outright ban on its instruments. She asked whether revenue really might not go to zero, whether the odds have improved as trade tensions eased, and whether the high-single-digit growth target holds without China.

Thaysen's answers:

- China is "less than 5%" of revenue and investors should not spend too much time on it. The cost reductions were made so that China is now "an upside from a P&L perspective." The key focus is delivering high-single-digit growth and 500 bps of margin improvement "besides China." If China returns, it is upside.
- The original long-range plan did not anticipate China contributing growth, so "without China, it's going to be a little bit easier."
- Illumina would like to stay in China. The population and healthcare trends point to more genomic testing, and keeping optionality matters over five or ten years.
- Chinese employees and salespeople told him many Chinese customers, in clinical and research, want Illumina to stay. They see Illumina as the highest quality and are committed to its innovations.
- However, "with the current situation where we're not able to place instruments," if that does not change "we don't have a future in China." It is his job to try to change those odds by talking to the right regulators in both countries and approaching from different angles.
- A potential U.S.-China trade agreement could possibly address it, but "that is a little bit out of our hands." Illumina is trying to ensure there is no misunderstanding about why it is in this situation and to prove its commitment to China.

He did not say the odds had improved.

## Clinical market and competitive risk

Burstein noted clinical is about 55% of revenue and was particularly strong in Q1 2025, with good NovaSeq X placements and mid-single-digit consumables growth. She said investors worry that tests could one day move off Illumina machines, with little warning. She noted that at the Investor Day (the strategy presentation last summer) Illumina estimated low-double-digit clinical market growth underpinning its high-single-digit company growth, and asked what share of future clinical tests Illumina needs to keep to hit those numbers.

Thaysen did not give a share number. He said "investors worry too much," and gave this reasoning:

- Clinical customers need a supplier they can rely on for years. They have built assays, infrastructure, sample prep and an informatics backbone around Illumina's platform.
- Switching because of "a new shining object" promising slightly better pricing and different features would require a new laboratory infrastructure, possibly a new pipeline and sample prep, and added staff to run it separately.
- Illumina is spending heavily on innovation. "There's nobody in the industry that will match us on the innovations coming out" over the next three years. Customers who buy in will be part of the innovation roadmap and be kept differentiated.
- "Nobody that made a decision going with Illumina has ever regretted it, even though they could maybe get a little bit different pricing out the gate."
- Illumina commits to hit the price point needed to be successful.
- Some customers may move, and "we will welcome them back when they realize they made a mistake." Illumina will keep all others competitive versus those customers.
- On competition generally: "competition is great. It keeps us all up." A big part of his job is worrying about everything that could happen to the company.

## Innovation roadmap shared at AGBT

Burstein said that at AGBT (the Advances in Genome Biology and Technology conference) in 2025 Illumina shared a roadmap extending much further out than it historically has. She asked whether this was to keep customers from jumping to competitors, how early-access feedback has been, and whether there are examples of it working.

Thaysen reframed it. His philosophy is to share as much as possible with customers so they can succeed. Academic customers need early knowledge to write grants for new technologies, and clinical customers need it to prepare for next assays. Not sharing "is not customer-centric," and customer centricity is a key pillar of the strategy.

He said it has played out. Illumina hosts customers for full-day sessions on its campus with R&D and marketing teams, shares roadmap details not shared publicly, and hears what customers are working on. This gives early feedback on whether innovations fit, and it informs the next round. He said it drives "a completely different customer intimacy."

### Constellation and the 5-base genome

- **Constellation** is a new way of using short reads to also obtain "long read" insights (long reads are sequences of long continuous DNA stretches that help resolve complex genome regions). Thaysen said customers showed "tremendous interest," both for eventual clinical use and for research.
- **5-base genome.** This adds epigenetic (methylation) information on top of the standard four DNA bases, with a simple workflow. Previously it was "too complex" and "too costly," and the informatics required comparing data from two workflows. Illumina is streamlining this. He expects more clinical customers to add epigenetic insights to assays, and in research, epigenetics is poorly understood. Interest in access has been high.

**Follow-up: how much of the long-read landscape could Constellation take?** (Burstein noted it improves structural variant performance versus standard library prep.) Thaysen: "a lot," especially for human resequencing (sequencing a genome against a known reference). The technology is agnostic to species, so metagenomics and other uses are possible. The first focus is resequencing, "probably not de novo" (assembling a new genome from scratch) at first, though Illumina will not necessarily hold back there. Resequencing is the biggest market and is the first entry point.

**NICU example.** For critically ill newborns, good analysis normally needs a trio (child plus both parents), meaning three times the sequencing, and parents are not always available. With Constellation, only the patient needs sequencing because phasing information is available. He said competitors now claim the fastest sequencing, but they still need three times the sequencing and the parents, whereas Constellation has little sample prep and an easy workflow. In terms of true turnaround time, he expects Constellation to be faster and simpler.

**Follow-up: could on-flow-cell sample prep spread to many workflows?** (Burstein cited an Illumina blog post saying the first application is human whole genome sequencing, with others under evaluation.)
- Thaysen said it opens "a completely new universe." What excites him more than many workflows is adding the 5-base genome on top, which is on the roadmap.
- Sample prep is only a few minutes. Illumina had on-flow-cell prep working "a long time ago," then discovered it could also obtain proximity reads that give structural advances.
- Separately, he explained that on-flow-cell prep alone was not differentiating enough. For many applications, such as batch runs, labs use automation anyway. The NICU and similar single-sample, immediate use cases were the exception. The proximity read changed that view; he said both elements are needed. Automation is another way to simplify workflows that Illumina also considers.

## Spatial and connected multiomics

Burstein noted early feedback has been positive, and the commercial release is not until 2026. She asked what resonates and what Illumina is still working on.

**Multiomics.** Thaysen said multiomics itself is not new; one could pair an immunoassay for protein with NGS (next-generation sequencing). What is new is running everything on one platform, one software pipeline and similar sample prep, which makes it simple, uses the same sample, and gives consistency and standardization. Illumina's software, including a visualization tool from the Partek acquisition, can look at DNA, RNA, epigenetic and other data in one place, all the way to spatial, so customers do not need "10 different software."

**Spatial (measuring gene activity while keeping track of where in a tissue it occurs).**
- He said spatial has been "way too expensive," so papers often involve one to three samples over areas of one to two millimeters, looking at only part of the transcriptome (all the RNA a cell expresses).
- For Illumina to enter, it had to "revolutionize" the area. Real science requires a price point where hundreds of samples can be run, or at least 40, 50 or 60 samples, to get statistical relevance for clinical-trial-type work.
- Illumina also wanted the whole transcriptome at the same time, single-cell resolution, and large tissue areas. The product can image a whole slide, with one or multiple tissues on it.
- He said this combination of price point, resolution, whole-transcriptome coverage and large-tissue ability is "very unique" and will change how people think about spatial.

He did not directly answer what Illumina is still seeking feedback on before the 2026 release.

## AI (audience question)

An audience question asked about opportunities and threats AI presents to bioscience R&D, and how Illumina can capture value.

- Thaysen said the full informatics technology stack is needed. Few research environments want to spend scarce resources building pipelines that "hundreds and hundreds of people" at Illumina work on daily. Illumina's DRAGEN (its genomic data analysis software) and interpretation tools are "much stronger than really any individual science or company can do." He said large research institutes, academic medical centers and commercial customers are increasingly shifting to Illumina's technology stack.
- Big-data applications (Perturb-seq, spatial) need three things: an end-to-end workflow that hits the right price point, informatics with compute power, and analysis. Without AI capabilities it will be very hard for customers to get insight from so much data.
- Illumina's NVIDIA relationship gives access to NVIDIA tools to build foundation models. More than 40 people at Illumina work on foundation models and AI.
- He said the opportunity requires the right workflow, cost structure, informatics stack and AI together, and "I don't know any other company in this industry that have the capabilities or the power to do so than Illumina." He framed AI as an opportunity and did not address threats directly.

## Insider share purchase and valuation

Burstein noted investors were pleased by the company's share repurchase program (buyback) and by Thaysen's recent personal open-market purchase of stock. She said CFO Ankur (surname not given; he did not attend) also bought shares, and asked what prompted it and why now.

Thaysen's answer:
- Executives can only buy during an open trading window. Changes to the Board kept them from buying earlier because it "would be inappropriate." They bought as soon as the window opened.
- He called it "probably one of the best investment decision I've ever made in my life." He said the stock trades at a point with "a lot of upside," especially given the commitment to the long-range plan. He and Ankur both felt it was a great time.

**Why is the stock undervalued, and what unlocks it?** Thaysen acknowledged major uncertainties: China, NIH, tariffs. He said he understands why some investors see too much uncertainty. His message:
- Management has conviction and is making swift decisions to keep growing the top line and bottom line.
- The long-term opportunity is vast, and the algorithm to high-single-digit growth is "intact," primarily based on the continuing X transition, with new innovations adding growth.
- There are competitors that want a part of this, but "there's nobody out there... that have such an ecosystem, such a moat that Illumina have," with a commitment to innovate and help customers succeed.
- Repeating a line from earlier: no one "has really ever regretted deciding that they went with Illumina."

## Tone and what is new

Thaysen's tone was confident, defensive of the long-term plan, and repeatedly optimistic ("nobody has ever regretted going with Illumina"). He conceded real headwinds but framed them as external and controllable through cost cuts. Items beyond the Q1 2025 call included: the 65% volume versus under 50% revenue split on the X with a second-half 2025 crossover, the explicit volume and price model (mid-20% volume, mid-teens price decline), the 10-12% academic exposure estimate, the "subdued through 2025" academic outlook, and his explanation of the insider purchase.
