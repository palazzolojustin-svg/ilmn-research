# Oxford Nanopore CFO Nick Keher at the Barclays 28th Annual Global Healthcare Conference (fireside chat with Barclays analyst Jon Unwin)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ONT/transcript/ONT_2026-03-11_Barclays_28th_Annual_Global_Healthcare_Conference.md` · Date: 2026-03-11 · Seeking Alpha transcript of the Barclays 28th Annual Global Healthcare Conference; speakers Nicholas Keher (CFO & Director, Oxford Nanopore Technologies) and Jonathon Unwin (Barclays Bank PLC, Research Division) · conference*

**Summary.** Oxford Nanopore's CFO describes the company's 2025 revenue mix by end market (research 67%, clinical 13%, applied industrial 12%) and its push into clinical diagnostics and biopharma quality control. He compares its technology with PacBio, Roche's nanopore and Illumina's newly launched TruPath long-read approach, and explains why the original ">30% CAGR 2024-27" growth target is no longer expected to be met while the 2027 adjusted EBITDA breakeven target is kept. Guidance cited is 21-25% constant-currency growth for 2026, after 24% in 2025.

# Oxford Nanopore (ONT) at the Barclays 28th Annual Global Healthcare Conference, 11 March 2026

## Format and participants

This was a fireside chat (a question-and-answer conversation rather than a slide presentation) on 11 March 2026. Jon Unwin, an analyst on Barclays' European med tech and services team, interviewed Nicholas (Nick) Keher, Chief Financial Officer and a director of Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT). Unwin framed the discussion around ONT's four end markets: research, clinical, biopharma and applied industrial. He noted that research is the largest but not the fastest-growing segment, and he asked about the journey from research use, to lab-developed tests (LDTs, tests a lab designs and runs in-house), to fully regulated tests. [Barclays, 2026-03-11]

## Revenue mix and growth by end market (the year just ended, 2025)

Keher gave the following figures for the year just completed. All growth rates are for that year. [Keher, Barclays 2026-03-11]

| End market | Share of 2025 revenue | 2025 growth | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Research | 67% | "just over 15%" | Hit by NIH (US National Institutes of Health) funding turmoil, especially in the Americas |
| Clinical | 13% | 60% (about 85% in the Americas) | Almost entirely LDT revenue |
| Applied industrial | about 12% | 27% | Synthetic biology and core service providers |
| Biopharma | not quantified | not quantified | Described later as a high-priority segment |

- **Research.** Keher called the roughly 15% growth "a good result given the turmoil that kind of everybody has seen, particularly in the Americas with the NIH funding situation." The NIH-funded business is about 13% of ONT's total revenue. He said the company was "thankfully" seeing fast adoption in the applied markets.
- **Applied industrial.** ONT badges as applied industrial anyone doing synthetic biology plus core service providers (labs that run sequencing for others). Keher said there is a market of about $1 billion to $1.5 billion in synthetic biology where ONT has "a right to win." His reasons were long reads, richer information, quick turnaround time, price point, and the ability to "debatch" (run small numbers of samples on demand rather than waiting to fill a large batch). ONT is replacing Sanger sequencing (the older, lower-throughput method) there. He cited Plasmidsaurus, a plasmid-sequencing company, as a customer doing "very well."
- **Clinical.** Keher attributed the 60% growth (about 85% in the Americas) to the technology now being "really of age" in rare disease, infectious disease and oncology (particularly rare blood tumors). Unwin confirmed, and Keher agreed ("Almost, yes"), that nearly all clinical revenue comes from LDTs, and that some LDTs are second-line (a follow-up test) while there is also some adoption as a first-line test.

## Clinical strategy

### Rare disease
- Keher said rare disease needs long-read information to detect structural variation (large rearrangements of DNA). He claimed ONT has a higher diagnostic yield than the conventional legacy short-read technologies and can deliver results in an "ultra-quick fashion."
- LDTs are available, and reimbursement is becoming available in the US. As a result, the adoption curve is increasing, and customers are starting to see ONT as a frontline test rather than a "reflex test" (a follow-up test ordered only after another test).

### Infectious disease
- ONT's long reads give rich metagenomic information (sequencing everything in a sample when you do not know what you are looking for), and methylation (chemical tags on DNA) is also relevant. Keher said the workflow takes 3 to 4 hours and the technology can be miniaturized. He joked that "Gordon," a colleague, "always" pulls out a sequencer at this point.
- The market today is LDTs, but forward-looking players are moving toward in vitro diagnostics (IVD, regulated commercial test kits). ONT has signed deals with Danaher (through its Cepheid unit) and with bioMérieux, who will take the technology into the IVD space.
- Keher said the target is high-value infectious disease segments where pricing is "very high today" and where PCR tests "don't quite do it," because full metagenomic information is needed quickly to treat patients. He said these partners see ONT as the "intel inside" for that market.

### Regulatory status
- ONT built the AmPORE TB test in-house and has it to market with CE-IVD status (the European regulatory mark for in vitro diagnostics). Keher said "hindsight is always 2020, but maybe we might have thought about doing that in a different way."
- The GridION Dx box (a benchtop sequencer) also now has that status.
- In most markets, customers only need ONT to reach what Keher called a "Q-line" status, a "semi-regulated lockdown version" of the platform. That would let partners such as Danaher and bioMérieux develop regulated tests on top of it.

### Oncology and adaptive sampling
- Keher cited St. Jude (a children's research hospital) as a partner. At a recent ONT commercial kickoff, St. Jude said it can replace about $1,000 worth of pathology tests for diseases such as AML (acute myeloid leukemia) with adaptive sampling on ONT's platform. That costs $150 and gives "the exact same answers" needed to diagnose the patient.
- Adaptive sampling means programming the nanopore to look for genes of interest, "like a software program." If a DNA strand is not the right gene, the pore ejects it. ONT already offers a hereditary cancer panel, and a user can ask it to look for 100 different genes. This avoids whole-genome sequencing, is quicker, and is "a hell of a lot cheaper" because prep time and third-party kit cost fall. Keher said it "could revolutionize that space" and "is going to be a big driver for this company in the oncology space in particular."

### Where ONT has a right to win
Asked by Unwin whether the strongest applications are rare disease, infectious disease and anything needing fast turnaround or point-of-care use, Keher agreed and added "or where methylation is needed as well because we have the gold standard for methylation."

## Methylation as a biomarker

- Keher said conventional platforms take a deep but narrow approach to the methylome (the full pattern of methylation), so they do not capture 100% of the data. ONT can capture 100% and customers can go as deep as they want depending on the coverage they choose.
- A customer Keher met in New York the day before is using methylation in transplantation as a predictive marker of when an organ is going to fail. The same logic applies to oncology, where methylation increases as cancers become active. The marker predicts whether patients need to return to hospital, for example because a transplant is failing or a cancer is returning.
- He summarized the difference: ONT can look at the "entire haystack" across all sample types, rather than searching for the "needle in the haystack" you already know is there.
- ONT has a 50,000-person UK Biobank study under way that Keher said is "untapping all of this biology." He expects it to create "a whole new wave of interest," particularly from pharma (for drug discovery) and from diagnostics. He added, "We've got more to do here."

## Market sizing

Unwin asked how big the opportunities are and whether they involve share gain or new white space. Keher gave these figures (his estimates, for the market today). [Keher, Barclays 2026-03-11]

- Total NGS (next-generation sequencing) spend is $9 billion to $10 billion, and the clinical market is roughly one-third to one-half of that.
- ONT estimates its serviceable addressable market (the portion it can realistically serve) is $20 billion to $25 billion, because it also includes conventional tests such as PCR assays.
- The addressable market in clinical is about $10 billion.
- Biopharma is "maybe $3 billion to $4 billion" and is a high-priority segment that ONT "haven't quite got to" yet.
- Later in the talk he referred to "this $13 billion to $14 billion that actually we should win at," which appears to be the combined set of target end markets.

### Share gain versus expansion
- Keher said legacy technologies that are already adopted and work well "are going to be very hard to displace," and that is where ONT is "trying to avoid going." In diagnostics, "the test that works, the test that's used," and dislodging those tests is "maybe a fool's errand." ONT's focus is to provide value where customers cannot see it today. Its belief, which he said it can prove out, is that "you can see more on a nanopore."
- Customers have multiple conventional devices in their clinical labs, and ONT is used on top of them. Reasons include the need for quick turnaround rather than batching, methylation, infectious disease work, quality and accuracy.
- Keher conceded that ONT does not yet have the surrounding ecosystem that some competitors have, "but that is coming." The strategy is "land and expand." He said the technology has reached the point on robustness, accuracy and cost where this is working.

## Competition

### PacBio (the other long-read company)
Keher called PacBio's long-read technology "good technology… nothing bad on that front." ONT's claimed advantages are as follows.
- It sees native DNA and RNA.
- It can do longer reads, and any read length.
- Methylation comes from direct data points, and it can see RNA modifications that "any other platform" cannot.
- It scales, and form factor (the size of the instrument) matters.

He said "we all have benefits over each other in certain instances." As evidence that nanopore has a place, he cited ONT's growth of 24% last year (2025) "in a market which is definitely challenged" and 23% the year before (2024). He added, "we've got a long way to go still."

### Roche (the other nanopore-type player)
- Keher said it is a "very different type of technology." With ONT, "you don't need to take a copy" and it can see any read length. It gets all the methylation data and direct information from DNA in native form, or from RNA (he also mentioned protein, though the transcript text is garbled: "1-day protein").
- He said long-read information matters for structural variation in rare disease, and "over time, it will probably be proven out that it's important in everything." He cited form factor and debatching as further advantages.
- On competition generally: "there's more competition in the market. I wish there was less. But where we are today, the reasons why we're growing, we don't see these things changing."

### Illumina's TruPath (Constellation)
- Unwin said a Broad Institute genomics expert on a Barclays panel the previous day characterized Illumina's TruPath as "a direct shot at PacBio and ONT" and asked how ONT sees it.
- Keher said it is the Constellation technology renamed ("we've seen it coming for quite a while") and "yet another attempt trying to do long read."
- His main criticism is that it "is essentially a technology that's stitching together. So it's imputed. It's not direct. So it's taking short reads and putting them back together." He said "there's a lot to be proven out still," including how good it will be against ONT's direct reading of "the entire read length or any read length you want to go for."
- He believes ONT will "continue to have an edge" on long variants. For customers who want long reads plus methylation, "we give you the methylation for free. You're not going to have to do a second test."
- He raised open questions about the product: its price point ("yes, they put one up there, but how much volume has to come with it"), the device it must run on versus ONT's own, and the timeline for these pieces to come together. By contrast, for ONT's product "you can do it today."
- He also said it is "quite nice and endearing that everybody is recognizing that long reads are important and multi-omic information is important," and it would be "great if you can do it all on one platform, that you can, nanopore."

## Biopharma and quality control (QC)

Unwin asked what ONT does in biopharma QC and why it can save pharma money. Keher said pharma interest has built over a few years. [Keher, Barclays 2026-03-11]

- **Plasmid sequencing as the "door opener."** Plasmids are DNA circles used in cell biology experiments, and a control step is needed to confirm that what goes into a cell line is correct. Sanger has long been the gold standard, and Keher said "the gold standard will become ONT." Many customers are evaluating a move of plasmid sequencing to ONT because it reads the full plasmid, sees more, and is more competitive on price and speed than competitors.
- **mRNA vaccine production.** ONT signed a contract with "one of the leading players in the quality control environment." Here the comparison is not against other sequencing but against "7 to 8 orthogonal types of tests" (HPLC, mass spectrometry, gels) used to approximate what the vaccine looks like. ONT reads the molecule directly and can report its weight and its modifications, which gives a more direct measure of what has been made. This matters for safety and efficacy QC, and Keher sees a "big part" for ONT in personalized cancer vaccines. He said the deal is exciting because "we're not just being evaluated by one player. We're being evaluated by all of the players," though "these things take time."
- **Other workflows.** There is also interest in AAV (adeno-associated virus, a gene-therapy vector; the transcript renders it "[ AVA ], AAV") and in sterility testing more broadly. This is effectively infectious-disease-style work, using the same workflow and rich metagenomic information, quickly, in biopharma QC.
- **Why pharma is pulling.** Keher described an economic pull from the pharma company's CFO and a QC pull because ONT sees more information, faster. He said ONT can "take sequencing into an area where you don't see sequencing today."

On test volume (one test or 5,000?), Keher said it depends. For mRNA personalized cancer vaccines, testing is "literally per patient." For a European provider that has also signed on for sterility work, one facility monitors several sites, which is much more big-batch biologic production.

## Outlook, guidance and the change versus the 2024 targets

### Figures put to Keher by Unwin
- Constant-currency revenue growth was 24% in 2025.
- Guidance for 2026 is 21% to 25%, and "broadly similar" growth is guided for next year (2027).
- The target set in 2024 was a CAGR (compound annual growth rate) of over 30% for 2024 to 2027. Unwin said this is "unlikely to happen at this stage" and that actual growth is 5 to 10 percentage points below it. He asked what changed.

### Keher's explanation
- **The 2024 starting point.** When Keher joined in 2024, ONT had been aiming for breakeven in 2026, but a review of the business made clear that was not achievable. Guidance was pushed back so that (adjusted EBITDA, i.e. earnings before interest, tax, depreciation and amortization, adjusted) breakeven would come in 2027 and cash flow breakeven in 2028. These were "the north stars" for the company, Board and management.
- **The 2024 guidance package.** To help models and investors, ONT said it expected to reach breakeven by growing over 30% a year (a CAGR from 2024 to 2027, underlying and in constant currency), with gross margins over 62% and cost growth of 3% to 8% a year.
- **What happened.** ONT delivered 23% growth in 2024 and ran a big internal operational review. Last year (2025) it ran a strategic review on how to grow faster, because the top line was not reaching the desired level.
- **The cost response.** Keher said ONT "always said if we didn't achieve the top line, we would modulate the bottom line," and that this is written in the 2024 release. In 2025 it "took out a considerable amount of cost from the business twice," which he said shows "we do what we say." The strategic review also led to "a lot of changes internally."
- **Why growth fell short.** (1) The market was tougher than expected: the NIH funding issue "has happened post the guide," and China was tougher because of export control restrictions. "We didn't expect it to be this size of a headwind." (2) Execution: "on balance, we could have done better as well," with "a lot of soul searching." Keher claimed ONT is "growing a lot faster than all of the competition, undoubted," but could still do better.
- **Commitment.** He said the company can see end markets worth $13 billion to $14 billion that it "should win at," and the question is how to get there quickly. "We're aligning on that path." The 2027 breakeven adjusted EBITDA target was "written in stone, and we've kept it in stone. We've not moved it out," even with slower top-line growth, because "we're doing better on margin, we're doing better on cost control."
