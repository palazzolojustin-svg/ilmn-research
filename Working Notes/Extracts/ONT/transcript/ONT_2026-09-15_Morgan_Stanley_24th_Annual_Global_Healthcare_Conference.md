# Oxford Nanopore (ONT) at the Morgan Stanley 24th Annual Global Healthcare Conference – fireside chat with CEO and CFO, H1 2026 review, 2030 strategy, margins and path to EBITDA breakeven

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ONT/transcript/ONT_2026-09-15_Morgan_Stanley_24th_Annual_Global_Healthcare_Conference.md` · Date: 2026-09-15 · Seeking Alpha transcript of the Morgan Stanley 24th Annual Global Healthcare Conference; speakers Francis Van Parys (CEO), Nicholas Keher (CFO), interviewer Kallum Titchmarsh (Morgan Stanley Research, Life Sciences) · conference*

**Summary.** Oxford Nanopore's CEO (about 7 months in the role) and CFO discuss the first half of 2026, which was held back by China and the Middle East but still leaves them confident in 2026 guidance of 16-20% constant-currency core revenue growth. They explain the new strategy of focusing on about 20 high-value applications (clinical, biopharma), the research share of revenue falling from about two-thirds to about 40% by 2030, and the path to higher gross margins (flow cell recycling, consumables above 80%) and adjusted EBITDA breakeven in 2027. They also describe pricing, competition with the other long-read player, and the AI opportunity.

# Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT) at the Morgan Stanley 24th Annual Global Healthcare Conference, 15 Sep 2026

**Format and participants.** This was a question-and-answer session (a "fireside chat") held on Day 2 of the conference, at 10:45 AM EDT on 15 September 2026. Kallum Titchmarsh of Morgan Stanley's Life Sciences research team interviewed Francis Van Parys (CEO and Executive Director of Oxford Nanopore) and Nicholas "Nick" Keher (CFO and Director). The company is a UK-listed maker of nanopore sequencing, a technology that reads DNA or RNA by passing it through tiny pores and detecting changes in an electrical current. It produces "long reads" (very long continuous stretches of sequence), in contrast with the "short reads" of competitors such as Illumina. No analyst ratings or price targets were given. Speaker tags below follow the form [Speaker, MS conf, 2026-09-15].

## 1. How 2026 has gone so far

**CEO view.** Van Parys said he has been in the role for about 7 months and is as excited as on day 1. He said the first half "has gone reasonably well." There were "a few headwinds in the Middle East," which he said are "not exclusive to us," and headwinds in China that "we've been struggling with." He does not expect either to go away immediately, and they put pressure on first-half revenue. Even so, the company has "a good line of sight towards the full year" and expects to reach the full-year guidance it has given: "So, so far, so good." [Van Parys, MS conf, 2026-09-15]

**CFO view.**
- Keher said management always knew revenue would be weighted to the second half, because several contracts had rolled off, particularly in Europe and Asia Pacific. He said this "all played through as expected." [Keher, MS conf, 2026-09-15]
- China was "clearly a bit of a weak point," because of export control restrictions (government limits on selling technology into China) and "some things on our side."
- Despite that, he said gross margin improved meaningfully. Adjusted EBITDA loss (a measure of operating profit before interest, tax, depreciation and some other items) narrowed to GBP 22 million in the half. That is about GBP 26 million better than the prior-year half and about GBP 16 million better than the preceding half (sequentially). He described this as progress toward breakeven.
- He also cited a positive: a global diagnostics contract had been signed. Overall, "first half had a bit of ups and downs, but we're certainly on track."

**What stood out to the CEO.** Van Parys said he joined because of the technology and its potential, and that this has been confirmed. His view: "I don't think we've seen the adoption of the technology as much as the science deserves." His ambition is to scale the company faster by focusing on "fewer applications, higher-value applications that we feel we have a really clear path to win." He praised the team's talent, said he has added selectively to the management team to complement existing skills, and said he is pleased that after 6 months the company communicated a new direction, including how it intends to scale and how it sees the business by 2030.

## 2. Device sales, consumables pull-through, and China/Middle East

**Question.** Device sales were very strong in H1. When will consumables (the reagent kits and flow cells customers must buy for every run, which is where recurring revenue comes from) pull through, and what must change for China and the Middle East to recover?

**Answer (Keher).**
- First-half device sales were very strong, as they were in the first half of last year. There is usually about a 3-month gap between placing a device and the customer ramping up, with timing varying by customer.
- He is "confident" consumables pull-through will pick up in the second half and into 2027.
- Consumables growth was only single digit in H1, but volume on the key PromethION lines (the high-throughput instrument range) rose over 20%. New devices placed with existing customers should lead to pull-through in H2.
- **China.** He does not expect a recovery in H2. Export control restrictions have been very tight and tightening over several years, which is why a number of existing customers have been cut off from supply; this increased again going into 2026.
- Part of the China problem is Oxford Nanopore's own: it is restructuring its China commercial organization (mostly done) and reviewing and changing its distributor network.
- For H2 he expects "stabilization," with the decline expected to be the same 16% (he did not specify the exact measure or period), but stabilization from a fundamental perspective. "We're not giving up on China as a market for us as we go into next year."
- Middle East: no further detail was given beyond Van Parys's headwind comment above.

## 3. Mix shift to "applied" markets (clinical, biopharma, industrial)

Applied markets are clinical, biopharma and industrial uses, as opposed to academic research. The interviewer noted very strong H1 growth there and asked what is driving it.

**Clinical (Keher).** Adoption is happening in three areas: rare disease, infectious disease and oncology. Today the company mostly sits on top of existing platforms. Infectious disease is becoming more "frontline" (a first-choice test). In rare disease the company is landing on top of existing platforms but has a bigger opportunity to become a frontline test over time.

**Biopharma (Keher).**
- The company is "very excited." Uses include R&D (target identification, antibody characterization) and quality control (QC) testing, especially for mRNA personalized cancer vaccines.
- Adoption and growth are good, but "arguably, it could be better." He said that the company has "historically tried to do too much," and that the focused strategy with aligned commercial teams should allow better performance over time.
- The interviewer cited biopharma growth of 25% and noted that the company called it likely the largest contributor to growth out to 2030, with 20 biopharma customers actively evaluating the technology.

**Industrial (Keher).**
- Applied industrial grew "slightly weaker than we'd like." Reason: the plasmid market (testing of circular DNA used in biotech manufacturing), where the company does very well, is seeing "quite a bit of price deflation," with customers squeezing as much as possible out of each flow cell and multiplexing more (running several samples on one flow cell).
- Prices are now at a point that may open a new market segment and drive considerable volume growth, but this will take time. On the R&D roadmap side, this may remain a low-growth market for "the next couple of years."
- In food safety testing and other historically targeted areas, the new focus on about 20 target application areas means the company will not put resource into those areas. It may keep taking share, but it is not guiding to that, because the market opportunity elsewhere is arguably greater.

## 4. 2026 guidance and what moves results within the range

The interviewer cited 2026 guidance of 16% to 20% constant-currency revenue growth for the core business (constant currency strips out exchange-rate effects). Keher said:
- The company has the same visibility at this point in the year as last year, when it guided 20% to 23% growth and delivered 24%. The same relative coverage exists now from revenue already done, orders booked and scheduled to ship, plus the underlying run-rate business.
- Position within the range depends on the opportunity pipeline. Some of it is in U.S. NIH and other research activity, where projects "can fall in or out." In applied markets the swing factor is how quickly they continue to ramp.

## 5. Clinical business (up 35% year over year in H1)

**Pipeline and path from pilot to production (Van Parys).**
- There is a lot of inbound interest from reimbursement-funded labs.
- For rare disease and oncology, the strategy is "LDT enablement." LDTs are laboratory-developed tests, assays a lab builds and validates itself. Providers are validating and adopting the technology, typically as a "reflex test" (a follow-up test run after an initial result) versus existing short-read diagnostic tests.
- As labs see diagnostic yield rise, and generate clinical evidence that yield "can move from 30% to maybe 60% in some cases," there is an opportunity to broaden adoption and make it a frontline test. The company is "really in that uptake phase now," which is where the revenue growth comes from.

**Budget and competition.**
- Sometimes the company replaces existing tests, but mostly in rare disease and oncology it comes in as a reflex test on top of what exists, and may then push down into earlier use.
- "We're not the only one active in that market. It's usually competitive situation, but we've seen good win rates." [Van Parys]

## 6. Biopharma ramp economics

- **Timing (Van Parys).** Early revenue shows up as clinical-trial volume, which is smaller than commercial volume. After commercial approval, volume depends on the therapy's or vaccine's ramp. Typically there is a moderate take-up in year one, then full scope of the target patient population as the therapy is accepted, prescribed and commercialized.
- **Personalized vaccines.** Patient populations are typically 20,000, 30,000 or 50,000. "Each batch is a patient, each batch is a test." Full-scale impact takes time, but there are revenues before then: as one indication is approved, others are explored in further trials that generate volume.
- **Expansion across molecules.** For the same molecule and other indications, it first means more clinical-trial volume. For other molecules, pharma companies become familiar with the technology, the quality management system and the documentation supplied, so much validation need not be repeated; only molecule-specific validation needs new data. Once in with one molecule, joining the QC workflow for others in the pipeline is "a lot more easy."

## 7. Research market

- **Today (Van Parys).** The research market "has been pretty good for us," with mid-single-digit growth in H1 2026. In the U.S. there have been pressures on NIH (U.S. National Institutes of Health funding); "it's not getting worse," which he described as an impression rather than a clear stabilization. Europe "has been good."
- **Share of revenue.** Research is about two-thirds of revenue today and is expected to be closer to 40% by 2030. Applied markets will accelerate growth, but research remains important.
- **Levers if funding stays poor to 2030 (Keher).**
  - Accessibility: price points to get started are low. A MinION (entry-level portable device) costs $5,000 with 5 flow cells; a GridION $58,000; a P2i, which he called "a fantastic device," about $80,000. He said this is a considerably lower upfront capital cost than competitive products, and consumables can be batched with flexibility.
  - Financing: third-party financing is available in the U.S. and Europe, and reagent rental (paying for the instrument through reagent purchases) is always open for discussion, as with other big companies.
  - The company plans to "just continue to go as we are."
  - Interviewer's point: customers find budget for innovative products so as not to fall behind. Keher agreed: research dollars go either to repeating the same experiment or to unlocking new biology, and the company's advantage is long reads and the fact that it reads DNA and RNA directly, "which means we see all modifications."

## 8. 2030 revenue ambition: key assumptions

- Keher said the plan is "pretty prudent." On utilization (how heavily customers use their instruments), the company has historically seen considerable year-on-year growth but is not assuming it continues; it is only assuming further platform adoption and specific biopharma wins.
- On pricing, the plan assumes PromethION flow cell prices decline, because cost per genome keeps falling.
- The difference is a focus on specific, high-value end markets where the company has "a real right to win," avoiding spaces where other players compete.

## 9. Strategy, R&D prioritization and product constraints

**Process (Van Parys).** The company did a gap analysis of customer needs and requirements in each target application versus where it is today, and of product and software-workflow enhancements needed to win its "fair share." This will inform the product roadmap, R&D priorities and go-to-market structure, with refinements expected in all three.

**Reset.** R&D today is "a long tail of activities." Strategy was historically "driven by what's scientifically possible versus what -- where we can create customer value." There is an opportunity to reset with more operational discipline and a clear link between R&D investment and the customers served. The interviewer asked what would be stopped; the answer is the shift away from lower-priority applications such as food safety (see section 3).

**Biggest constraint.**
- Dependability and robustness of the technology "is not a theme anymore"; customers are not raising it.
- The gaps are workflow enhancements, some bioinformatics gaps in specific segments, and generating evidence and data to substantiate claims: showing why long reads are valuable, the "so what" of methylation (chemical tags on DNA that affect gene activity) and structural variants (large rearrangements of DNA), and which questions these answer.
- These are "tweaks" and "enhancements," not "huge showstoppers." "We don't need to develop any new platforms or anything. The technology is there."

**Software/bioinformatics as revenue.** It could be a revenue driver, depending on segment. In biopharma in particular, customers expect "sample to answer and to report" (sample prep, library prep and bioinformatics all in one). This is not necessarily so in clinical applications. "Software as a revenue generator is certainly an opportunity for us going forward."

**R&D spending outlook (Van Parys).**
- Current R&D spending is where it needs to be and should be sustained for the foreseeable future (2 to 3 years or so). The company can reprioritize within it.
- From 2028 onward, as the business scales, spending might "probably increase that spend a little again," but only with operational discipline and clear prioritization in place (described as "Phase 1").
- He is comfortable that winning in the target applications can be done within current spending levels.

## 10. AI

- **Opportunity.** The interviewer said investors view data generators as AI winners. Van Parys said sequencing generates the richest data (long reads, methylation, structural variants), and "if you use agentic AI, you've got to base it on the richest data."
- **UK Biobank.** As a first step, UK Biobank is processing 50,000 samples on Oxford Nanopore data, with results due at the end of 2026, as a use case for biological insights. This could lead to looking at the other 450,000 samples in the database and building on it with agentic AI models.
- **Internal use.** AI and machine learning is already core to base calling (converting the raw electrical signal into DNA letters). The company is also looking at improving data quality and using it to inform decisions, and hired a new Chief Information Officer who started about 3 months before the conference.

## 11. Gross margin

**H1 2026 gross margin of 62.2%.** Keher explained that:
- It is up about 400 basis points (4 percentage points) from the same period last year. Last year included a 330 bps write-off related to inventory, which has now dropped out; the company said at the time that the "see-through" margin was about 61%.
- Product mix hurt the margin: more device sales relative to consumables (devices have lower margins). The interviewer cited a 160 bps gross margin headwind from mix in H1; Keher clarified it was "customer and product" but the product piece is what matters.
- He expects mix to normalize. Currency headwinds have faded as the dollar stabilized.
- Over several years, changing the pricing model to selling devices rather than placing them with customers has considerably improved margin.

**Outlook.**
- The company aims to do better than 62%, with further increases as it exits the year and beyond.
- **Flow cell recycling.** One of the most expensive parts of a flow cell is the wafer (the chip). Under an ESG push, the company gets chips back from the vast majority of customers, particularly on PromethION, and strips, cleans and reuses the wafer. On MinION this works and has driven MinION margins to "quite high levels." On PromethION it is just starting, with a "low percentage" of flow cells recycled so far and work under way with different wafer suppliers so customers cannot tell the difference. Success could give "a circa 10 percentage points increase in the margin for the PromethION range alone," its biggest revenue line.
- **Consumables gross margin** is about 75% today, versus about 63% a few years ago; "getting over 80% is absolutely possible" over the next couple of years. [Keher, citing slides from recent results]

**Why clinical and biopharma mix is initially dilutive.**
- Biopharma: mix shift will help gross margin. Most QC-pipeline customers use MinION with GridION Q, with substantially higher margins.
- Clinical: greater competition in some segments. Against "the other long-read player" there is at times price competition, so margin is lower than desired. Upcoming product advances will allow more samples per flow cell; since pricing is per sample, that should lift margin. For now, adoption is the priority.

**Devices and services margin.**
- The interviewer cited devices and services gross margin of about 34% with potential for about 40%, and asked which journey is harder.
- Keher said devices and services is harder. Compute and memory costs are rising, and the company is completing development on products to take out cost, but those benefits will be offset by headwinds next year. He expects device margins to stay broadly flat in 2027.
- The difference will come from services, which he defined as fixed contracts for enhanced support plans (not sequencing performed for customers). The share of customers signed up could be higher, and as it scales, margin should "improve significantly."

## 12. Path to adjusted EBITDA breakeven in 2027

Keher said the plan requires continued growth and has these elements:
- **Revenue:** 2027 guidance already given to the market is mid-teens growth, which he believes is "absolutely possible."
- **Gross margin:** expansion of "a few hundred bps" above the 62% guided for this year.
- **Costs:** adjusted operating expenses fell 7% in H1 2026. Full-year 2026 guidance is negative 2% to 0 (a change of -2% to flat), which he feels "very comfortable" with.
- For next year, there are opportunities to remove non-headcount spending, particularly logistics and IT costs, with focused programs of work; this frees money to invest elsewhere. With internal reprioritization, costs stay broadly flat. Then, "we'll do one of those magic things that the company does turn profitable."

## 13. What is underappreciated

- **Van Parys:** the long-read versus short-read debate, accuracy and technology maturity are "all behind us now." Customers tell the company "they want us to succeed" and "don't want us to give them a reason not to validate our technology."
- **Keher:** the company is often grouped with other sequencing companies "far too often." It is a "true sensing platform" that can see things other platforms and technologies cannot, as shown by the mRNA opportunities. "I think we're playing in a different park than those guys actually. And I'm not sure the investment community necessarily appreciates that yet."

## Risks and caveats raised

- China: export controls and company-side restructuring mean no recovery expected in H2 2026.
- Middle East: headwinds weighed on H1.
- U.S. NIH research funding pressure, described as "not getting worse" rather than clearly recovering.
- Price deflation in plasmid testing, and price competition with the other long-read player in clinical.
- Rising compute and memory costs offsetting device cost reductions in 2027.
- Consumables growth only single digit in H1, so H2 pull-through must materialize.
- Biopharma ramps take time and depend on therapy and vaccine approval and uptake.
