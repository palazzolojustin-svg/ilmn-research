# Oxford Nanopore (ONT) fireside chat with RBC Capital Markets: leadership hires, target applications, biopharma QC, partnerships, cross-licensing deal and 2030 guidance

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/ONT/transcript/ONT_2026-08-28_Discusses_Leadership_Team_Expansion_and_Strategic_Focus_on_Key_Applications_and.md` · Date: 2026-08-24 · RBC Capital Markets (Charles Weston, European Life Sciences analyst) hosting Oxford Nanopore CEO Francis Van Parys and CFO Nicholas Keher; transcript published by Seeking Alpha on 2026-08-28 (session held 2026-08-24) · conference*

**Summary.** Oxford Nanopore's CEO and CFO answered RBC's questions after the company's strategy update, covering new senior hires, a focus on 15-20 high-value applications, biopharma quality-control (QC) adoption and pricing (about $2,000 list per mRNA test pack), the clinical partnership structure, a patent cross-licensing deal, and the guidance of at least £700 million revenue by 2030 with an operating margin above 15%. They described research-market conditions as healthy in Europe and Asia ex-China, flat to down in the Americas and down in China. They said the $700 million guidance is deliberately conservative and excludes licensing and royalty income.

# Oxford Nanopore Technologies (ONT): RBC fireside chat after the strategy update

**Event.** A Q&A-only session hosted by Charles Weston of RBC Capital Markets (who covers European life sciences) with Oxford Nanopore CEO Francis Van Parys and CFO Nicholas Keher. The session took place on 24 August 2026 and was published by Seeking Alpha on 28 August 2026. The analyst said at the outset that he would not recap the investor presentation (the strategy update), and instead probed the main drivers of the business in the near and medium term. Oxford Nanopore sells nanopore sequencing, a technology that reads DNA or RNA by passing it through tiny pores and detecting the electrical signal; it is often called a "long-read" technology because it can read long stretches of DNA in one piece.

Note on currency: the market-size figures are quoted in pounds (£), but the revenue and licensing figures in the transcript ("$700 million", "$20 million upfront") are quoted in dollars. The speakers also say "$700 million" in places where the analyst earlier framed the market in £. The transcript does not reconcile this, so I report each as stated.

## The four strategic pillars and leadership

The analyst summarized the four pillars of the strategy as:

- customer-centric growth (a focus on key applications);
- focused innovation (using R&D to support those applications while keeping some "blue sky", or exploratory, investments);
- disciplined execution (simplify and standardize); and
- a high-performance culture.

Van Parys said this paraphrase matched what the company intended to communicate.

### Leadership gaps
- **Hires announced that morning.** [Van Parys, Aug 2026] The company announced a Chief Marketing and Communications Officer and a Chief Development and Product Officer. The latter will be responsible for product development and product management.
- **Why these roles.** The company ran a gap analysis comparing its current capabilities with customer needs in each high-value application. It found opportunities to close gaps in reasserting its value proposition, generating evidence, product development capabilities, and simplifying the portfolio. The two hires are meant to help with all of these.
- **Business development (BD) and biopharma.** The analyst noted several senior BD and biopharma hires on the company's website. Van Parys said BD and biopharma is an area the company still wants to strengthen, because the strategy update described different "participation models" by market segment.
- **Timing.** [Van Parys] "fingers crossed" the company will have a full management team by Q4 2026, to operationalize the strategy in 2027. Asked whether more senior-hire announcements were coming, he said "There's a couple. That's not more than that."

### What comes next on strategy detail
- The strategy update laid out "where and how we will play"; the middle two pillars (innovation and execution) were to be detailed before the 2026 full-year results.
- [Van Parys] Some decisions are underway and some are not finalized. By full-year results the company expects to publish "a true operational plan" containing a future product road map, go-to-market refinement, governance structure and organizational developments "if appropriate". Work on it will take "the next 2, 3 months".

## Target applications and market size

- **Number of applications.** [Van Parys] After a detailed prioritization and characterization exercise, the company has selected "anywhere between 15 and 20" high-value applications to focus on, not necessarily all at the same time. They were identified as significant contributors to growth over the next 4 to 5 years.
- **Disclosure.** The chart in the strategy update (ease of realization versus Oxford Nanopore's differentiation) sits within a serviceable market opportunity of £14 billion to £16 billion. More detail will come through deep dives in the coming year. The full analysis was withheld because of commercial sensitivity. The analysis of the company's "entitlements" in each application underpins its medium- and longer-term guidance.
- **Share of revenue in target applications.** [Keher] About 40% to 45% of current revenue is within the target applications, so 55% to 60% is in applications the company will still support but will not put resources behind in the same way.

## Biopharma

### Biopharma QC evaluations
- **Who is evaluating.** [Keher] About 20 pharma companies are evaluating the technology for quality control (QC, the testing that checks a drug batch meets specification). Global biopharma companies make up "over half", and the rest are companies, maybe not in the top 20, known for their specialisms and focus areas.
- **Why evaluations have been slow.** The analyst noted that Keher had admitted before that some delays were caused by Oxford Nanopore's own execution. Keher said:
  - Customer timelines cannot be controlled. Global companies assess once a year, so if the company misses that yearly meeting, "it kind of comes around again".
  - Customers have had delays on their own drug assets, because in most cases the work is on new assets rather than established ones. Sterility testing is the exception, since it runs across established assets.
  - On Oxford Nanopore's side, the path "has not gone in a straight line".
- **GridION Q.** The GridION Q is the company's instrument for this QC work. Version 1 was released nearly two years ago. Version 2 was released the week before the session and is now available to buy. It adds incremental features, particularly from an audit perspective, that customers required so the system can be part of BLA filings (biologics license applications, the regulatory submissions for biological drugs) and similar.
- **Does version 2 require revalidation?** [Keher] No. It is "more the latter" (ticking final boxes). "It's not a revalidation as such"; the updates are software features customers "perhaps would have expected first time around".
- **Assays (test packs) available.** Two key assays are on the market: the mRNA test pack and the plasmid test pack. Their critical attributes have been improved and now cover "the vast majority of what the customers want". Keher added that further work could allow greater value capture over time, particularly on mRNA.
- **Still to come.** Three more workflows (sterility testing, AAV and similar) remain to be worked through and released. They are described as key to unlocking the value in biopharma. Keher said the headline issues on the company's side have been worked through, though it cannot control issues on the customer side. His summary: "very confident on those things pushing forward from where we are."

### Which drug modalities
- **Why mRNA first.** [Keher] The company focused on mRNA first because it is "quite unique in how we sequence direct RNA" (reading RNA molecules directly rather than converting them to DNA first). The analyst noted Lonza and Moderna have talked about using sequencing in their processes.
- **Pipeline.** The immediate QC focus is mRNA, AAV (adeno-associated virus, a vector used in gene therapy) and a second vector type that the transcript renders as "[ AVA ]". There are also cell therapy applications, and cell line characterization may become relevant in the development process.
- **R&D versus QC.** [Van Parys] QC is where the company has talked most because of its capabilities. But there is also an R&D side, including antibody characterization and genomic characterization for cell therapy. Those are not QC workflows. He said biopharma has become significant because the R&D applications sit alongside QC applications, which are "a tech consolidation place ultimately to replace other modalities that are out there".

### How the £4.1 billion to £5.5 billion biopharma market was built
- [Keher] The estimate uses both bottom-up and top-down approaches. For QC, it is bottom-up, using known contract values and assets, risk-adjusted with industry-average probabilities of drug success, plus a top-down view built with external advisers. For R&D it is more top-down, because reliable bottom-up information is harder to obtain.

### Moderna and cancer vaccines
- The analyst referred to the large move in Moderna's shares the previous week on a melanoma vaccine, and the fact that sequencing companies rose because each patient's tumor must be sequenced.
- [Van Parys] Oxford Nanopore's immediate opportunity, "given the resolution of our technology", is on the QC side of mRNA, replacing existing tests in the quality-control process of an mRNA molecule. Tumor characterization is an opportunity but "perhaps not where we [are] as differentiated".

### Pricing and modelling biopharma
- **List price.** [Keher] The mRNA test pack is about $2,000 and is one test per patient (because each patient's mRNA vaccine is its own batch). He confirmed to the analyst that $2,000 is the list price.
- **Discounts.** For multiyear, high-volume commitments the company will discount, so analysts should not model $2,000 per patient. There is also growing interest in using the test pack in R&D by companies not far along in development. There the company discounts "more meaningfully" so that customers "get hooked on it" and use it early in their process.
- **Modelling approach.** Asked whether to model patients multiplied by $2,000 or about $1,000 after discounting, Keher said for mRNA that is how to think about it (patient volumes times price per test) and $1,000 is "not a bad place to start". The analyst later cited Visible Alpha as showing the Moderna vaccine expected in 100,000 patients per year by 2034 on an unrisk-adjusted basis, which he would multiply by $1,000. That figure is the analyst's, not the company's.
- **Why other applications are harder to model.**
  - Sterility testing is priced per batch, like a "vat", so it depends on how many batches a biologics manufacturer makes, which is hard for outsiders to model.
  - Plasmid testing is priced per plasmid and plasmid kit. Keher said the company was "surprised about how much companies actually spend on plasmid internally on Sanger" (the older sequencing method). He said each company could spend "quite a few million dollars" on in-house plasmid sequencing, beyond what it sends to external providers such as Plasmidsaurus.
  - The company will not give that level of visibility, but may report a "deeper level of KPIs" externally when it presents the operationalized strategy.

### Direct sales versus partners in biopharma
- **QC.** [Van Parys] Customers need a start-to-finish workflow that works every time and gives a consistent report, so the company intends to deliver the full solution from sample in to test report out, including bioinformatics.
- **R&D.** Partnership models vary. Partners may supply complementary kits. Oxford Nanopore focuses on what it fully owns, from kits to base calling (converting the electrical signal into DNA letters). Partnerships are primarily for automation, sample preparation, customized reporting and bioinformatics. For a specific workflow, the company typically wants to own the full solution.
- Partners named by the analyst: Lonza (direct RNA), ViruSure (pathogen testing) and PathoQuest (recently acquired).

## Clinical and diagnostics

### Growth drivers
- The analyst noted clinical grew 35% in the first half of 2026, making it the fastest-growing division.
- [Keher] Disaggregating the growth is "a bit more subjective than I'd like". The company has about five tagging layers of data and has retagged three years of data, reaching about 70% revenue visibility into what customers do with the product. It does not try to track every MinION customer (the small, low-cost device) because there are too many and each spends too little.
- **Order of growth.** In relative terms (fastest first): rare disease, then infectious disease, then oncology. In absolute terms, infectious disease has been a big growth driver. Oncology is seen particularly in Europe and parts of the U.S.
- **Oncology.** Use is in rare tumors such as ALL (acute lymphoblastic leukemia), where volumes are small but a quick, instant result is needed.
- **Infectious disease.** Oxford Nanopore is "the first technology to reach to" because of its speed and accuracy.
- **Rare disease.** [Keher] This is more competitive, but the company has data showing "a 30% uplift in diagnostic yield versus short-read technologies". He cited the MyOme contract and said it was a positive that Natera, "quite large and big in the space", is using Nanopore for rare disease because it thinks it is the best technology.
- The analyst said Natera would attend RBC's life sciences conference at the end of the month, and that Oxford Nanopore would take part in RBC's Virtual Life Sciences conference on 21-22 September.

### Three categories of partnership
The analyst listed Danaher, Cepheid, bioMérieux, Bio-Techne, Natera (with MyOme) and smaller partners, and asked how they fit together. [Van Parys] described three categories:

1. **Infectious disease IVD (in vitro diagnostic, a regulator-certified test) with Cepheid and bioMérieux.** This market is likely to require IVD certification, a capability the company chose not to develop entirely itself. OEM-type or co-development arrangements are needed. Because IVD certification takes a long time, including clinical trials, the company expects the return not to be significant "before 3 to 5 years from now". In the meantime it is ramping up manual workflows that will precede these developments.
2. **LDT enablement for oncology and rare disease.** LDTs are laboratory developed tests, run by a lab under its own validation rather than as a certified kit. Partners such as Natera are service providers that specialize in developing LDTs. Oxford Nanopore is the component and technology provider, the service provider develops the assay, and the partners give access to markets, particularly in the U.S. but also in Europe.
3. **Compatibility program.** Third parties develop assays or kits on the Nanopore platform and validate them there. These are smaller and more of an add-on, but could become significant over time as they enable new LDTs.

- [Keher] The deck included a slide mapping every partnership to these categories and to the end-market analysis of target applications, and this framework will guide future deals.
- **Automation.** Instead of developing its own automated platform on the ElysION (its automated sample-preparation instrument), the company will partner with all the automation providers, which is easier for customers. Keher said "you're going to see more, not less" partnerships, and that signing more of the right type is good.

### Nearer-term lever in clinical
- [Van Parys] The long timeframe applies to IVD for infectious disease. A nearer-term opportunity comes from service providers' LDTs. Several are validating the technology for oncology and rare disease tests. As these roll out to hospitals, the company expects near-term take-up. Because they are not IVD they can be adopted faster.

### Per-test value in clinical
- [Keher] Pricing is varied and negotiated per sample with the LDT provider, so he would not give detail now but may come back with more help. For the IVD setting he suggested looking up list or reimbursement prices for those tests, estimating a realistic value share for Oxford Nanopore, and multiplying by patient numbers.

## The cross-licensing agreement

- **Not a partnership.** [Van Parys] It is a cross-licensing agreement, "not a partnership", and does not involve Danaher or bioMérieux. He did not name the counterparty.
- **Why Nanopore IP is valued.** Asked what could be inferred about who would want Nanopore IP, he said the company has a broad IP portfolio, built over many years, and is "committed to asserting the value of those patents". The deal is one of the "participation models" for doing so.
- **Product sales.** The analyst noted $15 million of product sales to be booked in 2027 and 2028 with nothing mentioned beyond. [Van Parys] "That's what's been agreed," so assume value beyond that is royalties. Further product sales have not been agreed but "could evolve into something broader".
- **Deal economics.**
  - The analyst recalled $20 million upfront plus $15 million of product sales at company-average gross margin, which together represent 10% of the economic value (net present value) of the deal. His back-of-envelope estimate was peak royalties above $40 million a year and peak licensee end-market sales approaching $1.5 billion. These are the analyst's figures.
  - [Keher] He would not discuss the royalty rate specifics beyond what was said (low to mid-single digits), nor peak sales of others' products. When guidance was prepared, the company used market expectations for the counterparty's products covered by the IP and applied the known royalty rate. He confirmed 10% of the NPV sits in the $35 million ($20 million upfront plus $15 million product sales), "because of the duration of the patents and the royalty stream". He did not confirm or correct the analyst's math.
- **More deals?** [Van Parys] "There may be." It is not built into guidance and should be seen as upside to the minimum of $700 million opportunity by 2030. The company will assert its IP value where appropriate, "but it's not something that we should be assumed as a regular cadence".

## AI and decentralized diagnostics

In response to an investor question, [Van Parys] said the company has framed this conceptually as "biological intelligence". Nanopore, as a sensing technology, generates "one of the richest data sets" in sequencing, including methylation (chemical tags on DNA that affect gene activity) and structural variants. AI should help in analyzing larger datasets and gaining biological insight, which will drive opportunity. He said it is early, and personally he believes decentralized testing near patients, in clinics or doctors' offices, "has some time" before it arrives. Oxford Nanopore is "the only technology that is as scalable and could monetize that opportunity". Infectious disease could move to a decentralized setting sooner. Whether it drives significant value between 2028 and 2030 "remains to be seen. Beyond 2030, no doubt."

## Research market

### Regional conditions (company triangulation from peers' comments)
The analyst described Europe as healthy, the U.S. as challenged (particularly government-funded) and China as challenging. [Keher] said:

| Region | Market condition per Keher | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Europe | Healthy, which he confirmed means high single-digit growth | Oxford Nanopore outperforms the market |
| Americas (all funding sources) | "Flat to down" | Not healthy; NIH funding "just isn't flowing" even after lapping it a year; the company outperforms the market |
| Asia ex-China | Healthy, high single digit | |
| China | Down "for everybody" | Attributed to being the 5th year of a 5-year plan, so "less going on" |

The company is "not the bellwether", but triangulating from peers it is performing better than end markets without being able to separate from them as much as it would like.

### NIH funding
Asked about positive headlines on grants being released, Keher said some things are happening but not as much as hoped, and the process still needs people to action it. The company has "lots of opportunities... that have not been funded". If funding returns, "we should spring out the gates", but it is not seen yet.

### Self-help in research
- [Van Parys] Some target applications are in research and are sizable, such as research into disease mechanisms where methylation makes a significant difference. There the company can grow faster than the broader academic and biopharma research base.
- The company needs to do a better job asserting its value proposition and demonstrating evidence. The Chief Marketing and Communications Officer appointment should be seen in that light.
- Much of the data already exists but is not in a form a salesperson can present compliantly. "We just haven't proactively done some of that work historically. We've counted on our customers to develop the applications on what's scientifically possible. I think it's time to rebalance that a bit and say this is -- these are the 5 or 7 things we do really well in research."

## Pricing premium versus short-read and cost reduction

- The analyst noted cost per genome has fallen substantially in short-read and also in long-read, and asked what premium to short-read Nanopore can sustain.
- [Keher] The company sees that premium today and needs to underpin it. On a customer call the day before, a customer said it would pay a healthy premium (a multiple) for the technology, "but they won't pay where it is today", so the company has to "meet them somewhere".
- **Focus as the answer.** The historical strategy was to "go for everything like anyone anywhere". Now the company will focus on fewer applications, because developing all the required materials (marketing materials, bioinformatics, workflow, kit, sample prep, binders) for all applications means "you end up not satisfying anybody". With focused complete product sets it can answer the premium question better. In biopharma, he said, "there's a lot more value capture still to go against where our current pricing is, if we can do some new things as well."
- **Technology.** [Van Parys] In parallel the company is advancing the nanopore, software, kits and chemistry to remain competitive where volume is needed. The analyst's summary, which Van Parys confirmed ("That's correct"), was that work on more genomes per flow cell (the consumable that holds the pores) is ongoing and will be deployed as and where needed.

## Guidance and financial framework

### Growth path
- The analyst described guidance as mid-teens underlying growth in 2026, accelerating through to 2030, excluding royalties, with the company aiming to exceed $700 million. He noted a simple linear acceleration to that figure would imply an exit rate above 20%. He asked whether the acceleration is mix shift towards faster-growing applied markets or growth change within clinical and biopharma.
- [Keher] It is mainly mix. Over half the business (the 55% to 60% outside target applications) will grow more slowly, in line with the market, "up or down depending on the halo effect from technology developments". The target applications will grow faster and become a larger share. The company applied "10 haircuts" to target applications to get a baseline.
- **Cross-checks.** The last three years of history map out this way, and a pipeline split by target application and product type aligns, which he called confirmatory ("thank God").
- **Not linear.** Biopharma will have busier periods and year-to-year spikes, for example when certain drugs are approved and go into mass market, or when more clinical trials happen in other indications "like for lung", since Oxford Nanopore will be used in trials as well. "I wouldn't expect it to be completely linear, but acceleration because of that underlying base essentially just getting bigger and bigger."

### Philosophy behind "greater than $700 million"
- [Keher] The company deliberately chose a number it can execute against and that is credible, balancing an ambitious target for the Street with one it knows it will deliver. A greater-than figure leaves room to "meaningfully beat" it; a range would limit upside. The number captures "a lot of risk elements".
- **Method.** A series of about 10 "haircuts" from total market value down to what the company believes it can win, then a cut for market share. A big assumption is market share: the company assumed "quite healthy competition", so it could do better.
- **Exclusions.** Guidance excludes BD items such as the royalty agreement and the upfront payment. Keher said these will be additive, and that the company should not forecast other people's product sales. The aim was to show the core business it can deliver today.
- [Van Parys] "full confidence we can deliver $700 million or more." The internal plan may be somewhat more ambitious. The company was "cautious, realistic" and not "overly bullish", since drugs in the pipeline may or may not be approved and successful. "It's certainly our ambition to get to the $1 billion sooner rather than later." (The analyst had mentioned Keher's earlier illustrative references to $800 million and $1 billion when discussing margins.)

### Operating margin (over 15%)
- The analyst asked how much room the guidance leaves to spend even at $700 million, especially with gross margin likely to rise.
- [Keher] The baseline assumptions carry headroom. Across all modelled scenarios, worst to best case, the spend profile is nearly the same ("within a fraction of each other") because the company must spend to do these things and revenue may just take longer. He said that even against the greater-than-15% figure, "we feel comfortable".
- **Gross margin.** There are things the company can do to move it higher. Because investors ask often, the strategy deck added color on consumable mix, which he said had probably not been shown since around the IPO, so investors can see progress. "We're not finished", and there are "really encouraging things that we can do to improve gross margins over time".
- **Why BD is excluded.** [Keher] Asked what counts as BD versus a customer or partner sale, he used the global diagnostics company deal as the example, calling it "transformational for our P&L": if the analyst's numbers were right, the income would go "straight down to EBITDA" and be "quite meaningful to the margin profile". The company did not want people to say it reached its margin only because of the royalty agreement, so it gave a baseline core view for investors to add to. He hoped for more deals, "not necessarily like that deal", that help the outer years.

## Competition and differentiation

- The analyst said the company had clearly outgrown its direct long-read competitor (not named) and asked for one or two things that set it apart in adoption.
- [Van Parys] Adoption has come "where the rich data, the near-term real-time nature of the sequencing really drives different biological answers", which has informed the choice of future high-value applications. A third differentiator is the scalability of the platform, which will drive future opportunity. He described these as the clearest differentiators of the platform.
- There was no specific discussion of Illumina in this session. The only references to short-read were the 30% rare-disease diagnostic-yield uplift versus short-read technologies [Keher] and the falling cost per genome in short-read raised by the analyst.

## Closing remarks

[Van Parys] "We believe and I believe the technology is ready to mature and scale. And it's about now delivering to the potential that it has shown and execute on that." He said the company looks forward to updating investors on its path to "a minimum of $700 million by 2030".
