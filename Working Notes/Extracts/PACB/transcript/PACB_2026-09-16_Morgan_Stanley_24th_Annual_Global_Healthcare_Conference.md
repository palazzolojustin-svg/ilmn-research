# PacBio CEO Mark Van Oene at the Morgan Stanley 24th Annual Global Healthcare Conference (fireside chat)

*Source file: `Working Notes/Sources/PACB/transcript/PACB_2026-09-16_Morgan_Stanley_24th_Annual_Global_Healthcare_Conference.md` · Date: 2026-09-15 · Seeking Alpha transcript (published Sep 16, 2026) of Morgan Stanley 24th Annual Global Healthcare Conference; speaker Mark Van Oene (CEO, President & Director, Pacific Biosciences); interviewer Michael Podoll (Morgan Stanley, leads U.S. life science tools and diagnostics coverage) · conference*

**Summary.** New PacBio CEO Mark Van Oene says his job is execution, not a strategy overhaul: land the latest restructuring (about $40 million of savings next year), move customers to the new SPRQ-Nx multi-use chip reagents (list price cut 30%), and deliver the high-throughput sequencer in 2027. He describes clinical germline genomics (rare disease, carrier screening, newborn screening) as the main growth path, says U.S. NIH/academic funding is not recovering, and frames AI-company data deals (Basecamp Research) as upside that is not in the base case. He targets gross margin above 40% and cash flow breakeven in 2028.

## Context and format

This is a fireside chat (a one-on-one interview rather than a prepared presentation) held at the Morgan Stanley 24th Annual Global Healthcare Conference on September 15, 2026, at 4:50 PM EDT. The transcript was published on Seeking Alpha on September 16, 2026. Mark Van Oene, Pacific Biosciences' (PacBio's) CEO, answered questions from Michael Podoll of Morgan Stanley. Podoll congratulated Van Oene on "stepping into the new CEO role." The transcript contains no analyst ratings, price targets, or new numeric financial guidance. It does include several quantitative statements from management, covered below. Illumina and Oxford Nanopore are not named. The only competitive references are to short-read sequencing in general and to labs' fragmented U.S. testing.

A note on terms. PacBio makes "long-read" sequencers, which read long continuous stretches of DNA. The accuracy-optimized version of its data is called HiFi. Short-read sequencing (Illumina's approach) reads small fragments. Revio is PacBio's current high-volume sequencer. A SMRT Cell is the consumable chip on which sequencing runs happen. "Consumables" are the chips and reagent kits customers must buy for each run. "Germline" testing looks at inherited DNA (as opposed to tumor DNA).

## Strategy and priorities for the new CEO

**Execution, not a new strategy.** Van Oene said he has been at PacBio "a little over 5 years" and that he and Christian (the prior CEO, named only by first name) "worked hand-in-hand on really defining where we could build out the portfolio and the strategy." He said: "this is not me making a wholesale change on the strategy. It really is... the execution." [Morgan Stanley conf., 2026-09-15]

**Focus on clinical markets where the technology is differentiated.** He said the focus is "germline genomics right now," meaning carrier screening (testing healthy parents for gene variants they could pass on), newborn screening, and rare disease diagnosis. Part of the execution task is "bringing the commercial organization together" to pursue these markets and "seize the momentum that we're starting to see both in Europe and now starting in the rest of the world." He described the change as "very execution-focused... more so than a big strategic shift."

**Priorities for the next 12 to 18 months** (answer to Podoll's question about the top two or three priorities):
1. *Land the restructuring.* PacBio has just been through a restructuring that reduced cash burn. Van Oene wants to settle the commercial and R&D groups that were affected. He said investors should "look for the cash savings next year," which he put at "$40 million savings between headcount and nonrecurring expenses." He expects to be judged on delivering those operating-expense savings over the next year.
2. *Execute the SPRQ-Nx transition* (details below). He wants PacBio to "exit 2026 with consumable growth, margin growth, setting ourselves up on Revio for 2027."
3. *Deliver the high-throughput platform* (PacBio's next, higher-capacity sequencer, not yet named in this transcript). He said it was "not impacted through the restructuring" and that development is aimed at execution "later on next year" (2027). SPRQ-Nx gives PacBio "the economics to have conversations for large deals that we haven't been involved with in the past," and customers now want a higher-throughput platform.

**Capital allocation and the pipeline.**
- Van Oene said "we've had to go through 3 restructurings in 3 years," and that PacBio "exited the short-read business." That business had been meant to help PacBio reach "the piece of oncology that long reads can't really go after very well." He said "we've done a lot of the hard work from a capital deployment" standpoint.
- The company is "very focused on Menlo Park" (its California site). It has "started in-sourcing all of our Vega manufacturing" (Vega is a PacBio sequencer product, mentioned here only in this context). He wants to better use the Menlo Park space to absorb that manufacturing.
- "The biggest expense right now is the R&D program around the high-throughput platform. And that's not going to change." He called it the priority and said he wants to stop distractions and not "add a bunch of new stuff to the development pipeline." His goal is to launch the "complete portfolio of the 3 different HiFi sequencing technologies" (the three are not itemized in this transcript).
- Other R&D items: "some updates to the Kinnex RNA" (PacBio's RNA sequencing kit family) and "smaller updates to some improved automation around library prep" (library prep is the step that prepares DNA for sequencing). He described these as extensions of the high-throughput effort: "You can't launch a high-throughput platform in the market and not fully enable people to access and utilize that data."
- He stressed the high-throughput program "is end-to-end... I don't want you to confuse that for just making another sequencing box." It includes the back-end data software, built or partnered, which he called "precision health intelligence," a term that "other people will call... an AI platform." The aim is to productize the software so that customers, and others who just want the data as a product, can get access "through data exchange."

## Long-term vision (3 to 5 years)

- Van Oene said the last two years have been "tough" for life science tools companies, and that this is pushing PacBio toward non-traditional funding sources. PacBio was built mostly on plant, animal and other non-human research, and more recently on human academic research. He said PacBio will keep supporting those customers but that the "drive into clinical is just critically important."
- His long-term goal is "making HiFi a frontline genetic test using whole genome sequencing, not just a reflex or not just a subset, but a frontline test for rare disease diagnostics." (A reflex test is a follow-on test ordered only after an earlier result. Frontline means the first test ordered.) The enabler is higher-throughput technology with "even more affordable pricing per genome."
- His reasoning: in the "rare disease odyssey" (the long search for a diagnosis) labs use many different technologies, and PacBio's test could consolidate them. That would change the economics for labs and for the health systems deploying the tests. He said "publications are coming out regularly now to show the increased diagnostic yield and benefits of using HiFi as frontline."
- He expects PacBio to get funding from "AI therapeutic companies and nontraditional customers" over the next 3 to 5 years, a "new category of customers" that will bring multimodal data (several data types combined) into models of biology, health care, therapeutics and diagnostics. He said this "will likely replace academic and government in many ways."

## SPRQ-Nx: product, customer feedback, and what to watch

**What it is.** SPRQ-Nx is a new reagent set that lets customers reuse the SMRT Cell "up to 3x." Van Oene said this "multi-use chip" gives PacBio the economics to lift gross margin and to "drive some price elasticity in the market by lowering the price" (price elasticity meaning that lower prices should bring higher volumes). Shipments started at the end of May 2026.

**Customer feedback so far** (only a few months of data):
- Customers see "really good first and second runs, a little bit less yield on the third runs." Much of the work is in "the workflow and the timing and how that will play out in the customers' hands." He said customers are understanding that dynamic better and PacBio's team is equipped for those conversations.
- "What they do love is the economics." PacBio "lower[ed] our list price by 30%." He said this is driving new conversations, new and larger projects, and some fleet expansion, where customers with enough interest add more Revio instruments. He said it is "doing what we had expected."

**Financial impact and timing.**
- In Q2 2026, annual consumable "pull-through per instrument" (the yearly consumables revenue generated per installed instrument) "came down closer to $200,000." He first said utilization, then corrected himself to pull-through.
- He expects that level to "continue through this quarter" (Q3 2026). He called the shift "a 2-quarter transition," with consumable revenue and annual pull-through expected to be "back up again" by the end of 2026, "setting ourselves up for 2027."
- Gross margin: SPRQ-Nx is "the first product that's going to help us start to increase the gross margin for the company again more meaningfully." He said "you're going to start to see us recover some of the gross margin that we had to hit on last quarter" as adoption and utilization rise. (He did not give the Q2 gross margin figure here.)

**Barriers.** Asked about barriers, he said "I don't think it's a barrier." The slowness has been in "getting people to put testing into clinical production." Labs must run a commercialization process: move validated tests from R&D to production sites and train their commercial teams. He said customers "are on board, they're validating, they're working with us," and PacBio can "do a better job of supporting them as a customer segment."

**Why the multi-use SMRT Cell matters** (his answer to "most underappreciated"):
- Being able to use the SMRT Cell more than once is a breakthrough that outsiders do not appreciate. The cell "is the majority of the cost of our sequencing," and having to throw away "this really expensive chip every time" had held PacBio back from being "economical at large-scale programs."
- Amortizing it over "2 or 3 or over time, 4 or 5 uses" changes both the price PacBio can offer and its gross margin.
- "For the first time, I'm staring at a product line that will have the gross margin that will actually make us a sustainable company." He said one of his biggest questions before joining PacBio was whether it had "a technology that will give them the gross margin to be successful."

## Clinical beachheads: Europe versus the U.S.

- **Initial beachhead is germline genomics, and it varies by region.** Whole genome sequencing is growing well in Europe. Revio volumes at academic and medical centers there are in "the thousands of samples, not... the tens or hundreds of thousands." He expects rare disease whole genome sequencing to spread across more hospitals and countries.
- **Europe has moved to frontline.** "Europe has already started to transition this to frontline, not reflex, not a subset." Taking that success to the rest of the world, he said, "is about getting it to frontline."
- **U.S. is different.** "The fragmented U.S. system and the centralized testing in the U.S. does not afford itself to the same ways of thinking about deploying this." He expects the earlier U.S. clinical adoption to be carrier screening.
- **Quest example.** "We've already seen Quest launched ataxia panels" (Quest Diagnostics; ataxia is a group of inherited movement disorders). He expects others to launch ataxia carrier screening and other targeted panels before moving into whole genome sequencing for rare undiagnosed disease.
- **Volume reasoning.** "We can do hundreds of thousands of samples on Revio for targeted approaches. We can't yet do hundreds of thousands of samples well on Revio for whole genome sequencing in the U.S." So the U.S. approach is to start labs on "subsets of reflex testing so that they're scaled and ready." Longer term he sees the higher-throughput platform and its economics enabling whole genome sequencing "here in the U.S. and Asian markets as well."

## Funding environment and resilient demand

- **U.S. NIH and academic funding.** "It's been a tough few years." Even before the current administration, NIH grants were getting "smaller and more decentralized and supported the smaller researchers." The problem now is "the uncertainty of the timing of the funding" and of the next round.
- **No budget flush.** In mid-September he had hoped for a year-end "budget flush" (a late-fiscal-year burst of spending by labs using up budgets), but said "I haven't seen a budget flush." PacBio never bakes one into forecasts but would welcome the upside. He does not know whether others in the industry are seeing it.
- **Outlook and U.S. results.** "We're not expecting any major changes in the U.S. NIH academic environment for us." The U.S. business was "relatively flat because we're growing the clinical business to offset the NIH shortfalls, but it's the reason why we're not growing." He said PacBio is "absolutely poised to take full advantage" if money starts flowing again.
- **Where PacBio is shifting.** Biopharma, philanthropy, and clinical opportunities.

**What the clinical push involves.**
- PacBio has "really good workflows for academics" and has invested in DNA and library prep, automation "to feed the Revios," and computational biology tools for variant calls (identifying differences in a person's DNA). It has not invested in "the end of the workflow through interpretation and clinical reporting," so it plans to partner there. The goal is "recurring revenue... from those clinical customers in a more decentralized way," with "more routine sample volumes and more routine revenue."
- **Biopharma:** "renewed interest from biopharma and not just for access to data, but larger data sets for enrichment of their data sets for their own target discovery work." He said this is separate from clinical and preclinical trial recruitment, and he sees "discovery work for biopharma through enrichment with HiFi" as a segment worth resources.

## Software and data

- **Secondary analysis pipelines.** PacBio has "made big investments" in pipelines for phasing (sorting which variants sit on which parent's copy), structural variations, copy number, paralogs (near-duplicate gene copies), and methylation (chemical tags on DNA). He said these are not "native short read" information, so they had to be built and are now integrated and streamlined.
- **HiFi Solves.** Described as "a federated network of hospital systems around the world" that can run and share HiFi data and run secondary and some new tertiary analysis within the network, including querying for low-frequency variants. He said PacBio is "starting to build up the sovereign data infrastructure and the federated network" so labs can learn from each other. He called this "a really important part of this next phase of PacBio."

## What has to go right: indicators to watch

Van Oene gave three indicators for reaccelerating growth:
1. **SPRQ-Nx and gross margin.** A successful transition "through 2027" should push gross margin "above the 40% threshold that we're under right now." This is the first and most immediate indicator. Many current multi-system Revio deals around SPRQ-Nx are with customers who have demand for high throughput, and PacBio has "figured out how to price these deals to get them ready and scaled for the high throughput when we launch it later next year." He said to watch Revio utilization through 2027 as preparation, because "not many labs have ever run 10,000 genomes a year."
2. **Clinical consumable growth.** Excluding the lumpiness of Revio instrument placements, clinical consumables show "getting into that routine testing." The target is to "keep growing our clinical consumables 50-plus percent over the course of the next couple of years."
3. **High-throughput platform.** "The majority of the revenues are made" and "the majority of the margin comes from" high-throughput sequencing. PacBio must execute the launch next year, build a sales pipeline, and drive utilization. That "sets up 2028 with the gross margin and the higher utilization of a high-throughput technology to get to cash flow breakeven."

## AI and the Basecamp Research deal

- **The deal.** The first PacBio deal in the AI-and-health-care area is with Basecamp Research (England). Basecamp is building a foundation model of biology (a large AI model trained broadly on biological data) based on a "1 trillion gene atlas of genes found in soil samples," from metagenomic (mixed-organism) bacterial samples. Basecamp believes the gene diversity will help it build better models for antibiotics, vaccines and other therapeutics.
- **What PacBio does.** Basecamp sends soil samples and DNA from around the world, and PacBio sequences them with HiFi. Van Oene said early indicators are "really encouraging" and that "higher quality data is what they've been looking for."
- **Why long reads.** He said the differentiator is "the comprehensiveness and the quality of the data." PacBio has "the most complete genome," and models built on it are "future-proof" because users will want to return to the samples to retrain. He said this will not be just genomics: PacBio is "having conversations around using Kinnex and transcriptomics for this as well." He expects more such deals.
- **Not in the base case.** "I'm not putting any of this in our base case model. And so when we talk about gross margin and... time to cash flow breakeven, that is outside of anything that I think would be a unique category and a new category of customers here for us."
