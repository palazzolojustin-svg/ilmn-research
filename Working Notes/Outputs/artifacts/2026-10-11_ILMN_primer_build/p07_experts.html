4What the expert calls say

The folder holds 23 expert calls, mostly from 22 July to 6 August 2026, with two later Tegus calls. The panel is good on switching costs, Roche and the research market, and weak on the clinical numbers that matter most: no expert gave a clinical gigabase growth rate, and only three are close to Illumina's big clinical customers.

4.1 How to weigh the panel

The calls are not 23 independent votes. Two of the July interviews are almost certainly the same former Illumina product director, interviewed a day apart. Several former employees left Illumina between 2014 and 2024, so their inside knowledge is one to twelve years old. Six work for competitors (Roche, PacBio, Oxford Nanopore, a SOPHiA software partner of Element), which gives them a reason to describe Illumina either as slow or as unassailable, depending on the point. Four are academic core labs, the weakest part of Illumina's market. 4.2 Eight things the calls agree on

  1. Clinical customers are very sticky. Revalidating a clinical test costs $100,000–200,000 at a small lab and up to $200 million for an FDA-approved test; trials cannot switch platforms midway; converting an established lab takes four to five years X.
  2. Rivals are added beside Illumina, not instead of it. A core adds "one Element, Ultima or AXELIOS versus maybe six NovaSeq Xes" X. Share loss comes from new labs, new tests, new projects and contract renewals.
  3. The NovaSeq 6000 base is the most exposed pocket. Several experts independently said rivals win where a lab is still on a 6000, because the price gap is largest there X.
  4. Price per gigabase keeps falling, and cuts no longer buy as much volume. Historically a 4× price cut brought 5–10× more sequencing; now halving the price brings perhaps 25% more X.
  5. Clinical growth is concentrated in 10–20 large labs that have the margins and the incentive to cut sequencing costs X.
  6. Research demand is weak and US funding is the swing factor, with one genome centre turning more pessimistic about 2027 X.
  7. Roche is a real but slow threat. Views range from "die out" to 10–20% share in three years; most experts expect little impact in 2026–27 and more pressure later X.
  8. Customers have barely touched the multiomics products. No interviewed customer reported buying SomaLogic, StrataMap or PIPseq; TruPath intent ranged from 0% to 6% of a lab's volume X.

4.3 Where they disagree

Market share. Estimates range from 95% (a loose guess) to 80%, 70% and the mid-60s, and nobody defines whether share means installed boxes, gigabases or dollars. Whether elasticity is dead. The former product director says cheaper sequencing barely matters now; academic cores and rare-disease labs say whole genomes became routine precisely because cost fell. Reagent rental. Two experts say Illumina never offered it until recently; a Roche employee says "that's what Illumina used to do." The filings disclose almost nothing on it.

4.4 What the calls say about the three Illumina questions

Clinical volume. No call quantifies it. The calls do test management's explanations: experts agree that reimbursement and ordering behaviour, not sequencing price, limit oncology volume; that NIPT is near saturation at 70–80% of US pregnancies; and that the growth is in MRD (tested three to four times a year) and in moving tests from exome to genome.

Over-earning. The calls support the idea that the 6000 tail converts slowly and that rivals target it, but no expert gave its size or price. One former employee's comment that large clinical labs pay "half or even a quarter" of a small lab's price suggests the tail is not earning the full list-price premium.

Multiomics. The calls give no purchase evidence for the 1–2 points. They describe interest from large academic centres (PIPseq, 5-base) and a sequencing-based spatial market that is "not more than maybe low eight figures" for Illumina today X.
