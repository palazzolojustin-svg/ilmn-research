Illumina: how it makes money, what the stock is pricing, and where the Street is wrong

A full breakdown of the DNA-sequencing leader: its revenue lines and their economics, the competitive fight over price per gigabase, management's guidance and the Bloomberg consensus, and a written list of where we think estimates are mis-set.

The answers up front

Illumina sells the machines that read DNA and, far more importantly, the chemicals every run consumes. Clinical labs testing for cancer and inherited disease now drive almost all of its growth. The business is improving. The stock has run well ahead of it: it trades at 52 times this year's earnings because the market is paying for faster growth than either management or the sell-side forecasts.

The business in five sentences

About three-quarters of Illumina's $4.6B of 2026 revenue comes from consumables, the flow cells and reagents used in every sequencing run, and about 90% is recurring. Customers are sequencing more than 30% more DNA each year, but consumables revenue grows only about 5%, because Illumina's newer NovaSeq X sequencer sells each gigabase for about a third of what the older NovaSeq 6000 did. Clinical labs now buy about 65% of sequencing consumables and grew 15–20% in recent quarters; research customers are shrinking on US funding cuts but stabilising. Management targets high-single-digit growth, a ~26% operating margin and double-digit to teens EPS growth in 2027, and has beaten every quarterly earnings guide since 2024. Competition has so far shown up in price rather than share, and the main new rival, Roche's AXELIOS, has only just launched.

Where we differ from the Street

Variance summary
  • Near term, upside. Illumina has beaten its revenue guide by about 2% and its EPS guide by about 13% on average. We expect Q3 near $1.17B and $1.42–1.45 (consensus $1,154M and $1.36) and FY26 EPS near $5.45–5.50 (consensus $5.36). The extra week in Q4 may be worth more than consensus models.
  • 2027–28, downside. Consensus needs sequencing consumables to accelerate to +8.8% and +9.3% and gross margin to rise to 69.5%. That requires revenue per gigabase to fall more slowly than it has, just as the 35B flow cell, Roche's AXELIOS and Medicare cuts arrive. Our base is about +7% consumables growth, 2027 EPS near $5.90 (consensus $6.11) and 2028 EPS of $6.50–6.70 (consensus $7.01).
  • Watch items. The X pull-through guide ($1.35–1.40M) looks stretched, though a small miss would mostly reflect more boxes placed; free cash flow and buybacks are running behind; research stabilisation and new products are the main upside risks to our view.

Valuation

Illumina trades at 52 times 2026 and 40 times 2028 consensus earnings, against a median of about 26 times for large tools companies and about 17 times for Roche. Peer-based methods give $155–265 a share. On our estimates a fair range is about $190–220; consensus numbers support about $225–245; only our bull case (2028 EPS of $7.60 at 40 times) reaches today's price. The average sell-side target is $222–234.

What decides it

On 29 October, watch clinical consumables growth against a +12% comparison (15% or more rebuts the slowdown, below 12% confirms it), the X's share of high-throughput revenue, and gross margin. X pull-through is disclosed only once a year, so its test comes with Q4 results in February 2027. In early 2027, the 35B flow cell's price and the first 2027 guide matter more than anything else. History says that first guide will be cautious; in February 2026 a cautious guide cost the stock 10%.

0Glossary

Every technical term used later is defined once here, in plain English. If a later sentence uses a word you do not recognise, it is in this list.

The science
DNA, bases, genome
DNA is the molecule that carries genetic instructions. It is written in four chemical "letters" called bases (A, C, G, T). A human genome, one person's full set of DNA, is about three billion letters long.
Sequencing
Reading the order of the letters in a DNA sample. Illumina's machines (sequencers) do this.
NGS (next-generation sequencing)
The modern method of reading millions or billions of short DNA fragments at once, in parallel. Illumina invented the dominant form.
SBS (sequencing by synthesis)
Illumina's chemistry. The machine copies each DNA fragment one letter at a time using tagged building blocks and a camera records each letter as it is added. XLEAP-SBS is the newest, faster and cheaper version.
Short reads vs long reads
Illumina reads fragments a few hundred letters long ("short reads"), which is cheap and very accurate. PacBio and Oxford Nanopore read thousands to millions of letters in one go ("long reads"), which helps with large rearrangements and repetitive DNA but costs more.
Gigabase (Gb), terabase (Tb)
A billion and a trillion DNA letters read. The industry's unit of output. A standard 30X human genome uses roughly 100 Gb.
Coverage, "30X"
How many times each position is read on average. Inherited-disease genomes use about 30X; tumour testing often needs 150X or far more because cancer mutations are rare in the sample.
Exome vs genome
An exome is the 1–2% of the genome that codes for proteins (about 8 Gb per test). A whole genome (WGS) reads everything (more than 120 Gb). Moving a test from exome to genome multiplies the sequencing needed by about 15 times.
Panel
A test that reads only a chosen set of genes. Small panels use little sequencing; large cancer panels such as TSO 500 read about 500 genes.
Library prep
The lab steps that turn a raw blood or tissue sample into DNA fragments the sequencer can read. Kits for this are a separate consumable.
Methylation, "5-base"
Chemical tags on DNA that switch genes on and off. Illumina's "5-Base Solution" reads the four letters plus methylation in one workflow.
Multiomics
Measuring several biological layers (DNA, RNA, proteins, methylation) from the same samples. Illumina's strategy is to make its sequencer the read-out for all of them.
Proteomics
Measuring proteins at scale. Illumina entered via SomaLogic, whose SOMAmer binders attach to proteins and are then counted by sequencing.
Single-cell, spatial
Single-cell sequencing measures gene activity cell by cell instead of averaged over a tissue. Spatial biology also records where in the tissue each signal sits. 10x Genomics leads both.
Q score
A logarithmic accuracy scale. Q30 is one error per 1,000 letters, Q40 one per 10,000, Q60 one per million. Higher matters most for cancer blood tests hunting one tumour fragment among millions.
Illumina's products and business
Instrument / sequencer
The machine. Sold once (or leased). NovaSeq X (high-throughput, roughly $1M), NovaSeq 6000 (older high-throughput), NextSeq 1000/2000 and 550 (mid-throughput), MiSeq i100 and iSeq (low-throughput benchtops).
Consumables
Reagent kits and flow cells a customer must buy for every run. About 74% of Illumina's revenue and the source of its profits.
Flow cell
The glass chip on which DNA is read. Sizes are named by how many billions of reads they hold: the NovaSeq X uses 1.5B, 5B, 10B and 25B flow cells, with 14B and 35B due in the first half of 2027. The NovaSeq 6000 used S1 to S4 flow cells.
High-, mid-, low-throughput (HT/MT/LT)
Classes of sequencer by output per run. High-throughput (NovaSeq) serves big clinical labs and genome centres and is about two-thirds of sequencing consumables.
Installed base
The number of Illumina instruments in active use: about 21,000 at end-2025, including about 890 NovaSeq X and 1,360 NovaSeq 6000.
Placement
An instrument newly installed at a customer, whether sold, leased or rented.
Pull-through
Annual consumables revenue per installed instrument. About $1.35M for a NovaSeq X, $0.6–0.67M for a NovaSeq 6000, $80–100K for a NextSeq 1000/2000.
NovaSeq X transition
Customers moving work from the NovaSeq 6000 to the NovaSeq X, which reads the same DNA for roughly a third of the price per gigabase. It cut revenue per unit of data in 2023–2026.
"The 6000 tail"
The work still running on NovaSeq 6000s: about 17% of high-throughput gigabases but about 41% of high-throughput consumables revenue in Q2'26. Mostly clinical tests that are hard to move.
Reagent rental
Placing an instrument cheaply or free in exchange for a multi-year commitment to buy consumables.
Open offer
Illumina's standard multi-year supply terms for oncology customers, created during the GRAIL merger fight. They cap price increases, give most-favoured pricing and lock in no minimum volumes.
DRAGEN
Illumina's software for turning raw sequencer output into a list of genetic variants ("secondary analysis").
TSO 500 / TSO Comprehensive
Illumina's 500-gene tumour profiling kit and its FDA-approved version, Illumina's main own clinical test product.
TruPath (formerly Constellation)
Illumina's "synthetic long read" whole genome, launched March 2026 at $395 per genome, which recovers long-range information from short reads with ten-minute prep.
StrataMap
Illumina's sequencing-based spatial product, launched June 2026.
PIPseq (Fluent)
Illumina's instrument-free single-cell prep, from the 2024 Fluent BioSciences purchase.
BioInsight, Billion Cell Atlas
Illumina's data-and-AI unit, launched late 2025. Its first product is a giant gene-perturbation single-cell dataset sold to drug companies; revenue sits in "sequencing service and other".
SomaLogic, SomaScan, Protein Prep
The proteomics business Illumina bought for $363M net cash in January 2026. Its revenue sits mostly in the microarray line.
Microarrays
An older chip technology that checks for a preset list of known DNA variants. Cheaper but less complete than sequencing; about 8% of revenue and shrinking.
Greater China / ROW
Greater China (mainland, Hong Kong, Taiwan) is shrinking after China blacklisted Illumina in February 2025. "ROW" (rest of world) means everything outside it; management's preferred growth metric is ROW organic growth.
Customers and clinical testing
Clinical vs Research & Applied
Clinical customers test patients (cancer, inherited disease, prenatal screening) and are about 65% of sequencing consumables. Research & Applied customers are universities, government labs, drug companies and agriculture (about 35%). Illumina defines "clinical" broadly; it includes clinical research.
LDT
Laboratory-developed test: a test designed and validated by one lab, as most large clinical labs do, rather than an FDA-approved kit.
CLIA / CAP
The US certification and accreditation every clinical lab needs. Changing the sequencer under a validated test means revalidating it.
Liquid biopsy, ctDNA
A blood test that finds cancer from tumour DNA fragments (ctDNA) floating in the blood.
CGP
Comprehensive genomic profiling: a large-panel tumour test to pick a cancer drug ("therapy selection").
MRD
Minimal residual disease testing: repeated blood tests after treatment to catch cancer returning. Tested three to four times a year per patient, so it is a recurring, sequencing-heavy use.
MCED
Multi-cancer early detection: blood tests that screen healthy people for many cancers at once (for example GRAIL's Galleri).
NIPT
Non-invasive prenatal testing: a pregnant person's blood test that screens fetal DNA. Already used in about 70–80% of US pregnancies.
Reimbursement, PAMA, CLFS
Payment by insurers for a test. PAMA is the US law that resets Medicare lab prices; the Clinical Laboratory Fee Schedule (CLFS) is the price list. CMS proposed cutting genomic-sequencing codes about 23% for 2027, capped at 15% a year.
Finance and valuation
Organic growth
Growth excluding currency moves and acquisitions. "ROW organic" also excludes Greater China.
Non-GAAP / adjusted
Management's figures excluding acquisition amortisation, restructuring, investment gains and similar items. Illumina's non-GAAP still includes stock-based compensation.
Gross margin, operating (EBIT) margin
Revenue left after the direct cost of making products, and after all operating costs (R&D, sales, admin), each as a share of revenue.
Incremental margin
Change in profit divided by change in revenue between two periods: what each extra dollar of sales keeps.
Free cash flow (FCF)
Operating cash flow minus capital spending.
Consensus ("the Street")
The average forecast of the sell-side analysts who cover the stock. Here, the Bloomberg printout dated 10 October 2026.
EV, EV/EBITDA, P/E, PEG
Enterprise value (market value plus net debt); EV divided by operating cash earnings; share price divided by earnings per share; and P/E divided by the EPS growth rate.
Reverse DCF
Working backwards from today's share price to the growth and margins a discounted-cash-flow model would need to justify it.
LSD / MSD / HSD / DD
Low-, mid- and high-single-digit and double-digit percentage growth (roughly 1–3%, 4–6%, 7–9%, 10%+).
53-week year
Illumina's fiscal 2026 has an extra week (ending 3 January 2027). Management says it falls in Q4 and adds about 0.5 point of revenue growth; Bloomberg's period dates imply Q3.

1The business model and every way Illumina makes money

Illumina is a razor-and-blades business. It sells sequencers, then earns most of its money for years afterwards from the consumables every run requires. Roughly three-quarters of revenue is consumables, about nine-tenths is recurring, and almost all of the value sits in one product family: the NovaSeq high-throughput sequencers and their flow cells.

1.1 How a dollar reaches Illumina

Picture a large US cancer-testing lab such as Natera or Guardant. It buys or leases a NovaSeq X for roughly $1 million, often with a volume discount when it takes five or ten at a time MGMT. That instrument then generates about $1.35 million a year of consumables purchases (flow cells and reagent kits), for five to six years DMGMT. Over its life a single X is therefore worth roughly $7–8 million of consumables to Illumina, about seven times the box price Der. Illumina also sells a service contract on the box and often the library-prep kits and analysis software that feed it.

What the lab is really buying is gigabases of DNA read. A 30X human genome needs about 100 Gb. On the largest NovaSeq X flow cell, a government-channel price list implies Illumina receives about $2.30–3.00 per Gb S, or $230–300 per genome; big customers pay less after discounts, and Illumina's own launch claim was $2.00 per Gb (about $200 per genome) MGMT. For the same work on the older NovaSeq 6000, Illumina earned roughly three times as much per gigabase Der. That gap, and the fact that a minority of work still runs on the older machine, is the centre of the "over-earning" debate in section 5.

The lab's own economics explain why the price of sequencing is no longer the main lever. Sequencing a genome costs the lab perhaps $150–250. Sample preparation adds under $100 and data analysis and interpretation add $500–1,000 for a clinical case X. So a 20% cut in sequencing price lowers a clinical lab's total cost per test by only about 5% X. That is why each new round of price cuts now produces less extra volume than it did a decade ago, and why management has shifted its pitch from "cost per gigabase" to "total cost of workflow" MGMT.

1.4 What drives monetisation, in order of weight

  1. High-throughput consumables volume and price. Gigabases sequenced grew more than 30% a year for eight straight quarters D, but consumables revenue grew only 2–5% because the average price per gigabase fell with the shift to the X. Everything in the forecast depends on that gap closing.
  2. The clinical mix. Clinical consumables outside China grew 20%, 20% and then 15% in the last three reported quarters D. Clinical volume is driven by test volumes at roughly 10–20 large US labs, the amount of sequencing per test, and reimbursement.
  3. The research cycle. Research and applied consumables fell 7% in FY25 and 12% and 7% in Q1 and Q2'26 D, on US funding cuts and on its own completed move to the cheaper X. It is about 35% of sequencing consumables, so stabilisation is worth several points of growth.
  4. Placements. Each X placement feeds consumables six to nine months later MGMT. Record placements in Q4'25 to Q2'26 are the reason the Street expects consumables growth to accelerate into 2027.
  5. Margins. Gross margin of about 68% and operating margin of about 23% are now set mainly by mix (consumables vs instruments), tariffs and memory-chip costs, and cost programmes, not by price, at least so far.

2Segments: mix and margin, last six quarters

Illumina reports one segment, so it does not publish a gross or operating margin for any part of the business. What it does disclose is revenue by product line, growth by customer type, the NovaSeq X's share of high-throughput volume and revenue, and the gross margin of "product" versus "service" revenue. How the segment margins are built

Gross margin. The 10-Qs disclose the cost of product revenue (consumables plus instruments) and of service revenue. That gives a disclosed product gross margin and service gross margin each quarter Der. To split product into consumables and instruments we assume instruments earn about a 35% gross margin, consistent with management repeatedly calling instruments "lower-margin" and with industry norms; consumables gross margin then falls out of the arithmetic at about 75–77% every quarter Der. Moving the instrument assumption between 25% and 45% moves the consumables estimate by only about ±1.5 points.

4.2 Eight things the calls agree on

  1. Clinical customers are very sticky. Revalidating a clinical test costs $100,000–200,000 at a small lab and up to $200 million for an FDA-approved test; trials cannot switch platforms midway; converting an established lab takes four to five years X.
  2. Rivals are added beside Illumina, not instead of it. A core adds "one Element, Ultima or AXELIOS versus maybe six NovaSeq Xes" X. Share loss comes from new labs, new tests, new projects and contract renewals.
  3. The NovaSeq 6000 base is the most exposed pocket. Several experts independently said rivals win where a lab is still on a 6000, because the price gap is largest there X.
  4. Price per gigabase keeps falling, and cuts no longer buy as much volume. Historically a 4× price cut brought 5–10× more sequencing; now halving the price brings perhaps 25% more X.
  5. Clinical growth is concentrated in 10–20 large labs that have the margins and the incentive to cut sequencing costs X.
  6. Research demand is weak and US funding is the swing factor, with one genome centre turning more pessimistic about 2027 X.
  7. Roche is a real but slow threat. Views range from "die out" to 10–20% share in three years; most experts expect little impact in 2026–27 and more pressure later X.
  8. Customers have barely touched the multiomics products. No interviewed customer reported buying SomaLogic, StrataMap or PIPseq; TruPath intent ranged from 0% to 6% of a lab's volume X.

4.3 Where they disagree

Market share. Estimates range from 95% (a loose guess) to 80%, 70% and the mid-60s, and nobody defines whether share means installed boxes, gigabases or dollars. Whether elasticity is dead. The former product director says cheaper sequencing barely matters now; academic cores and rare-disease labs say whole genomes became routine precisely because cost fell. Reagent rental. Two experts say Illumina never offered it until recently; a Roche employee says "that's what Illumina used to do." The filings disclose almost nothing on it.

4.4 What the calls say about the three Illumina questions

Clinical volume. No call quantifies it. The calls do test management's explanations: experts agree that reimbursement and ordering behaviour, not sequencing price, limit oncology volume; that NIPT is near saturation at 70–80% of US pregnancies; and that the growth is in MRD (tested three to four times a year) and in moving tests from exome to genome.

Over-earning. The calls support the idea that the 6000 tail converts slowly and that rivals target it, but no expert gave its size or price. One former employee's comment that large clinical labs pay "half or even a quarter" of a small lab's price suggests the tail is not earning the full list-price premium.

Multiomics. The calls give no purchase evidence for the 1–2 points. They describe interest from large academic centres (PIPseq, 5-base) and a sequencing-based spatial market that is "not more than maybe low eight figures" for Illumina today X.

5The three Illumina questions, and what the buy side is implying

These are the four questions the stock turns on. For each one we give the answer first, then the evidence, then what would change our mind. Where the answer is our own judgment rather than a disclosed fact, we say so.

5.1 What is the true go-forward growth in clinical sequencing volume, and is management's explanation for the slowdown right?

Answer

Clinical sequencing volume is still growing fast: we estimate about 35–50% a year in gigabases over the past year, centred near 42%, and we expect roughly 25–35% in 2027. But clinical consumables revenue is decelerating, from +20% to +15% in Q2'26 and, on management's own full-year guide, to roughly +13% in the second half. Management's explanation (a temporary Middle East and Latin America disruption) is true but incomplete. The bigger reasons are tougher comparisons in the second half, the continuing move of clinical work to the cheaper NovaSeq X, and a narrowing base of very large customers. We expect clinical consumables revenue to grow in the low teens in 2027, not the mid-teens the bulls assume.

Volume and revenue are different things

Illumina reports two kinds of growth, and the gap between them is the whole story. Volume is measured in gigabases (Gb), billions of DNA letters read. Management says gigabases sequenced on its connected high- and mid-throughput instruments grew more than 30% year over year in every quarter since Q3'25, and that clinical volume grew "well above that" in both Q1 and Q2'26 D. At the January JPM conference the CEO was more specific: clinical volume growth is "above 30%" and should stay there "for a while", while the whole business should settle at about 25% MGMT. Revenue grows much more slowly because the price Illumina earns per gigabase keeps falling as customers move from the NovaSeq 6000 to the NovaSeq X, which sells a gigabase for roughly a third of the price.

Illumina does not publish a clinical gigabase number. We can back one out. Clinical consumables revenue outside China grew 15–20% in the last three quarters. If revenue per clinical gigabase fell about 20–28% (the range implied by the X's share of clinical work rising from about 64% to 78% over the year), clinical gigabases must have grown about 35–50% SDer. A second check, built from the gigabase and revenue shares of the X and the 6000, says total high-throughput gigabases probably grew 33–45% in Q2'26, because any lower figure would require the 6000's price per gigabase to have risen, which its falling list prices rule out S.

What the customers are doing

The bottom-up evidence comes from Illumina's largest clinical customers, who report test volumes every quarter. In Q2'26 Natera's MRD tests (minimal residual disease, blood tests that look for cancer coming back) grew 56%, Guardant's oncology tests 63%, GeneDx's exomes and genomes 32%, Tempus's oncology tests 31%, Caris's cases 18% and BillionToOne's tests 35%, while Myriad fell 1% S. Test counts are not gigabases, because tests use very different amounts of sequencing. A prenatal screen (NIPT) uses about 1–2 Gb, an exome about 8 Gb, a tissue tumour panel about 15 Gb, and a 30X whole genome or a liquid-biopsy panel 100–120 Gb or more. Natera's amplicon-based MRD test uses less than 1 Gb per blood draw S. Weighting the Q2'26 test growth rates by gigabases rather than by revenue gives clinical gigabase growth of about 31–34% S, a little below the revenue-implied range but consistent with management's "above 30%".

Two structural drivers support continued high volume growth. First, intensity: moving a rare-disease case from an exome to a whole genome uses about 15 times more sequencing, and MRD patients are tested three or four times a year rather than once MGMTX. Second, penetration: management's slides put comprehensive tumour profiling, MRD, multi-cancer early detection and genetic-disease testing each at less than 20% of their eventual market MGMT. Against that, several drivers are maturing. Experts say NIPT already reaches 70–80% of US pregnancies and tissue tumour profiling is "quite saturated" X. Whole-genome testing is still "much, much smaller" than exome testing, so the 15-fold intensity jump applies to a small base today MGMT. And J.P. Morgan's June survey of 32 clinical labs that use Illumina expects only +15% volume growth in 2027 and +19% in 2028, measured in runs and samples rather than gigabases E: JPM.

Is management's explanation for the Q2 slowdown right?

On the Q2'26 call the CEO explained the drop from +20% to +15% as "some in and outs" in the Middle East and Latin America, with US clinical still growing faster than 20% MGMT. The regional data back the claim: in Q2'26 the US and Canada grew 13% organically while Europe, the Middle East, Africa and Latin America were flat D. So the explanation is accurate. It is not complete, for three reasons.

  1. The full-year guide already assumes further slowing. Management raised the 2026 clinical consumables guide to "mid-teens". The first half grew about 17.5%. For the year to land at 14–16%, the second half must grow about 11–15%, centred near 13% Der. That is a guided deceleration, not a one-off.
  2. The comparisons get harder. Clinical consumables grew 8%, 10%, 12% and 20% in the four quarters of 2025 D. Q1 and Q2'26 were measured against the two easiest quarters; Q4'26 is measured against +20%. Even on a two-year basis, growth slowed from 28% in Q1'26 to 25% in Q2'26 Der.
  3. The price transition is still running in clinical. Clinical work on the X rose from 76% to 78% of volume between Q1 and Q2'26 and is targeted at 80–85% by year-end D. Every point of conversion lowers revenue per gigabase. The research market, which finished converting a year earlier, shows the pattern: its revenue fell for most of 2025 while its gigabases kept growing.

Management also points to the 6–9 month lag before a newly placed X reaches normal consumption, saying "most of that benefit will come in 2027" MGMT. That is fair: placements of more than 80 and more than 95 X systems in Q1 and Q2'26, about 70% to clinical labs, have not yet shown up fully in consumables. Our rough estimate is that the 2026 placements could add about $300M a year of consumables once fully used, about 10% of the line, before price effects E: 03 B5d. That is the best argument that clinical revenue growth can hold up in 2027.

Two risks that management did not mention

First, reimbursement. On 21 September 2026 the US Medicare agency (CMS) proposed cutting 2027 payment rates for generic genomic-sequencing codes by about 23%, phased in at no more than 15% a year from 2027 to 2029 S. Branded tests such as Natera's Signatera are mostly on separate, protected codes, so the direct hit falls on hereditary and exome panels. The indirect effect is that clinical labs will push harder on their sequencing costs. Second, concentration. Roughly 10–20 large US labs drive most clinical volume X. Their own growth rates are high but will slow as they get bigger, and every one of them has a strong incentive to negotiate down the price of sequencing.

What would change our view. A Q3'26 clinical growth rate of 15% or more, against a tougher +12% comparison, would show the Middle East explanation was the whole story. A rate below 12% would show the deceleration is structural.

5.2 Is Illumina over-earning, and what happens to consumables pricing as the last 17% of volume leaves the NovaSeq 6000?

Answer

Yes, in a measurable sense. About $0.9B a year of revenue, roughly 28% of sequencing consumables, comes from NovaSeq 6000 work that earns about 3.4 times what the same gigabase earns on the X. If all of that work moved to the X tomorrow, high-throughput consumables revenue would fall about 29%. That will not happen quickly: perhaps a fifth of the work never moves, regulated tests are anchored to the 6000, and customers are still buying new 6000s. We expect the tail to shrink by about 4–6 points of high-throughput gigabase share in 2027, which costs roughly $90–130M of revenue that year before volume growth. The larger pricing risk in 2027–28 is no longer the 6000 tail. It is the price of the X itself: the 35B flow cell, contracts that only let prices fall, and competitive quotes from Roche, Element and Ultima.

Sizing the over-earning pool

Management discloses two numbers each quarter: the X's share of high-throughput gigabases and its share of high-throughput consumables revenue. In Q2'26 the X carried 83% of gigabases but only 59% of revenue. So the remaining 17% of gigabases, almost all on the NovaSeq 6000, earned 41% of revenue D. Dividing one ratio by the other, a gigabase on the 6000 earns about 3.4 times what it earns on the X Der. This is not a pure price comparison: it mixes list prices, flow-cell sizes (small flow cells cost more per gigabase) and discounts. The CFO's own 2024 estimate was that the move from 6000 to X cut the price per gigabase by "easily more than 60% to 70%" MGMT, which is consistent with a ratio near three.

The chart shows how the two shares have moved. The persistent gap of about 24 points between them is the over-earning. Note that the two lines rose by about the same number of points (+15 and +16). That is not a sign of stable pricing: if the X's price relative to the 6000 had held at its Q1'25 level, an 83% gigabase share would have produced about a 63% revenue share, not 59%. The X's implied price per gigabase relative to the 6000 fell from about 0.36 to about 0.30 over the period Der, consistent with Barclays' observation that the X's revenue share lagged its volume share.

Forward pricing on the X itself

Once the 6000 tail is small, the price of the X becomes the variable that matters. Five forces push it down:

  • The 35B flow cell, due in the first half of 2027, should lower the cost per gigabase by about 20% for the largest users XE: UBS. Management has refused to discuss its price: "we are not ready at this point to go out and talk about pricing" MGMT.
  • Yield improvements on existing flow cells. Illumina's own roadmap quotes a customer saying usable output per X run rose about 30% since purchase; at an unchanged flow-cell price that is a 23% cut in price per gigabase that never appears as a list-price change S.
  • Contracts that can only move down. Natera's agreement freezes prices and caps the price per gigabase on new flow cells. Caris's agreement, written on the "open offer" Illumina made to win approval for the GRAIL deal, includes a most-favoured-customer clause: if Illumina gives a comparable customer a better price, it must refund Caris the difference on units already bought S.
  • Competitive quotes as leverage. Large academic centres negotiate "insane" discounts using rival quotes, and Illumina now gives away instruments, automation and software at threatened sites X.
  • Reagent rental and volume discounts. A small but rising share of X placements are leased or rented, with the instrument's cost recovered through higher consumables prices, and multi-unit orders get volume discounts MGMT.

One more data point argues the X's price is already slipping. Our analysis of the Q2'25 and Q2'26 share data shows the X's revenue per gigabase fell about 10% year over year if total gigabases grew 30%, and about 16% if they grew 40% S. Barclays made the same point in May: the X's revenue share stayed flat at 55% in Q1'26 while its gigabase share rose five points E: Barclays. Management called that comparison "a frivolous exercise" because flow-cell mix moves it MGMT.

Our forward view. Revenue per high-throughput gigabase fell about 19% in the last year. We expect about 12–18% in 2027: the mix effect from the 6000 shrinks, but within-X price declines from the 35B and contracts take its place Der. With clinical gigabases growing 25–35%, that supports clinical consumables revenue growth of roughly 8–16%, centred in the low teens.

What would change our view. In the Q3'26 deck, a derived 6000 revenue figure below $210M a quarter and an X revenue share above 60%, followed in February 2027 by a disclosed FY26 X pull-through of $1.35M or more, would mean the tail is converting without hurting revenue. The reverse would mean the drag is arriving faster than we assume.

5.3 Does multiomics uptake support the "1–2 points of growth" bridge, or is it a plug?

Answer

The 1–2 points look like a reasonable but unevidenced assumption, not a demonstrated number and not an outright plug. Our bottom-up estimate gives $21M, $59M or $134M of new-product revenue in 2027 (low, base, high), which is 0.45, 1.3 or 2.9 points of growth. Only about $31M (0.7 point) rests on disclosed evidence. The figure is "plug-like" in one specific sense: the high-single-digit 2027 target needs roughly 1–2 points on top of a core business growing 5–6%, and management has chosen not to disclose the pieces. Importantly, consensus does not need it.

What management has promised

"Multiomics" here means measuring other biological molecules (proteins, RNA in single cells, tissue location, DNA methylation) using Illumina's sequencers as the readout. The promise has evolved. On a $4.62B base, 1–2 points is $46–92M of extra revenue in 2027 Der. Management has never said whether SomaLogic's growth after its first anniversary (February 2027) counts toward that figure.

    GRAIL royalties restart at the end of 2026. GRAIL's filings show its royalty to Illumina (9%, falling to a 7% floor with offsets) was suspended until 24 December 2026. It then resumes. GRAIL's revenue is small today, so this is a few million dollars a year unless the multi-cancer test wins Medicare coverage, which a House committee advanced 43–0 in September. Illumina also owns about 14.5% of GRAIL. Separately, the GRAIL supply agreement's first non-renewal deadline falls around 31 October 2026.

How the list nets out

Near term (Q3 and Q4'26) the evidence favours upside to consensus: a long beat record, a possibly under-modelled extra week and strong placements. Medium term (2027–28) it favours downside: consensus assumes consumables accelerate and gross margin expands while price per gigabase falls more slowly than it has. That combination, a likely beat followed by a cautious first 2027 guide, is the same pattern the stock showed in February 2026, when a strong Q4'25 beat met a conservative 2026 guide and the shares fell 10.4%.

9.3 How competition turns into lower prices

The important point for the model is that competition shows up in Illumina's price long before it shows up in share. There are six channels, roughly in order of size so far.

  1. Illumina competing with itself. The biggest price cut of the last three years was Illumina's own: the NovaSeq X cut the price per gigabase by 60–70% versus the 6000, a pre-emptive move against Ultima, Element and MGI. Every gigabase that moves from the 6000 to the X is a price cut (section 5.2).
  2. Rival quotes as leverage. Large academic centres and clinical labs use competing quotes to negotiate. An academic expert described "insane" discounts at the largest centres; another said promised discounts were "not honored", which pushed work to Novogene and Ultima X.
  3. Contracts that only let prices fall. The supply agreements of Natera (to August 2033) and Caris (to August 2033) cap or freeze prices and, in Caris's case, require Illumina to refund the difference if it gives a comparable customer a better price. Guardant (to about January 2033) and Caris can leave on 90 days' notice and have no minimum purchases S. These terms mean a price cut won to fend off a rival at one large lab spreads to others.
  4. Bundles, giveaways and reagent rental. At threatened sites Illumina now gives away instruments, automation and software; a small share of X placements are on reagent rental, where the instrument is paid for through higher consumables prices XMGMT. JPM's survey found a reagent-rental offer would make 37–39% of respondents less likely to buy AXELIOS E: JPM.
  5. Application-specific pricing. Since 2024 Illumina prices by application and region rather than one global list. "Counting" applications (RNA expression and single-cell work, where many short reads are counted rather than assembled), about 14–16% of sequencing consumables, are the most exposed: Illumina's 25B list is about $0.68 per million reads against Roche's $0.06 in high-output mode E: UBS.
  6. New Illumina flow cells and yield upgrades. The 35B flow cell (about −20% per gigabase) and software that squeezes more output from existing flow cells lower price per gigabase without a headline cut S.

9.4 Why share moves slowly

Customers have strong reasons not to switch, especially in the clinic. Each reason is a moat around a specific part of the revenue.

  • Revalidation cost. Re-validating a clinical test on a new sequencer costs $100,000–200,000 at a small lab and up to $200M for an FDA-approved test; MRD trials last 5–7 years and cannot change platform midway X.
  • Regulatory status. Illumina has diagnostic-grade (IVD) versions of its 6000 and NextSeq 550; we found no rival high-throughput IVD system S.
  • Data quality and workflow. Ultima's own clinical users report weaker accuracy in long runs of the same DNA letter (homopolymers) S. Roche's amplification-free workflow needs 150–500 ng of DNA, which many patient samples cannot provide XS. Illumina's DRAGEN software and partner ecosystem (10x Genomics, Twist, Agilent kits) are built around its sequencers.
  • Labs add, rather than replace. A core typically adds "one Element, Ultima or AXELIOS" beside several NovaSeq Xs X. Share is lost through new labs, new tests and contract renewals, not through decommissioning.
  • Sequencing is a small share of the clinical test's cost. At $150–250 of sequencing within a $600–1,100 total cost per test, a 20% cheaper sequencer saves about 5% X.

9.5 Roche's AXELIOS: the threat that matters most

Roche is the first rival with a big balance sheet, a global diagnostics sales force and existing hospital relationships. It priced AXELIOS on 26 February 2026 at $150 per genome with no volume discounts; Illumina's stock rose 10% over two days because investors had feared $100 E: JPM. It launched for research use on 29 June with a target of about 100 systems in the first year, about a third placed by early September, and a ">$1B" ambition E: JPM.

The evidence on how fast it bites is mixed. In J.P. Morgan's June survey, 40% of customers had not heard of AXELIOS; among those who had, only 8% would consider buying in the next 6–12 months (mostly clinical), 35% in 12–24 months (mostly academic) and 32% after two years. If they bought one, 65% would reduce their Illumina use and about 11% would decommission at least one Illumina instrument E: JPM. Experts range from "will just simply die out" to taking 10–20% share in three years; a former Illumina product director thought Roche would win "10, 15 tops" of 100 instruments bought in 2027; a Natera scientist called it "the main disruptor" but noted its input-DNA problem X. Single-cell data from a Brigham & Women's core were "very similar" to Illumina's X.

Our read: AXELIOS takes little share in 2026–27 but becomes the reference price in research counting work and the main bargaining chip in clinical contract renewals from 2027. It hits Illumina's price first and its volume later.

Net assessment

Illumina's position in clinical sequencing is genuinely strong and will erode slowly: we expect it to lose perhaps 2–3 points of share a year in dollars, mostly at the margins (new labs, research, mid-throughput). We expect revenue per gigabase to keep falling at 12–18% a year through 2027–28, which is faster than consensus can absorb unless volume growth stays near 30% (section 8.5).

10.4 The reverse DCF: what the price assumes

A discounted cash flow (DCF) model values a company as the present value of all its future free cash flow. 13Caveats and open items

The conclusions above rest on some estimates that Illumina does not disclose and on sources that disagree. These are the ones that could change an answer.

  1. Segment margins are our estimates. Illumina reports one segment. Consumables and instrument margins assume a ~35% instrument gross margin; segment EBIT is an illustrative allocation (section 2).
  2. The X/6000 dollar split is derived. It assumes high-throughput is about 69% of sequencing consumables, which an installed-base cross-check supports to within about 1%. Integer-rounded share disclosures limit precision: the 3.4x price ratio is known only to about ±10%.
  3. Clinical gigabase growth is inferred. Illumina publishes no clinical gigabase figure. Our 35–50% trailing range depends on the assumed clinical price decline.
  4. Which quarter holds the 53rd week. Bloomberg's period dates put it in Q3'26; management says Q4. We follow management. Its size (management ~0.5 point; mechanically up to ~2%) is unresolved.
  5. SomaLogic's reporting line. The earnings decks book it mostly in microarrays; the 10-Q puts it in service and other revenue.
  6. The clinical and research growth rates do not reconcile exactly to total sequencing consumables growth (they imply about 1 point more). Either the bases differ or price within each segment is worse than shown.
  7. The GRAIL open-offer expiry (~August 2027) is unverified. Illumina's terms page gives a start date and no end date; the 2027 date is inferred from the GRAIL separation documents.
  8. Sell-side models are stale. The newest full broker model in the folder is from 5 August; the stock is up about 36% since. Target changes after that date come from web aggregators, without the notes' reasoning.
  9. Peer multiples mix bases. Peer EV/EBITDA is trailing and GAAP-style; Illumina's is forward and adjusted. We lean on P/E and EV/sales.
  10. Expert calls are mostly July–August 2026, several from former employees who left one to twelve years ago, and six from competitors.
  11. Market share figures are weak. The only survey is from 2023; the rest are expert estimates with undefined bases.
  12. Net debt ranges from $0.33B to $1.36B depending on source and date; it moves enterprise value by about 1% and changes no conclusion.
  13. Unexplained moves. About a quarter of the stock's 7%-plus days since 2023 have no identifiable cause.
