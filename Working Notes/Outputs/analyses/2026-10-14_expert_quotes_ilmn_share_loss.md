# Expert quotes saying Illumina will keep losing share

*Prepared 14 Oct 2026. Sources: all 23 expert calls in `Sources/ILMN/expert_call/` (Jul–Oct 2026), read in full. Every quote was checked against the original transcript. "[...]" marks text cut from inside a single answer. Timestamps are the transcript's own.*

## Bottom line

Six of the 23 experts say plainly that Illumina will keep losing share. Only two give numbers.

- **A former Illumina field application scientist** says Illumina has already fallen from about 80% to 70% and will lose "at least 30%" more by 2029–30.
- **A former Illumina product-management associate director**, citing DeciBio data, says share fell from about 80% to the mid-60s at about 3 points a year. He thinks it will "flatline" near 50%.

The other four say the same without numbers:

- a Roche UK precision-medicine lead;
- a Natera manager;
- a Brigham and Women's single-cell specialist;
- a former Illumina bioinformatician.

Almost every bearish expert adds the same caveat: the loss is gradual. It comes from new labs, research cores and new projects, while validated clinical tests stay on Illumina. Several of the strongest voices also work for competitors or left Illumina one to two years ago. The full set of caveats is at the end.

"Market share" is never defined in these calls, whether as installed base, revenue or sequencing volume. No expert cites an audited source.

## 1. The strongest quotes, with numbers

### Former Illumina field application scientist, now at SOPHiA GENETICS (24 Jul 2026)

He left Illumina in October 2024. SOPHiA partners with Element.

The interviewer asked what percentage of Illumina's share is at risk "in the next couple of years." The interviewer did not supply a figure.

> "Next couple of years, I think, what I see right now, Illumina already has or had 80% of the market. 80%+ a bit. I think Illumina is going to lose, from my perspective, from the field, at least 30%, if not even more sometimes. At this moment, if I'm going to do some predictions to 2029 or 2030, at this moment, Illumina already lost 10% of the market. They are losing the market. They used to be 80%. Now they're at 70%, and their earnings will speak of it. Their earnings are achieved by layoffs and other things. At the end of the day, the earnings speak of it, and the layoffs wave.
>
> Illumina is at risk, and at this moment, I would say for the next four to five years, it's around 30%. 30% Illumina is going to lose, and that 30% will be divided by others. Also, let's not forget the long reads players, like Oxford Nanopore. Illumina did the Illumina Complete Long Reads, and it failed. It failed drastically. Nobody wanted Illumina Complete Long Reads. Nobody." (00:48:16)

> "Number one, of course, is Illumina at this point. Illumina is losing many sites." (00:38:54)

On Element's new high-throughput sequencer:

> "Now, Element is moving customers from the X Plus to VITARI. [...] What they did, they brought VITARI to the game, and the cost is still half of the NovaSeq X. In this scenario, when you have the cost half of the NovaSeq X, and you can still fit the same number of samples where you are fitting on the X Plus, then here becomes a good game between NovaSeq X Plus and VITARI. Now you have a game."

On Ultima:

> "Ultima provides you the accuracy and provides you a good price. That's why some of the population genome projects are secretly validating Ultima instruments without telling Illumina, and I am working with one of them, to be honest with you."

How to weigh it:

- **The arithmetic is loose.** He says "at least 30%" and "around 30%," and moves between "next couple of years" and "four to five years." It is unclear whether 30% means 30 points off 80% or 30% of today's 70%.
- **He also argues the other way.** In the same call he says Element "will lose" at sites that already own an X Plus. He also says customers mostly use challengers to push Illumina's price down rather than switch.

### Former Illumina associate director, product management (24 Jul 2026)

He left Illumina in July 2025. This is the same person as the 23 Jul call below.

> "At the beginning there, when these new technologies were coming on the market, I think we were seeing that Illumina's ability to charge a price premium was very much being threatened, and we observed very quickly the market share fall from probably around 80% down now into the mid-60s percent and just continuously eroding over time. That was due to a number of factors. As people were looking to replace their existing Illumina instruments, they were finding that the competitors were offering them, one) better prices, better promos on the instruments themselves. two) better consumables pricing, either through contracts or even through retail prices, that Illumina had a really hard time price matching. Element, for example, it would have drop Illumina's consumable margins to below 50% in some cases, which was just not something that they had an appetite for. three) some of the technologies, to be honest, were just, and still are, very competitive and in some cases, better." (00:01:53)

The interviewer then asked where share would be "in a year or two or three." The interviewer misquoted the expert as having said "80% to 60%."

> "It's an interesting question because we were seeing erosion, and I think even DeciBio was reporting on this, too, and we were using them for a lot of our underlying data. Illumina was eroding at like three pps a year. That wasn't going to continue. It was going to level out at some point. I think around 50% is going to be a reasonable number to flatline at. At that point, everyone who wants to leave or has a good reason to leave will have left.
>
> This is all assuming, of course, that Illumina does continue on their current trajectory of bolstering their multi-omics offerings, of continuing to make iterative product improvements to keep pace with everyone else. I feel like 50% is a reasonable floor that will then just meander around there." (00:09:22)

> "I feel the commoditization is out of their hands because all of these other competitors now offer such low pricing. [...] there just is no reason to choose Illumina sequencers in most cases anymore, especially if you are a new customer starting out. Other options are going to give you data quality, functionality, and reliability that are on par. It feels like a race to the bottom right now in terms of pricing." (00:08:42)

How to weigh it:

- **The pace is historical.** About 3 points a year is the pace he saw, and he says it "wasn't going to continue."
- **The timeline is implied.** He gives no date. At about 3 points a year, mid-60s to 50% would take about four to five years. That is our arithmetic, not his.
- **There is a clinical floor.** In the same call he says moving an FDA-approved test is "incredibly hard." He calls Illumina's clinical first-mover advantage "a huge advantage that other companies, I honestly do not know how they're going to ever address."

## 2. Strong statements without numbers

### Roche precision medicine and genomics lead, UK (29 Jul 2026)

He works for Roche and previously worked at Thermo Fisher, both Illumina competitors. The interviewer opened with "market share is dropping from what I hear from other experts."

> "Definitely. I think, absolutely. In the past and even currently, Illumina is definitely the gold standard. They've had the dominant market share, but it definitely is being eroded. [...] As your Ultima Genomics, they're looking at massive population scale genomics, and they're driving down the cost at that high volume end. It's going to really impact Illumina's growth in that space, and that's again targeting clinical labs that are doing high throughput work. The other end is your Element Biosciences, where they're looking at more of the mid throughput labs [...] They're going to start tapping into that space of Illumina as well. [...] I think they're going to struggle to retain the dominance that they have had over the past few years because of that. I think each company that they're up against is bringing a unique feature. I think it's going to be hard for Illumina to address that across the board with the technologies that they currently have." (00:02:39)

On technology transfer in the UK, where a test company trains an NHS lab to run its test on the company's chosen equipment:

> "That's where potentially Illumina could have some erosion if these other companies, like Natera, start to validate their tests on other sequencing instruments." (00:11:40)

> "There is validation ongoing with some of the other platforms. Whether they'll be successful or not is still up in the air. That validation is still ongoing. [...] As soon as it's validated, the labs that have adopted the technology transfer would then have to change their instrumentation from Illumina to whatever the new platform would be." (00:14:32)

> "It will end up definitely happening. Illumina, if they don't keep up and they can't be competitive in that space, they will lose out to some of the other competitors coming through. I think we'll see that definitely over the next five to 10 years." (00:18:08)

### Natera associate director, oncology lifecycle management (22 Jul 2026)

Natera is one of Illumina's largest clinical customers.

> "Overall, I think their future is good. Of course, they're going to lose market share, but they have adopted first and quickly, and many of the big players, they believe on the technology, and they have established their success through Illumina, that this change and that loss of market share, it will be slow. They will lose, but how much, it's not going to be in six months, they will lose 30%. It will happen gradually and gradually, so they will have time to catch up." (00:47:09)

The "30% in six months" is an example of what will not happen, not a forecast.

> "For those that there's massive market, I do not think that you need the depth and the cost of Illumina. Definitely, they will start losing some market share." (00:06:36)

That quote is about Ultima at about $80–110 a sample in low-depth oncology, prenatal, transplant and local-lab work.

> "I think that would be the main disruptor. I think they will start really, really feeling it very soon with Roche." (00:02:19)

He also says rivals "haven't infiltrated so much" yet. Later in the same call he is "not fully convinced" Roche will "radically change the market."

### Single-cell genomic technology specialist, Brigham and Women's Hospital core (Tegus, undated, about Oct 2026)

She has analyzed Roche AXELIOS data but has not used the machine.

> "Nevertheless, Roche, if they deliver what they promise, they will have a position in the market, in the global market. I personally expect that the market will become more fragmented in the next five years, meaning that Illumina will not be the dominant player anymore. Probably it will not remain the major player. Alternative platforms will become more and more available, including Roche. This is because of the high throughput. You need more data. You need it at lower cost, which is what Roche here gives us."

> "For large biotech or biopharma, we see that they are willing to invest in newer platforms. [...] They are willing to put in more money and they are willing to try new technologies and they are aware that the market is changing and that Illumina will not probably be the major player anymore in the next five, 10 years."

In the same call she says researchers mid-project are unlikely to switch ("I will pay more, but I will not switch"). She also thinks Roche needs "five years. It could take 10 years" to build an ecosystem.

### Former Illumina bioinformatician, now a senior ML scientist in cancer diagnostics (23 Jul 2026)

His Illumina tenure ended about four to five years ago.

> "Naturally, the concern is that how secure is that dominant place, especially recently, they have their patent also expiring. It's definitely looking quite grim for Illumina. I have a few assessments on that point. I think the conclusion is that Illumina is definitely losing grip in this market. I think the take-home message is that it still has a lot of stickiness, especially in established clinical settings, oncology and diagnosis." (00:01:36)

> "With that said, I want to condition my claim on two aspects. One is that, what about the big pharma that is looking to have new product? Those products, it's anybody's game now. This is a very clear trend that you see, is that people are just diversifying. Second is, what about those really agile regions, where it's not about FDA approval, it's about R&D and really fast growing, especially in universities and smaller companies who can pivot much faster. In those regions, we do see there are a lot of more fierce competitions."

His own start-up chose Ultima over Illumina for its lab-developed test. He said Ultima was "2X, 3X cheaper" and had better base quality.

## 3. Roche-specific share estimates

### Oxford Nanopore senior director of strategic product management, at Illumina 2008–14 (Tegus, undated, about Oct 2026)

He brought up the number himself. He says the hard limit is Illumina's concentration in about 10–20 large clinical labs.

> "Hard to say with perfect certainty because there's always shoes left to drop and everything, but I think they could probably pick away 10%-20% relatively easily. It depends on how close to 80/20, the 80/20 rule Illumina is now, again, with their clinical diagnostics mega providers."

> "I don't think of Roche in the next three years as displacing more than that 10% or 20% that I lied with, but thinking out over 10 or longer, I think the potential is there for them to be quite threatening."

"Lied with" is how the transcript has it, probably a mis-transcription. In the same call he says replacing Illumina's installed base within three or four years has no realistic path, and calls switching a lab "a four- or five-year project."

### Former Illumina associate director, product management (23 Jul 2026)

This is the same person as the 24 Jul call. The interviewer framed the question as 100 instruments bought in 2027, and he answered:

> "Maybe 10, 15 tops. Let's stick maybe with 10, because I'm trying to think more broadly about all the other companies too."

That is a share of new instrument purchases, not of Illumina's base. In the same call: "I would be absolutely shocked if they were losing accounts to it."

## 4. Segment-specific loss: research cores, low throughput, adjacent markets

**Contracts in research accounts.** The former Illumina associate director (23 Jul 2026) on large research contracts:

> "I can tell you, those contracts can very quickly become null and void if another company comes in and says, 'Hey, we can undercut Illumina by 25%, 50%.' I did see that happen a number of times where Illumina had to scramble and then would either price match or not, depending on if they thought that it set a bad precedent."

**Georgia academic core director (31 Jul 2026).** This is the only expert describing volume actually lost.

> "I'd say it has been significant. I would say out of the volume we have lost, I would suggest that currently about 80%-90% is being driven to big centers such as Novogene that provide cheaper sequencing. However, the amount of work that is going to Ultima has been increasing. It started maybe two years ago at about 10%, but we are seeing the volume creep up to about 20%."

He expects the Ultima pressure to "Accelerate" (a one-word answer to an either/or question). For the market as a whole, though, he says "I expect Illumina instruments to maintain a current share." The large providers may run Illumina machines themselves, so most of that lost volume may not be lost Illumina share.

**South Carolina academic core director (30 Jul 2026).** He is buying an Element AVITI24.

> "I think the Illumina will lose some share but it won't be a significant loss." Gainers: "I think Element. Maybe Ultima and most probably MGI."

> "I hope that at least 80% of the sequencing we will do in-house with AVITI24."

That covers only his small in-house machines. His outsourced NovaSeq X volume grew about 40%.

**University of Arizona core manager (22 Jul 2026).** She runs Element as her main sequencer.

> "I would expect any lower-output Illumina platforms to lose share." She also says Illumina "has quite a stable share of the marketplace," and that its highest-output systems are gaining.

**Massachusetts genome-center VP (31 Jul 2026).** The center is likely to buy "a couple AXELIOS boxes."

> "I think there won't be major shifts in the coming year, but looking out further, there could be some shift away from Illumina since there are other options now in the market."

**Small-biotech head of synthetic biology (24 Jul 2026)** on NovaSeq-class instruments:

> "continuing to slowly lose traction just because of the competitor options that are available. I do think that it will be slow and probably not extreme."

**Daisy Lab co-founder (23 Jul 2026)** on MGI in New Zealand agriculture. His Illumina knowledge dates to 2021.

> "They're now ditching their very large Illumina sequencers for MGI. That's a major shift." His overall view is that Illumina will "dominate" for about five more years, with its market "cut away a little bit."

**Former Illumina staff scientist (30 Jul 2026)** on a shared market:

> "Illumina is not going to disappear. [...] The problem is that now that pie, they need to share those few pieces because you have all these other individuals or these other entities that are gaining momentum now."

**Former Illumina associate director, now at Kailos (6 Aug 2026)** on clinical concentration:

> "That is an important risk factor for these new competitors to come in with a price point, with a technology, with a use case to say, 'Why don't you start doing this with my sequence and you're just going to cut your cost by half and it's going to be much faster and you're going to get better results,' and you name it, something. It's a significant risk for Illumina business loss."

He himself leans against it happening, since executives who switch and fail "will lose big."

## 5. The counter-quotes

Most of the bearish experts qualify their own view. A sizeable group also expects little loss:

- **LifeStrands lab director (25 Jul):** Illumina has "something like 95% of the market" and "is always going to win."
- **Former Illumina scientist, now at Roche (5 Aug):** "In the clinic, I don't think anyone is really going to move away from Illumina."
- **PacBio segment lead (3 Aug):** "There's just a lot of stickiness around Illumina that's going to make it very difficult for some of the other instruments to gain traction."
- **Former Roche software engineer (27 Jul):** "No, I don't think anybody's going to replace anyone."
- **Kailos expert (6 Aug):** Illumina has "80%+ of installation."
- **JPM survey of 62 customers (10 Jun):** mid-sized clinical labs expect Illumina's share of their work to rise from 50% to 57%.

## How to weigh these quotes

- **Many speakers have reasons to be bearish.** The two experts with numbers are a SOPHiA employee whose employer partners with Element, and a former employee. The Roche UK lead, the Nanopore director and the PacBio lead all work for competitors.
- **Some views are dated.** The field application scientist left Illumina in October 2024. The bioinformatician left four to five years ago. The Daisy Lab expert left his core in 2021.
- **Interviewers often supplied the premise.** Examples are the Roche lead ("market share is dropping"), the "80% to 60%" setup in the 24 Jul call, and the 100-instrument framing for AXELIOS.
- **Two calls are the same person.** The 23 and 24 Jul associate-director calls should count as one voice.
- **The figures disagree, and none has a firm source.** Current share estimates run from the mid-60s to 95%, and only one cites a source (DeciBio).
