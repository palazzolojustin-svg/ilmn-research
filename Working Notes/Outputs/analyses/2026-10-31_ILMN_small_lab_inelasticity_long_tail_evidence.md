# Evidence that smaller customers are volume-inelastic, and that a long tail is still to convert

*2026-10-31. Four agents read every Illumina earnings call and deck (2023 to Q3'26), every investor conference, all 23 expert calls, the sell-side research, the alternative-data scrapes and the peer transcripts. I checked every management quote and the key expert quotes below against the original transcripts. Line numbers refer to files in `Working Notes/Sources/`.*

## The short answer

The case is strong on the existence of a long tail, and mostly inferred on small-lab inelasticity.

- **Long tail.** Management has said it in its own words for two years:
  - "a long tail" (Dhingra, Aug 2024).
  - "around 20% will stay" (Thaysen, Feb 2025).
  - "laggards… a long tail of transition" (Thaysen, Apr 2026).
  - Customers are still buying new 6000s "to remain on that platform for years to come" (Q2'26).
  - Conversion has stalled at 78% of clinical volume for two quarters (Q3'26).
- **A low-volume group that does not convert.** Management's own description of customer groups starts with one: customers who "don't have the volume… So they stay on the 6000" (Thaysen, Wolfe, Nov 2025).
- **Small labs can't fill flow cells and pay more per Gb.**
  - **Can't fill flow cells:** a former CFO said small customers "won't run it at full capacity" (2023). The CEO described "a small… clinical account that might not have the volume to fill up on a high throughput" (2025). Customers and ex-employees say the same in expert calls.
  - **Pay more per Gb:** experts and core-lab price lists show small labs paying 2–10x what the largest accounts pay.
- **Management has no visibility into small labs, and its proof of elasticity excludes them.**
  - The CFO admitted that Illumina has "lesser visibility" into smaller customers (Nov 2024), and that its line of sight on elasticity is "at least for the largest ones".
  - The 40-customer cohort that showed flat year-one revenue included "top-tier accounts". Management then stopped disclosing cohort data (Jan 2026) and will drop the X-transition slide after Q4'26.
- **What is missing.** No source measures a small lab's volume before and after converting. No source splits the remaining 6000 base by lab size. The inelasticity claim rests on three steps:
  1. small labs can't fill the big flow cells;
  2. they already pay the highest prices, so they get the biggest cut when they move;
  3. management's elasticity evidence comes only from large accounts.

  Present it that way in the pitch, not as a measured fact.

## 1. Management quotes (all verified against the transcripts)

### A. Management admits a low-volume group that stays on the 6000

**Jacob Thaysen, CEO, Wolfe Research conference, 19 Nov 2025**
- The question, from Wolfe's analyst: some customers "will never convert. And that's because they don't have the volume" (L314).
- Thaysen's reply (L320):

> "Yes. They don't have the volume. It makes sense for them to stay under 6000. They have good reimbursements level. They are likely even profitable in that assay. So -- and it doesn't make sense for them to make the investment to go over to the X at this point of time where they sit with the volume. So they stay on the 6000. So that's a smaller cohort of customers, at least volume-wise that is staying there."

This is the clearest statement that low-volume customers behave differently. Two cautions:
- The analyst framed the point, and Thaysen agreed with it.
- "smaller… at least volume-wise" means the group is small in Gb. It could still be large in number of customers.

Later in the same session, on which flow cells each group uses (L378):

> "the 2 cohorts in the middle… that's where most volume is sitting, that's where they go for the 25B. The 2 other cohorts is likely still staying on the 10B flow cell, which is more also -- have a different price point also that reminds more about the 6000 high volume."

So the low-volume group does not get onto the cheapest flow cell (the 25B), even after it converts.

**Ankur Dhingra, CFO, Wolfe Research conference, 20 Nov 2024 (L386)**

> "many of them are thinking of moving, some of them, I believe, personally, will never move. They will keep their tests on 6000… For example, we have customers who still use MiSeq for their clinical test. They haven't even… moved to NextSeq. They haven't ever moved to 6000 either."

**Jacob Thaysen, Q4 2024 call, 6 Feb 2025 (L426).** Tycho Peterson asked whether "20% of tests will never move over to X" was still the view. Thaysen:

> "there will always be a fraction of customers that for reasons that they see that their assay works well and they have a nice profitability or they don't… want to invest more in that part of the business… stay on… the older technology for a while… rural [rule of] thumb is probably around 20% will stay and they will, of course, become smaller, small over time but it's taking substantially longer to transition the last part than it did for the first, let's just say, 75% to 80%."

**Jacob Thaysen, Q2 2025 call, 1 Aug 2025 (L412)**

> "there are customers that will stay on the 6000 until the end of life, so to say, for the assay… it's simply from a financial perspective, it doesn't make sense for them to spend the energy and the investment to shift it over to the X."

### B. Management uses the words "long tail"

**Ankur Dhingra, CFO, Strategy Update, 13 Aug 2024 (L682)**

> "After a point with our clinical customers, it's going to be a long tail, but still anticipating roughly half of the revenue would have transitioned and what 75% of the volume would have transitioned by the time we get to the end of the year here in the second-half of '25."

**Jacob Thaysen, Q1 2026 call, 30 Apr 2026 (L400, L410)**

> "there are customers that have decided to stay on the 6K platform for a longer period of time… clinical customers that have a regulated assay sitting on those and FDA-approved assay… So that is some of the, I would call it, laggards on the transition… by end of this year, we feel like we are fundamentally transitioned the business that will transition, then there will be a long tail of transition going forward."

**Ankur Dhingra, Q2 2026 call, 30 Jul 2026 (L156)**

> "We also placed over 10 NovaSeq 6000 units as some consumers plan to remain on that platform for years to come."

**Ankur Dhingra, Q3 2026 call, 29 Oct 2026 (L79)**

> "In clinical, 78% of volume is currently on the X, and we have seen the pace of conversion slow in recent quarters… Certain clinical customers are keeping current on-market assays on the 6000 while adding X capacity for new tests. We anticipate clinical conversion to reach 80% to 85% in the coming quarters."

The finish line has moved:
- Q4'25 call: "substantially done" by end-2026.
- Q1'26 and Q2'26: 80–85% by end-2026.
- Q3'26: 80–85% "in the coming quarters", with clinical stuck at 78% for two quarters.

### C. Small accounts can't fill a high-throughput flow cell

**Joydeep Goswami, then CFO, Morgan Stanley conference, 11 Sep 2023 (L154)**

> "if it's the same number of samples… makes your capacity 2.5 times at least larger. So if you have the same number of samples, you're going to wait 2.5 times longer to batch your samples… they'll maybe wait a little bit more to match, but won't run it at full capacity. And what that translates to is you don't get the pricing that people assume… You get a slightly higher effective price that we recover… We generally never see customers wait and start matching samples at the rate that would be that they would need for a flow cell load."

Management meant this as good news: under-filled flow cells keep the effective price up. Read against the thesis, it is an admission that labs without enough samples don't scale up their volume to fill the X.

**Jacob Thaysen, Q1 2025 call, 8 May 2025 (L288)**

> "the mid throughput is less so of a production environment, but more of an environment where you have other research segment or a small you could say clinical account that might not have the volume to fill up on a high throughput."

Strictly, this was about the mid-throughput segment. It is still the CEO saying small clinical accounts can't fill high-throughput capacity.

**Jacob Thaysen, Q2 2025 call (L542).** He drew the same contrast between labs that run high-throughput as production ("high volume and with high utilization… a production-like logic") and labs where sequencing is "more project-based".

### D. Mid-sized labs want leases and rentals, the largest don't

**Ankur Dhingra, Q3 2026 call, 29 Oct 2026 (L391, L393)**, answering Tycho Peterson on operating leases:

> "the demand there has been growing, both in the research as well as for clinical customers… We haven't seen that a whole lot with our largest clinical customers who have been in extensive capacity expansion. But when you look at the midsized clinical laboratories all over the world, including in U.S. and in Europe, that's where we've seen interesting demand pickup in that space."

A lab that wants to rent rather than buy a ~$1M X is telling you it is unsure it can fill it. This is the newest piece of evidence that mid-sized labs behave differently from the largest ones.

### E. Management's line of sight is limited to the largest customers

**Ankur Dhingra, Wolfe, 20 Nov 2024 (L376)**

> "Especially from our largest customers, right? They are the ones who we end up hand holding through the transition as well versus a lot of smaller customers, we tend to have a lesser visibility to. We only find out, oh, they just bought X already."

**Same session (L398)**

> "the fundamental concept of elasticity and how our customers are using the technology, at least for the largest ones, we do get a good line of sight towards that."

**Ankur Dhingra, Q3 2025 call, 30 Oct 2025 (L164)**

> "Specifically, business with our largest customers is projected to grow faster than the overall company average rate, at least over our strategic plan period."

If the largest customers grow faster than the company average, the rest grow slower than it.

**Q3 2025 deck, cohort slide footnote.** The 40-customer cohort that showed flat year-one revenue was based on:

> "clinical customers (n=40) that have fully or largely transitioned to NovaSeq X, including a subset of top-tier accounts."

The cohort covers only customers who had already converted, and it includes the biggest ones.

**Jacob Thaysen, JPM, 13 Jan 2026 (L306)**

> "While we do not intend to continue to share the performance of individual or cohort of customers, I can tell you, we continue to see growth across the spectrum of different customers that are converting."

"Across the spectrum" is a claim management no longer backs with data.

### F. How many customers are small (old, but the only count)

**Francis deSouza, then CEO, Q1 2023 call (L372).** Salli Schwartz, Investor Relations, repeated it at UBS in Aug 2023 (L156).

> "we have roughly maybe 1,000 customers that are high-throughput customers of ours. About 750 of them have only one 6000."

**Joydeep Goswami, Morgan Stanley, Sep 2023 (L132)**

> "For the single unit customers, right now, we're at about 5% [X penetration]… the higher throughput customers… are the most likely to switch early… you have a higher switch weight amongst the multiunit customers than the single unit customers."

So three-quarters of 6000 customers were single-machine labs in 2023, and they were converting at half the rate of the multi-machine labs. The numbers are three years old, but nothing more recent has been disclosed.

## 2. Expert calls (key quotes verified)

**Small labs can't fill flow cells**

- **Laboratory Director, LifeStrands Genomics (small Australian clinical lab), 25 Jul 2026 (L143):**
  > "you definitely need to be able to fill the flow cells, otherwise it becomes quite costly piece of equipment to just sit in the corner and not run."
  - The interviewer then asked whether smaller labs avoided the X because they're unsure how to use the capacity for small panels, and the expert said "Yes" (L147). That was a leading question.
- **Senior Staff ML Scientist, Genece Health (customer), 23 Jul 2026 (L181):**
  > "with smaller companies, if you don't have the full flow cell, you have to wait until an entire batch is ready. That actually makes the turnaround time a little bit longer."
- **Former Illumina scientist, Medical & Scientific Affairs, 5 Aug 2026 (L137):**
  > "Their NextSeq system is a workhorse in a lot of clinical labs where maybe they don't have the batching needs for a NovaSeq X."
- **PacBio Global Segment Lead, 3 Aug 2026 (L105):** hospitals "may not have the throughput. They may have to wait to batch samples, and so they're looking for a lower throughput instrument." PacBio is a competitor, so treat this with care.

**Volume no longer responds to price the way it did**

- **Former Illumina Associate Director, Product Management (left Jul 2025), 23 Jul 2026:**
  - On the NovaSeq era (L93):
    > "Customers were still sequencing more, but the price drops were so aggressive that it didn't quite make up for it… maybe the price would drop by half, but they would only increase their sequencing by about 25%."
  - Compare the earlier step, when a fourfold price cut produced "10X more" sequencing (L109).
  - On the coming 35B flow cell (L161):
    > "is dropping the price by 20% going to spur excitement and huge demand? Probably not."
- **Former Illumina Associate Director, Precision Health (left Sep 2024), 6 Aug 2026 (L175):**
  > "In each run, you get four times more data than the previous generation of NovaSeq 6000, but that really meant people were running out of samples to sequence… they won't be able to utilize the sequencer at full capacity for Illumina to generate the theoretical revenue that they really projected."
  - He has been gone two years and still owns the stock.

Neither expert splits elasticity by lab size. Their point is that the price-to-volume response has weakened across the board.

**Small labs pay much more per Gb**

- **Former Illumina Associate Director (left Jul 2025), 24 Jul 2026 (L157):**
  > "the big discounts Illumina gives, they're going to the big customers… A big company like Natera or Guardant or Foundation Medicine could be paying half or even a quarter of what a relatively small CRO or core lab was paying per base, even with the same instrument. You add in the lower throughput instrument, and it can go up easily five or tenfold."
- **Former Illumina Associate Director, 23 Jul 2026 (L367).** Smaller labs have "similar pricing conversations" but lack the Broad's leverage, and:
  > "Even if it's a small lab, they're going to go and talk to their friends at the latest conference and come back and, all of a sudden, Illumina's at a 20% margin."
  - Illumina rations small-lab discounts so they don't spread.

**The long tail in the customer base**

- **Former Illumina Associate Director, 23 Jul 2026 (L411):**
  > "Illumina's customer profile, it's a very long tail. The top 10 or 20 customers make up a very large proportion of the business, and then it's just a long tail of the smaller customers."
- **Former Senior Manager, Global Market Development, Illumina (Tegus, undated) (L83).** On ~20% clinical growth ex-China: it "is a lot to do with the big accounts… like Natera, like Tempus, like Caris." Growth is concentrated in large accounts.

**When small labs leave, the revenue may go elsewhere**

- **South Carolina core director, 30 Jul 2026.** Outsources to X-based providers at "under $1,800 per lane" and expects "most will switch from Illumina to Element."
- **Georgia core director, 31 Jul 2026.** "The difference in price drove a lot of our customers to be seeking vendors that could provide X Plus prices," and is sunsetting its 6000.

## 3. Data, sell-side and peers

**Price per Gb by flow cell (core-lab price lists)**
- At Salk (Jul 2025) and Pitt (Jun 2025), the 6000's small flow cells cost far more per Gb than the X's 25B: the S2 3.6–4.7x and the SP 6–9.8x.
- The 6000's large S4 costs about 2.1x.
- So a lab running small 6000 flow cells gets the biggest price cut if it converts. Unless it fills the 25B, it doesn't get that cut.

**Fill-rate arithmetic (folder calculation)**
- A 192-sample panel run costs about $207 a sample on a 6000 S4 and about $89 on a full 25B (480 samples).
- At 192 samples on a 25B, it costs $222 a sample, which is more than the 6000.

**Revenue mix (Q2'26)**
- The 6000 held 17% of high-throughput Gb but 41% of high-throughput revenue, so it earns about 3.4x per Gb (about 3.5x in Q3'26).
- Management itself explained the gap with flow-cell mix. Barclays' Q1'26 call notes (7 May 2026, L224) paraphrase management:
  > "new volumes went exclusively to 25Bs on X while new volumes on the 6k could have gone to smaller panels that generate more revs per Gb."

**Roche, a competitor, says the same (Investor Day, 12 May 2026, L1097)**
> "lower throughput users don't get the headline number economics that other sequencing platforms enable. They have to pay a lot more per genome."

**JPM customer survey (15 Jun 2026)**
- Half of the 32 clinical respondents are small labs (250–999 samples a month).
- About 75% of clinical respondents own a 6000.
- The survey does not cut 6000 ownership or volume response by lab size, so it shows the tail exists but not how it behaves.

**Installed base (FY25 deck)**
- About 1,360 active 6000s against about 890 Xs.
- The average 6000 pulls through about $670K a year against about $1.35M for an X. FY26 guidance for the 6000 is $600–650K.

**Alternative data (weaker; research-weighted public archives)**
- 72% of remaining 6000 runs are under 3 Gb, by count.
- Low-volume 6000 owners are documented: Bambino Gesù Rome at about €130K a year in kits, NIEHS buying small kits, and a Taiwan institute buying about $24K a year of consumables per box.

## 4. What cuts against the argument (be ready for it)

- **By volume, the tail may be big labs.** Thaysen said the low-volume group is "a smaller cohort… volume-wise". The groups keeping old tests on the 6000 while adding X capacity are "absolutely the largest" (Wolfe, Nov 2025).
  - FDA filings and the test registry show large labs' flagship tests on the 6000 (Caris, Foundation Medicine, Mayo, Labcorp's MRD test).
  - Public run data has about 74% of remaining 6000 Gb in large samples.
  - Our own analysis (`2026-10-31_ILMN_6000_residual_cohort_small_lab_test.md`) concluded the tail is probably small labs *by count* but large labs *by volume*.
- **Small segments are small revenue.**
  - A former Illumina associate director: "I can't emphasize enough how little revenue that product line actually generates" (low-throughput, 24 Jul 2026, L145).
  - An ONT director sizes mid-throughput at $500M–$1B.
- **Small labs may never be 6000 owners.** Experts say labs too small to batch use NextSeq or MiSeq, or they outsource.
- **Management says elasticity is working.**
  - "We are not seeing a lot of customers right now that is just shifting over and stay with the same volume" (Thaysen, Q3'24 call).
  - "does elasticity still work? And it does" (Wolfe, Nov 2025).
- **Under-filling helps Illumina in the short run.** Goswami's point was that under-filled flow cells keep effective prices up. That softens the cost of a small lab converting, but it also gives the lab a reason to leave for a smaller platform.
- **The survey does not show small clinical labs leaving.** They are the most Illumina-loyal tier, at 63% of their NGS work today and in three years.

## 5. How to use this in the pitch

The defensible version of the argument:
1. Management admits a low-volume group that "doesn't have the volume", a long tail, and a stalled conversion.
2. Small labs can't fill the X's 25B and today pay the highest prices per Gb. So when they convert they take the biggest price cut with the least volume response.
3. Management's proof that elasticity works comes only from large accounts, which it says it can see. It admits it has little visibility into smaller customers, and it has stopped publishing the data that would test the claim.

Avoid claiming that the remaining 6000 volume is mostly small labs. The evidence does not support that, and the bull case will point to Thaysen's "smaller cohort… volume-wise".
