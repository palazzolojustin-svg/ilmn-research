Sara Goodwin (SG), Director of the Center of Biomedical Research Support - The University of Texas at Austin 

### **SUMMARY** 

### **Overview** 

The expert oversees a large academic biomedical core network spanning genomics, cryo-electron microscopy, imaging, flow cytometry, mass spectrometry, shared instrumentation, scientific computing, and technical services. 

Demand is broadly stable, but trends vary by technology. Genomics, single-cell, and spatial workflows are flat, while flow cytometry and advanced cell analysis are expanding. Rising equipment, reagent, labor, and computing costs are pushing institutions toward cautious purchasing, outsourcing, and system-wide collaboration. 

### **Key Insights** 

- High-throughput sequencing is increasingly suited to centralized hubs or commercial providers because individual institutions may struggle to fill expensive instruments. 

- Academic cores should prioritize specialized expertise, rapid turnaround, subsidized access, or workflows requiring local samples rather than duplicating routine services. 

- Spatial biology interest exceeds actual project growth. Cost, sample preparation, histology support, and limited technical expertise slow adoption. 

- Multi-omics and epigenetics may provide more discovery value for complex diseases than repeatedly expanding wholegenome cohorts. 

- Flow cytometry is the clearest growth area, supported by demand for multidimensional cell profiling and small-particle analysis. Core facilities are nearing a structural turning point as funding constraints and operating costs undermine purely fee-for-service models. 

The expert strongly believes large-scale institutional collaboration is essential for long-term sustainability. 

Roche sequencing may face slower adoption than expected because of late market entry, library-preparation challenges, and uncertain differentiation.
