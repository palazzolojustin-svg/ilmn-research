## 2026 AI Biopharma review: the dream is still Morphic, not yet mortal* 

##### Summary takes 

**Slightly behind schedule, we publish our 2026 annual review of AI activity and developments in the pharma space, building on our 2024 (** **_<u>EU Pharma: Deep dive on AI in Pharma: industry finally entering the in-silico era?</u>_** **<u>) and 2025 (</u>** **_<u>EU Pharma : 2025 AI pharma review: pholds and boltz in place, in silico era (almost) here</u> (20/06/25)_ ) reports.** This encompasses a survey of the publicly disclosed initiatives for our coverage universe and updated assessment of how AI is being deployed across both corporates and the therapeutic landscape more broadly. The summary is that whilst we clearly appear to be past the initial bolus of publicly disclosed AI initiatives earlier this decade from a big pharma perspective, the pace of activity remains steady. After the landmark computational advances of 2024 (AlphaFold3) and 2025 (Boltz-2), we also had a quieter year on the innovation front, with perhaps the most notable development from Isomorphic labs (see below). 

**Overall, our view remains that industry deployment principally remains operational (of note, GSK became the first to formally announce a cost-cutting programme partly attributable to AI this year…), but the industry’s greatest and unique structural benefit will emerge on the R&D side, albeit that remains some years away. It remains very hard to call out specific winners within the group in our view, with much of the ongoing work undisclosed and intangible.**

# Recent Literature 

_A select number of publications are summarised here, but note there is an increasing number of scientific research published in the main medical and science journals with AI/ML applications in healthcare and pharma/medtech/biotech development. The papers summarised below are intended to provide a representative flavour of the type of practical impact that AI is having in the clinical landscape._ 

##### **The promise and perils of AI in oncology (The Lancet, May 2026):** 

- n **Summary:** This editorial presents both positive and negative views of AI in oncology, arguing that AI has the potential to substantially improve cancer care through improved diagnosis, increased efficiency in clinical trials/ decision making, and reduced healthcare system pressures, but that concerns around patient safety, data privacy, bias, governance, and public trust must be addressed before widespread adoption. 

##### **Transforming digital pathology with AI (The Lancet, May 2026):** 

- n **Summary:** This editorial argues that AI is moving from a research tool to a clinically useful component of pathology workflows, particularly through digital pathology and whole-slide image (WSI) analysis. The editorial highlights AI as potentially transformative for pathology, while emphasising the need for responsible implementation, governance, and workforce training. 

##### **Sustainability of large-scale artificial intelligence models in health care (The** **<u>Lancet, May 2026):</u>** 

- n **Summary:** this comment in The Lancet argues that while large-scale AI models (particularly generative AI and foundation models) have enormous potential in healthcare, their environmental, economic, and social costs are often overlooked. The comment warns that healthcare should adopt these technologies cautiously and only when their clinical benefits clearly justify their resource consumption. 

   - n The paper argues that electricity use, water for data-centre cooling, mining of raw materials for hardware, and electronic waste disposal are especially important to the healthcare sector as the environmental degradation can negatively affect health outcomes opposing the mission of the sector. The enormous medical imaging datasets which are publicly available already have a large carbon footprint, this is only expected to increase with the growth and widespread uptake of large foundation models. 

   - n Additionally, the article highlights the potential worsening of existing biases and global healthcare inequities because the smaller/less wealthier countries may be excluded from building AI solutions.

### Regulator's common principles for AI in medicine development 

**In January 2026, the FDA/EMA published their key principles on AI use in drug development.** It highlights the transformative potential of Artificial Intelligence (AI) in drug development and evaluation, aiming to improve healthcare outcomes. 

This document outlines a common set of ten guiding principles intended to inform, enhance, and promote the use of AI for generating evidence across all phases of the drug product life cycle. These principles aim to establish a foundation for good practice, address the unique characteristics of AI technologies, and foster future growth in this rapidly evolving field. 

The document suggests that these principles can guide international regulators, standards organizations, and other collaborative bodies in advancing good practices through research, educational tools, international harmonization, and consensus standards. Strong partnerships with public health partners are deemed crucial for advancing responsible innovations in AI. 

_The 10 guiding principles for good AI practice in drug development are:_ 

- n **Human-centric by design:** AI technologies should be developed and used in alignment with ethical and human-centric values. 

- n **Risk-based approach:** The development and use of AI technologies should follow a risk-based approach, incorporating proportionate validation, risk mitigation, and oversight based on the context of use and determined model risk. 

- n **Adherence to standards:** AI technologies must adhere to relevant legal, ethical, technical, scientific, cybersecurity, and regulatory standards, including Good Practices (GxP). 

- n **Clear context of use:** AI technologies should have a well-defined context of use, outlining their role and scope. 

- n **Multidisciplinary expertise:** Expertise covering both the AI technology and its context of use should be integrated throughout the technology's life cycle. 

- n **Data governance and documentation:** Data source provenance, processing steps, and analytical decisions must be documented in a detailed, traceable, and verifiable manner, in line with GxP requirements.

### Future of AI regulation in drug development 

**The paper, titled "** **_The future of AI regulation in drug development: a comparative analysis_ " published on 7**<sup>**th**</sup> **November 2025** examines the evolving regulatory frameworks for AI in drug development, focusing on the FDA and EMA. 

The authors makes a comparative analysis of how the FDA and EMA have responded to AI advances (including US executive orders and the EU's AI Act), an analytical framework to understand regulatory divergence, and an evaluation of whether AI applications are mature enough for standardized regulation amidst shifting policies. 

#### USA/FDA Approach 

- n The FDA's model is characterized by a flexible, dialogue-driven, and casespecific oversight that evolves through direct engagement with specific industry applications. It emphasizes market-driven innovation and administrative flexibility, often relying on informal guidance documents and iterative engagement rather than prescriptive frameworks, which contrasts with its international counterparts. This approach allows the FDA to adapt oversight to emerging technologies and maintain flexibility, though critics argue it reduces accountability and regulatory certainty. 

- n Key initiatives include the CDER AI Steering Committee and programs like the Innovative Science and Technology Approaches for New Drugs Pilot Program. In June 2025, the FDA deployed an agency-wide large language model assistant ('Elsa') to support internal scientific review and inspection planning, focusing on operational efficiency rather than substantive regulatory change. The FDA's 2023 discussion paper introduced a riskbased framework emphasizing human-led governance, data quality, and robust model development and validation protocols. This framework

- n While this approach can encourage innovation via individualized assessment, it may also create uncertainty about general expectations for AI applications. Recent US political developments, such as new Executive Orders (e.g., EO 14148 rescinding EO 14110) and an "America First" stance, introduce further uncertainty and potential shifts in the regulatory landscape, potentially delaying guidance and increasing judicial scrutiny of agency rules. The FDA's stance is further shaped by intense Congressional and judicial oversight and public scrutiny, leading to an "artisanal regulation" approach where decisions are carefully calibrated. 

#### EMA Approach 

- n In contrast, the EMA employs a structured, risk-tiered approach that reflects a broader EU emphasis on harmonized market rules and precautionary regulation across member states. Its 2024 Reflection Paper establishes a regulatory architecture that systematically addresses AI implementation across the entire drug development continuum. This framework introduces a risk-based approach focusing on 'high patient risk' applications affecting safety and 'high regulatory impact' cases where AI significantly influences regulatory decision-making. 

- n In early development phases, the EMA applies lower regulatory scrutiny to drug discovery applications but emphasizes data quality, representativeness, and mitigation of bias. For clinical development, particularly pivotal trials, requirements become more stringent, including pre-specified data curation pipelines, frozen and documented models, and prospective performance testing, explicitly prohibiting incremental learning during trials. Post-authorization allows for more flexible AI deployment but requires ongoing validation and performance monitoring integrated into pharmacovigilance systems. Technical requirements are comprehensive, mandating traceable documentation, explicit assessment of data representativeness, and strategies to address class imbalances and discrimination. The EMA also expresses a preference for interpretable models but acknowledges black-box models when justified by superior performance. 

- n This framework aligns with the EU's broader AI Act and ethical guidelines for trustworthy AI, maintaining pharmaceutical sector specificity and complementing overarching EU regulations like the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), Medical Device Regulation (MDR), and In Vitro Diagnostic Medical Device Regulation (IVDR). While the EMA's clearer requirements can provide more predictable paths to market, they might also slow early-stage AI adoption and potentially create compliance burdens for smaller actors due to extensive upfront documentation and validation. This structured approach may favor established companies with resources to navigate comprehensive requirements, potentially limiting smaller innovators.

Despite these differing regulatory philosophies and implementation methodologies, both agencies show convergence on risk-based principles and the common goal of ensuring safe and effective AI implementation in drug development. However, persistent transatlantic implementation differences and political-economic contexts pose both opportunities and challenges for AI-driven innovation. # Appendix B: 2025 AI in Pharma/Medicine (selective literature review) 

**_Deep Learning Model for Predicting Immunotherapy Response in Advanced Non −Small Cell Lung Cancer (December 26, 2024)_ (link to publication)** 

- n **_Summary_** : This study investigated whether deep learning can predict responses to immune checkpoint inhibitors (ICI) in advanced non-small cell lung cancer (NSCLC) using histological images. Researchers developed and validated a deep learning model on a dataset of nearly 1,000 patients from the US and EU. The model's performance, measured by its ability to predict objective response rate (ORR), progression-free survival, and overall survival, was comparable to the current standard biomarker, PD-L1. Specifically, the deep learning model achieved an AUC of 0.75 in the internal test set and 0.66 in the external validation set. While the deep learning model performed similarly to PD-L1 in the validation cohort (AUC 0.66 vs 0.67), it showed improved specificity. Combining the deep learning model with PD-L1 further improved prediction (AUC 0.70) and resulted in a higher response rate (51% vs 41% for PD-L1 alone). This suggests AI could be a valuable tool for guiding ICI treatment, refining patient selection, and improving outcomes in advanced NSCLC. 

**Screening performance and characteristics of breast cancer detected in the Mammography Screening with Artificial Intelligence trial (MASAI): a randomised, controlled, parallel-group, non-inferiority, single-blinded, screening accuracy study (link to publication)** 

- n **_Summary:_** The study results suggests AI can improve cancer detection in mammograms while reducing workload. A randomized controlled study in Sweden involving over 105,000 women compared AI-supported screening to standard double reading. The AI system triaged exams for single or double reading and highlighted suspicious areas. AI-supported screening detected more cancers (338 vs 262), particularly small, node-negative, and non-luminal A invasive cancers, and high-grade in situ cancers. This resulted in a 29% increase in cancer detection rate. Importantly, the falsepositive and recall rates remained similar between the two groups, while the positive predictive value of recall was higher with AI support. The AI system also reduced screen-reading workload by 44.2%. The findings suggest AI can aid early detection of clinically relevant breast cancer without increasing unnecessary callbacks _._ 

- n **_Editorial summary_** _:_ This editorial supports that the study offers compelling initial results on AI-supported mammography screening. The increased cancer detection rate and significant workload reduction are noteworthy, particularly for overburdened screening programs. However, the rise in insitu malignancy detection raises concerns about potential overdiagnosis and overtreatment. The editorial also claims that, while the study design is innovative, the true impact of AI-supported screening hinges on the forthcoming interval cancer rate data. Only then can we determine if the increased detections translate to meaningful clinical outcomes. The future role of AI in breast cancer screening remains promising, but further research and careful consideration of patient perspectives are crucial before widespread adoption.
