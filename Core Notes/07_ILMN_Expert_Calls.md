# Expert Calls on Illumina and the Sequencing Market

*Core Notes section 07. Synthesized from 23 source extracts as of 2026-10-10. Bracketed tags cite the source; full detail is in Working Notes (see INDEX.md).*

## Key takeaways

This section collects about two dozen expert interviews held between early 2026 and October 2026. The experts include former Illumina employees, customers running academic sequencing cores (shared university labs that sequence for other researchers), clinical lab directors, and staff at rivals such as Roche, PacBio and Oxford Nanopore. Most of what they say is experienced opinion, not verified data. Many work for competitors, and several have Illumina knowledge that is years old.

Taken together, the calls draw a consistent picture. Illumina remains firmly entrenched, especially with clinical customers. Its core sequencing business is commoditizing, meaning it is becoming a standard product competed on price. Each competitor attacks a different slice of its market.

## Illumina is still the default, but its share is slipping

Every expert described Illumina as the incumbent. It was called "the gold standard" [Expert: Roche UK precision medicine lead, Jul 2026] and "the workhorse of the industry" [Expert: Oxford Nanopore senior director, ~Oct 2026]. Another called it "almost the IBM" of sequencing, meaning nobody gets fired for choosing it [Expert: former Illumina associate director now at Kailos, Aug 2026].

The experts pointed to three advantages:

- an end-to-end system of instrument, sample-prep kits and analysis software;
- a workforce that learned on Illumina;
- clinical tests already validated on its machines.

Experts agree that share is eroding, but their estimates of Illumina's current share differ widely and none cites a firm source. The table below lists each claim so the spread is visible.

| Expert | Share claim |
|---|---|
| Former Illumina associate director, product management [24 Jul 2026] | Fell from ~80% to mid-60s%, losing ~3 points a year, likely to level off near 50% |
| Former Illumina field application scientist [24 Jul 2026] | Fell from 80% to 70%, with "at least 30%" more to lose by about 2029–2030 |
| Former Illumina associate director, now at Kailos [Aug 2026] | 80%+ of installed instruments |
| LifeStrands Australia lab director [25 Jul 2026] | "Something like 95%" (a loose guess) |

The common thread is gradual decline rather than collapse. As a Natera manager put it, "It's not going to be in six months, they will lose 30%" [Expert: Natera associate director, Jul 2026]. The academic cores and a large genome center expected little or no share loss over the next 12 months.

## Clinical customers are both the growth engine and a concentration risk

The experts broadly agree that clinical sequencing (using DNA tests to diagnose and treat patients) is now Illumina's growth engine, and that academic research is the main drag. One former employee said clinical was 52% of Illumina revenue when they left in September 2024 [Expert: former Illumina associate director, Aug 2026].

Two clinical growth areas came up repeatedly. Liquid biopsy tests for cancer from a blood sample. MRD (minimal residual disease) testing looks for traces of cancer left after treatment.

Research demand is suffering from US funding cuts, including the 2025 NIH cap on university overhead charges [Expert: PacBio segment lead, Aug 2026]. Research-use sales have "fallen off a cliff" [Nanopore director, ~Oct 2026]. Academic channel checks were mixed:

- **Large genome center:** expects 2026 volume to fall up to about 5%.
- **Arizona core:** expects 2026 to be roughly flat, against an internal growth target of 10% to 20%.
- **South Carolina core:** output up about 40% year over year in Q2 2026, but it plans to move most in-house work to Element's AVITI24.

The worry about clinical growth is concentration. It comes from roughly 10 to 20 large labs such as Natera, Guardant, Tempus and Caris [Nanopore director, ~Oct 2026], and these labs have bargaining power. A rival offering to "cut your cost by half" could have "a really big impact," and Illumina risks being "only a supplier" [former Illumina associate director, Aug 2026].

## The core sequencing business is flat at best, so Illumina is building an ecosystem

Former employees explained why Illumina's growth stalled from about 2022. In earlier product generations, halving the price led customers to sequence four times as much. That pattern broke.

Since 2022, Illumina's blended price per gigabase (billion DNA letters) has fallen from about $5 toward $2.50, and volume gains have only offset that decline. For core sequencing consumables (the reagent kits customers buy for every run), "flat would be a huge win" [Expert: former Illumina associate director, 23 Jul 2026].

The NovaSeq X, Illumina's flagship sequencer, made this worse. It delivered so much cheap capacity that customers "sequenced everything in the freezer" and then ran out of samples [Kailos expert, Aug 2026].

The experts describe a three-part response from Illumina:

- **New consumables on existing machines.** The 35B flow cell cuts price per gigabase by about 20%.
- **Ecosystem products that run only on Illumina sequencers.** These include the five-base methylation assay, StrataMap spatial, PIPseq single-cell, TruPath synthetic long reads, and the SomaLogic proteomics (protein-measurement) acquisition.
- **Reagent rental.** Instruments are placed free in exchange for multi-year consumable commitments. One expert said Illumina refused this until at least mid-2025 [23 Jul 2026]. Another said Illumina now offers it [Expert: former Illumina field application scientist, 24 Jul 2026].

Experts called the multi-omics direction sensible but unproven. Customers have barely responded to SomaLogic. Illumina is widely seen as late against 10x Genomics in single-cell and spatial biology, which measure gene activity cell by cell or by location in tissue.

## Roche's AXELIOS is the most debated threat

Views on Roche's new sequencer span the whole range, and they shifted as launch approached. AXELIOS uses a chemistry called SBX (sequencing by expansion).

- **Early 2026:** a former Illumina manager said its speed matters little in cancer testing [Tegus, est. Jan 2026].
- **July 2026, around the research-use launch:** a Natera manager called Roche "the main disruptor" [Jul 2026]. A former Illumina product lead called AXELIOS "not ready" and expected it to win perhaps 10 to 15 of every 100 sequencers bought in 2027 [23 Jul 2026].
- **August onward, more measured views:** the PacBio lead called it "a lot of hype without a lot of data" [Aug 2026]. A genome center VP expected minimal impact within a year but more pressure than smaller rivals later [31 Jul 2026]. The Nanopore director thought Roche could take 10% to 20% over about three years [~Oct 2026]. A Brigham single-cell specialist found AXELIOS data "very similar" to Illumina's on a few datasets, but estimated Roche needs five to ten years to build a real ecosystem [~Oct 2026].

Experts broadly agree on AXELIOS's claimed strengths:

- reads of about 1,000 DNA letters;
- a roughly four-hour genome, versus about 24 hours on Illumina;
- a duplex 30X genome (each DNA position read about 30 times) at about $150.

They disagree on accuracy. One expert put it at Q38, a standard accuracy score, as a best case. Another cited about 45 to 52. Others ranged from "maybe not quite as good as Illumina" to higher than Illumina. The underlying chemistry was called "cumbersome."

## Switching is slow and mostly happens around new projects

Switching costs, especially for clinical labs, are the main reason experts expect gradual change. Their estimates scale with the stakes:

- **Clinical revalidation:** $100,000 to $200,000 [LifeStrands, Jul 2026].
- **Converting an established lab:** four to five years [Nanopore director, ~Oct 2026].
- **Moving an FDA-approved test:** up to $200 million in worst-case clinical trials [Expert: former Illumina bioinformatician, Jul 2026].
- **Adaptive's clonoSEQ:** still runs on an older Illumina model after about a decade [24 Jul 2026].

Rivals are added beside Illumina rather than replacing it. They win new labs, new projects, contract renewals and price-sensitive academic work. Ultima, for example, is pulling projects away from a Georgia core [31 Jul 2026].

One mechanism worth watching is technology transfer, in which a test company trains a hospital lab to run its test locally. A Roche UK lead noted that NHS labs must copy the partner's equipment. If Natera or Guardant revalidated on a cheaper sequencer, those labs would follow [29 Jul 2026].

## Long reads remain a niche

Long-read sequencing reads much longer continuous stretches of DNA. Nearly every expert sees it as valuable but specialized, at roughly 10% of demand, a figure usually suggested by interviewers rather than experts.

Its main uses are:

- rare disease;
- large DNA rearrangements;
- epigenetics (chemical marks on DNA);
- pathogens.

It is held back by cost, missing population databases and immature clinical software.

Illumina's synthetic long-read product, TruPath, drew mixed views. Adoption guesses ranged from 1% to 2% of volume [genome center VP] to 5% to 6% [South Carolina core]. The Georgia core was "not at all likely" to use it.

## Pricing and data are the new battlegrounds

Experts call short-read pricing "a race to the bottom" [PacBio lead, Aug 2026], with headline genome prices clustering around $100 to $200. Their complaints about Illumina's pricing centered on three points:

- **Opaque individualized quotes:** list prices differ from actual quotes.
- **Steep small-lab premiums:** small users can pay up to "100X" more per DNA letter than large ones [Daisy Lab co-founder, Jul 2026].
- **Broken discount promises:** promised discounts were not honored [Georgia core, Jul 2026].

Several experts noted that once sequencing is cheap, sample prep and data analysis dominate total cost. Interpreting results, not producing them, is now the bottleneck.

## What changed over the year

The calls show four shifts over 2026:

- **Roche:** moved from a theoretical rival to a launched research product with early real data, though no clear customer wins yet.
- **Illumina's commercial stance:** moved from premium pricing toward ecosystem lock-in, a cheaper flow cell and reagent rental.
- **Academic funding:** worsened. By late July, experts were less optimistic about NIH money for 2027.
- **Element and Ultima:** gained adoption more slowly than their "a lot of hype" a few years ago implied [PacBio lead, Aug 2026]. Roche has displaced them as the main concern.

## Former Illumina Senior Manager, Global Market Development (Tegus, undated, est. Jan 2026)

### Who the expert is and how to weigh the call

The expert spent about seven years at Illumina in sales, oncology, market development and global market development roles. Before that, they were a regional marketing manager at 10x Genomics and worked in technical sales at Thermo Fisher. They hold a PhD from Nanyang Technological University and did post-doctoral work at the Genome Institute of Singapore and Harvard Medical School. They now lead international precision medicine at the drugmaker Astellas, and twice flagged that this role may bias them.

The transcript is undated. References to Roche marketing AXELIOS, Illumina's SomaLogic deal and Thermo Fisher's ownership of Olink place it in late 2025 or 2026; the 15 Jan 2026 date is an estimate.

Three cautions apply:

- The expert spoke of Illumina in the past tense, so their view of current plans is dated or secondhand.
- The two Illumina statistics discussed (20% clinical growth and a 65% clinical share) came from the interviewer, not the expert. The expert did not verify them and at first did not understand the 20% figure.
- Apart from some workflow figures, everything is experience-based opinion.

### The real bottleneck is clinical adoption, not sequencing cost or speed

The expert called clinical sequencing the "holy grail," where the large revenue is. They said every company wants to be a clinical NGS company. NGS, or next-generation sequencing, reads millions of DNA fragments in parallel.

In the expert's view, the binding constraint is getting NGS adopted as a clinical test. It gets stuck in three places [Expert: former Illumina senior manager, Jan 2026 est.]:

- reimbursement (whether insurers pay);
- regulation (test approval pathways);
- prescribing habits (how oncologists order tests and how readily pathologists adopt them).

### Clinical growth is concentrated in a few big US labs

The interviewer said Illumina's clinical consumables grew about 20% excluding China for two straight quarters. The expert said this matched what they knew. They attributed it mainly to large US labs such as Natera, Tempus, Caris and Foundation Medicine.

They inferred, without stating it as fact, that the growth reflects the rise of MRD testing over the prior six to twelve months, and they expect it to continue. Inside Illumina, the unanswered question was how to achieve "organic or democratized" clinical sequencing across many smaller labs: "always the tough question to answer."

### "Clinical" is loosely defined and mostly lab-built tests

On Illumina's claim that clinical is over 65% of consumables revenue, the expert said the figure "has always been a very loose statement." They added that "Illumina has been very generous and very broad in terms of classifying clinical use."

The only FDA-approved comprehensive genomic profiling assay they cited was TruSight Oncology (TSO) Comprehensive, the approved version of TSO 500. Comprehensive genomic profiling, or CGP, tests hundreds of cancer genes at once. Beyond that are legacy assets the expert doubts anyone runs. Most clinical volume runs as laboratory-developed tests (LDTs, which a single lab designs and validates itself), mainly at Natera, Foundation and Tempus.

On TSO Comprehensive now being reimbursed in the US, the expert said it "helps expand access." They would not predict it taking share from Foundation Medicine's send-out model, in which hospitals ship samples to a central lab. Because CGP reaches few patients today, wider reimbursement means "market expansion as opposed to a cannibalization kind of scenario," though cannibalization may come later.

### Community oncology is "the billion-dollar question"

Smaller community practices will struggle to adopt NGS at scale. Physicians there are only now getting used to CGP reports, as academic centers did five to ten years ago. Running a test is getting easier, but community hospitals still lack the expertise to use the data. A hub-and-spoke model, with samples sent to a regional center, works best today. Turnaround is driven mostly by batching: a lab that cannot fill a weekly run may wait another week to stay cost-effective.

### Roche

On Roche's same-day genome claim, the expert thought speed matters little in oncology. Library preparation (getting DNA ready for the sequencer) plus sequencing takes about 24 to 36 hours on Illumina. Halving that saves about 12 hours across a long chain of biopsy, processing, prep, sequencing, bioinformatics and reporting. Speed matters more in pediatric rare disease, such as babies in neonatal intensive care. The expert had seen little or no AXELIOS oncology data.

Roche's diagnostics sales force is "a big advantage," but the expert remained skeptical while acknowledging possible bias. Converting Thermo or Illumina labs is one opportunity. The larger prize is bringing new labs into NGS, which needs a "push-button system" that pathologists trust.

On a possible regulator-cleared AXELIOS paired with a companion diagnostic (CDx, a test that decides whether a patient gets a specific drug) for Roche drugs, the expert raised two cautions. First, tying a test to drugs creates ethics and compliance risk. Second, there is precedent. After Roche bought Foundation Medicine, FoundationOne patients in some markets could end up on Roche drugs, and other drugmakers grew wary of using FoundationOne for their CDx and LDT programs. The expert expects the same if Roche links drugs and sequencing too closely.

The expert said Roche's 10x Genomics partnership for single-cell work on AXELIOS copies Illumina's playbook of adding functions to existing instruments. Customers like this, because Illumina's old habit of forcing new instrument purchases every few years "leaves a bad taste in people's mouths." The expert called Illumina's single-cell and spatial work "very well" executed, citing "five-Base" and methylation as added analytes (things measured).

### Sub-$100 genomes from Element and Ultima matter little in oncology

The expert called the sub-$100 genome "a very nice, sexy number" with limited oncology impact. A germline genome (inherited DNA) is read at about 30X depth, but tumors need "easily more than 120" and "not even 200 times." That pushes real cost well above $100, a point marketing rarely makes. Library prep, biopsy, FFPE blocks (preserved tissue), slide cutting and bioinformatics add more. "Even if sequencing is free," regulation and reimbursement take time, so the impact on somatic oncology (tumor-acquired mutations) will be "very limited."

### Competitors in oncology, single-cell and spatial

Thermo Fisher's Ion Torrent wins on a seamless DNA- or RNA-to-report workflow, small panels and somewhat faster turnaround. Illumina wins on technology and capacity, from small panels up to whole genomes. Labs often start on Thermo and move to Illumina, then CGP, as they scale.

10x leads for now in single-cell and spatial. Published comparisons, as the expert recalled them, had Illumina ahead on spatial and transcripts per cell (depth) and 10x ahead on single-cell and cells per sample (breadth). Overall "it's up in the air," but Illumina "can close it really, really quickly" because existing customers can try it cheaply.

On TSO Comprehensive versus FoundationOne CDx, the in-house comparison is Roche's AVENIO kit, since labs cannot run Foundation's central test. A savvy pathologist would favor TSO's roughly 500 genes over FoundationOne's "300 over genes" ("if I'm not wrong"). Oncologists, however, favor FoundationOne's brand, CDx claims and clinical evidence.

### Skeptical on MRD

The expert has "always been a bit skeptical" about MRD going global and flagged this as personal bias. MRD works today on strong US interest and some US reimbursement, but it is expensive. Most tests come from large US central labs and have not reached Europe or beyond, except Japan; even Foundation struggled outside the US. Building MRD in-house is "quite difficult." The clinical driver would be drugs with MRD on the label, but de-escalation trials "did not pan out."

Illumina's coming MRD kit for labs is tumor-agnostic, meaning it needs no prior tumor sequencing. That demands heavy sequencing and high cost. Adoption would help Illumina, but the expert struggles to see it given unclear benefits.

### Instruments, DRAGEN and SomaLogic

Labs upgrade to NovaSeq X for three reasons:

- new applications arrive there first;
- heavy users get lower cost per base;
- it is seen as "the pinnacle of the technology," with no fear of "the next new iPhone."

The expert gave no growth forecast. Smaller instruments keep a role in microbiology and fast-turnaround work: the new MiSeq is "really, really fast," and NextSeq "is pretty much there as well." Illumina has never "really worked that hard" in that segment, while Ultima and, less so, Element attack the "sexy" high end.

DRAGEN, Illumina's analysis software, is "a very important aspect of the strategy." Analysis has three layers:

- **Primary analysis** converts the instrument's light signal into DNA letters. Labs stopped worrying about it a decade ago.
- **Secondary analysis** aligns reads and calls variants. This is DRAGEN's role.
- **Tertiary analysis** turns results into a clinical report, where firms such as SOPHiA work.

Because most of the world's data is Illumina's, DRAGEN has "taken out the guesswork" and become the standard for secondary analysis, including SNP calling (finding single-letter variants). The expert thinks Illumina under-markets it because it is "not a very sexy thing to talk about."

SomaLogic fits "absolutely the right strategy" of more applications per box. Thermo's Olink purchase "sets up a new battleground," and a NovaSeq X owner can add SomaLogic more easily than Olink. In clinical oncology, however, NGS-based protein detection is some way off because of cost and difficult setup, including a library-prep robot.

### The core risk: technology company or clinical company?

The expert's biggest concern is that Illumina "has not decided whether it wants to be a technology company or it wants to be a clinical company." A clinical path would need many more regulator-cleared assays and companion diagnostics, plus R&D, commercial and business-development teams, pharma partnerships, and a proven route from research test to approval.

## Natera Associate Director, Oncology Lifecycle Management (22 Jul 2026)

### Who the expert is

The expert is a physician with academic research in molecular oncology and translational medicine (moving lab findings toward patient care) at Johns Hopkins and Ohio State. They worked on immunotherapy, RNA and circulating tumor DNA (ctDNA, tumor DNA fragments in blood). After six months at a Boston start-up, they joined Natera, "the leader in ctDNA," four and a half years ago.

They now lead clinical-trial and commercial strategy in GI cancers with P&L responsibility, focused on MRD, and were promoted from Senior Manager in August 2025. They are a current Illumina user. Natera uses aspects of Element for ctDNA, and the expert ran Element's benchtop sequencer at the start-up.

They speak directly on MRD and from "hundreds and hundreds of physicians every year," but only from reading on Roche and Ultima. They described themselves as "biased for MRD," "not taking sides" between Natera and Guardant, and knew little about Illumina's diversification.

### Illumina's position: real competition, gradual share loss

For "20+ years" Illumina had no serious rival in short-read sequencing (reading short DNA fragments, the dominant high-volume method). "This recently has changed. It has changed a lot" [Expert: Natera associate director, Jul 2026].

The challengers are:

- Ultima, on cost;
- Element, whose VITARI system is "almost equal with NovaSeq X";
- Roche, "the main disruptor," which Illumina will "start really, really feeling... very soon."

Yet rivals "haven't infiltrated so much." The expert recalled, unverified, that Illumina's revenue fell 2% to 3% "last year." Illumina will lose share gradually, "not... in six months." Something "super disruptive" and "AI-based" might come within two years, but today "the barriers to bypass, to change Illumina, are very, very high."

### Ultima and Element

Ultima now does whole-genome and whole-exome sequencing with "relatively high quality." Whole-genome sequencing (WGS) reads the full genome; whole-exome sequencing (WES) reads only protein-coding regions. Some of Ultima's presentations were "not as good."

The expert recalled Ultima pricing of $80 to $110 per sample for low-depth uses such as oncology, fetal and women's health, smaller and local labs, and transplant. In those markets Illumina "will start losing some market share." Ultima's Solaris chemistry, the expert recalled, needs about 50% less DNA input, cuts cost 30%, brings cost per million reads below $0.40 and supports "somewhere $50 genome." The UG200 is priced "comparable with Illumina," so "I don't think that there's any reason" to use it. The expert agreed ("Exactly") that many uses only need "good enough" data at about 30% of the cost.

The expert recalled Element's VITARI costs about $700,000, versus more than $1 million for NovaSeq X, and delivers 50X genomes for "somewhere over $70." That suits women's health, transplant, infectious disease, population genomes and cancer screening, covering "the majority of the oncology customers." But "you cannot run Signatera or ctDNA with 50X" (Signatera is Natera's MRD test). Element's benchtop AVITI is "somewhere over $100 per genome," runs single-cell and is like "having an espresso in your house"; at the start-up "You could run basically anything."

Element cannot do CGP, which "usually needs 150X or deeper, plus you need RNA sequencing," or tissue-informed MRD, which needs more than 80X or 120X. Targeted tests such as NIPT (non-invasive prenatal testing) use probes that act like a GPS, so more than 30X is "overkill." MRD is a "shotgun approach" seeking the "tiny fish in a big ocean." In the US, "the big four, plus Natera, they're just using Illumina" for MRD. This sits loosely against the earlier remark that Natera uses aspects of Element.

### Why switching is slow

New companies adopt new platforms far more easily than established ones switch. A large lab will not "throw away all the Illumina platforms" or run one product on two sequencers, and "FDA is not going to love it." Switching is likeliest in new applications, start-ups, and academic, new or local labs. MRD-guided trials last five to seven years, and "you cannot switch an assay or a sequencer middle of a trial. It's a no-no." A cheap secondhand market for Illumina machines adds stickiness, and some users prefer a five-to-seven-year-old Illumina for the brand.

### MRD market economics and accuracy

MRD is reimbursed mainly through Medicare, with limited private coverage. No company is reimbursed in Europe, and only Natera has approval from Japan's regulator, the PMDA. European local labs "can do whatever they would like," an opening for non-Illumina platforms.

The expert's market sizing:

- MRD is about $5 billion today and $30 billion by 2030, citing consultant projections of $30 billion to $50 billion.
- NIPT, transplant, infectious disease and regional panels combined are more than $40 billion.
- MRD will be "equal with everything" in five years.

Barriers are adoption, reimbursement, cost and clinical utility, since many physicians "don't know what to do with it." Margins will "automatically" rise as sequencing cheapens. Companies absorb high upfront costs because FDA and NCCN (the US cancer guideline body) attitudes are shifting: "if you leave early, you are missing out long term."

Consolidation is under way: "Tempus just bought Personalis two days ago" (about 20 Jul 2026, per the expert). The first actionable MRD use is the IMvigor trial in muscle-invasive bladder cancer.

On conflicting MRD results from two vendors on one patient, the expert said single-patient anecdotes are "not science." They cited two Guardant studies of 2,000 Japanese colorectal patients each ("quality of evidence 1b"). Guardant and Natera both show sensitivity in the "high 80s percent or 90%" and specificity above 95%. Many misses reflect biology: lung, brain and peritoneal disease shed little ctDNA. The expert is "more skeptical on how the physicians are using the test." Moving from exome to genome is "the future" and should cut false negatives. Signatera is tissue-informed and Guardant is not, but "the data are very consistent."

On standards of care, colonoscopy is the screening gold standard (claimed 100% sensitivity, 99.9% specificity). For MRD, NCCN's standard is imaging, but CT has about 70% sensitivity in colon cancer. The expert reads ASCO guidance as saying ctDNA should never decide alone but is useful for liquid CGP, in trials, and especially when imaging is ambiguous.

### Roche SBX, long reads and structural variants

Having seen SBX at AGBT, the expert called it "very promising" and "very innovative." It "goes very deep," has nanopore-like long-read capability "great for MRD," and costs about $750,000. They are "not fully convinced that it will radically change the market," for five reasons:

- Illumina users have many cheaper reagent and library-prep options.
- Adopters of SBX, launched end of June 2026, must "go all in."
- Commercial MRD labs are unlikely to reuse consumables up to 20 times or for three months, as Roche claims. The interviewer added that reused cells reportedly run slower.
- The four-hour deep run needs 150 ng of DNA, "nearly impossible from a real patient."
- It launched as research use only (RUO), not for CLIA-certified clinical labs, though CLIA plans are expected "in the very near future."

Anyone running "a multi-billion-dollar business on sequencing" will not switch for a brand: "You need to prove it first."

Illumina in long reads: "they're not part of it, basically." Long reads (Oxford Nanopore, PacBio, potentially SBX) are "very important research-wise" but commercially "specialized"; the interviewer suggested about 10% of sequencing. Uses include:

- viral sequencing (HPV, HCV, some COVID);
- repeat disorders such as Huntington's, Fragile X and Friedreich's ataxia;
- HLA typing for transplant;
- cancer gene fusions;
- structural variants in MRD, where short reads miss "almost 1/3."

A combined CGP-plus-MRD long-read test is "the very distant future," and "nobody is brave enough to use that" for MRD. Chronic kidney disease is a smaller growing use.

The expert described "structural variants" as clusters of single-letter variants within 50 base pairs that together signal cancer. That fits closely clustered variants more than true structural variants. Natera acquired Foresight Diagnostics, the Stanford CAPP-Seq spin-out, "on November" (presumably Nov 2025), and "the future is there." The expert was "not convinced" by SAGA's (Roche-acquired, per the interviewer) ASCO GI data, which "were not better than Signatera or Guardant." Nobody will "switch completely your whole army of labs" for the missing third.

Illumina's spatial and single-cell presence "is not great" but necessary. Its Q1 call leaned into AI, multiomics and drug discovery, as "everybody is."

## University of Arizona Genetics Core Manager (AI channel check, 22 Jul 2026)

### Who the expert is

The expert manages Genomic Research Services at the University of Arizona Genetics Core, which has offered NGS for about 15 years to internal, external, start-up, non-profit and international clients. They oversee short-read operations, equipment, reagents and staffing, and have used Ion Torrent.

They are a small Illumina customer, with one NextSeq 550 used only for methylation arrays (which read chemical tags on DNA). They are a larger Element customer, with AVITI as the primary sequencer. A sister campus facility runs a NovaSeq 6000 and another NovaSeq. That, plus a desire for multiomic output, led the core to choose Element. The interviewer was an AI.

### Volumes, spend and pricing in Q2 2026

Two forces depress volume:

- Research funding has "become a bit reduced," with "stress and anxiety over funding cycles," so researchers postpone studies and hoard funds.
- Low-cost for-profit contract research organizations (CROs) have "greatly reduced our sequencing volume," leaving more "boutique-type preps."

Q2 2026 Element output (in gigabases) was "about the same" as Q2 2025, then "probably about a 5% or 10% increase" when pressed. That met expectations but left spare capacity. The expert expects full-year 2026 to be roughly flat against a 10% to 20% internal growth target, with optimism unchanged over 90 days.

Element consumables spend was flat, with "Just steady pricing from Element," not discounts. Inventory is "excessive" because the core stocks reagents researchers have committed to, and should normalize in 8 to 12 weeks. Lead times were unchanged.

The expert saw no rise in vendor discounting over 90 days. Supplier prices are rising across library prep, arrays, reagents and equipment, except Element's sequencing reagents, which "do not change in price throughout the life cycle of the product." When Illumina kit prices "change frequently and go up exclusively, it becomes hard for me to remain cost competitive." Large providers get "quite high" discounts, giving them cost advantages over small facilities.

### Platform share and new products

Illumina's highest-output systems such as NovaSeq X are gaining favor. Lower-output Illumina systems are not, "because the cost per base is no longer competitive." Ultima awareness is rising, there is "chatter" about Roche, and Element is gaining, with a coming higher-output sequencer. Buyers want "the lowest cost sequencing they possibly can."

The expert's 12-month view:

- **Gain or hold:** Roche (though "I have yet to see that hit the market"), Illumina's top systems and Element.
- **Lose:** lower-output Illumina systems, older Element systems not upgraded to AVITI24, and remaining Singular Genomics machines.

The core plans no equipment purchases in the next 12 months.

The expert likes Illumina's room-temperature shipping and smaller cartridges ("fairly innovative"). They are "not at all likely" to use TruPath Genome, because genome work "has migrated away from facilities like ours." SomaLogic strengthens Illumina's multiomics pitch but would not make them buy more Illumina. AXELIOS may pressure Illumina "in the next six months" on excitement, cost per base and reusable parts, but "it's early." The threat is "somewhere in between," "minor at first," given Illumina's "very large foothold" and "stable share."

Illumina's staff "have been great," but "it became very hard to keep up with their tech cycle." Non-profit cores have "very, very thin" margins and capital budgets "as low as it has ever been." A good LIMS (lab sample-tracking software) is essential. "An outright purchase of a machine that's over $500,000 is just much less feasible," so vendors should favor upgradable equipment and creative access.

## Daisy Lab Co-Founder and CSO, Former Auckland Genomics Lead (23 Jul 2026)

### Who the expert is

The expert co-founded Daisy Lab, a pre-revenue New Zealand precision-fermentation company (microbes making food ingredients), and is its Chief Scientific Officer. They led the University of Auckland's core, Auckland Genomics, for about five years and have close to 25 years in molecular biology.

- a 1,000-sample exome study that switches platforms midway and ends up clustering patients by machine rather than biology.

The cause is chemistry: Roche's SBX is "kind of long polymeric," Illumina's SBS involves amplification, and Thermo uses hydrogen-ion detection. Still, "it is not critical," old samples need no re-sequencing, and switching is "kind of switching from Windows to Mac." Ion Torrent is the closest comparator to Roche. Mixing long-read with Roche brings "too many artifacts."

### Ecosystem, switching and adoption

The ecosystem is "the biggest competitive advantage of Illumina." It comprises bundled systems, support, mature library protocols, bioinformatics, compatibility with 10x and Takara Trekker (a spatial method), and a large community. "It is hard to break that ecosystem." "We sequenced it on Illumina, meaning I can trust this data." Roche needs "five years. It could take 10 years."

Adoption would speed up with error-minimizing workflows, publications, promotions (such as 50% off reagents) and entry via hospital pathology labs. It would slow if Roche targeted small labs.

Researchers say: "I will pay more, but I will not switch." The expert rated the likelihood of using Roche at 4–5/5 for large biotech and pharma and 2–3/5 for small academic labs. They agreed with the interviewer's summary of roughly 50/50 for new projects and about 1/5 for existing ones. One large company spent about $2 million just on comparative quality checks. Big players expect Illumina "will not probably be the major player anymore in the next five, 10 years," and cores can offer Roche as a second platform.

Roche's wins will come in large-scale clinical work, such as sequencing every patient (the UK model and an emerging US trend, per the expert), and in biopharma. Single-cell demand is "being replaced by spatial transcriptomics" that uses fluorescent probes "and do[es] not use sequencing."

### Turnaround and price

At the core, batching to fill cheaper large flow cells (10 billion reads versus 500 million) takes a couple of weeks, and sequencing about two more, before download and analysis. That adds up to six to eight weeks from library to data. Roche saves roughly a week per run through faster and parallel runs and less batching. The expert confirmed the interviewer's point that 5–10 samples suffice rather than 20–30. Illumina has small flow cells, but at much higher per-genome cost.

The expert scored how much turnaround matters to each customer type:

| Customer | Turnaround importance | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Clinical/pathology | 5/5 | Treatment decisions, such as a child with cancer |
| Biotech/pharma | 4/5 | Trial and financial decisions |
| Academic | 2–3/5 | "More chill," accepted "because everybody is following Illumina" |

Speed is Roche's top marketing point and cost the second. Publications are key: "Can I trust the data if it's not in a... paper? Probably not." 10x built its lead by funding comparison studies and conference talks, and two to three years could yield critical-mass publications for Roche.

If Illumina cut to the interviewer's $100–$200 genome, "it should be enough... They don't have to really go down too low," and Roche's timeline would stretch to five to seven years. The expert endorsed the summary that prices fall, Illumina is "not out of moves," and Roche can become a real contender over about five years, mainly with large organizations and new projects.

## Cross-call themes

### Illumina's entrenchment is the dominant fact, but every rival chips a different piece

Every expert called Illumina the incumbent: "gold standard" [Roche UK lead, Jul 2026], "workhorse" [Nanopore director, ~Oct 2026], "IBM" with 80%+ of installations [ex-Illumina associate director, Aug 2026], and "tried and true" [ex-Illumina scientist now at Roche, Aug 2026]. Its stickiness comes from the end-to-end ecosystem, trained staff, validated clinical assays and trust. Even those predicting share loss expect it to be modest near term, and the academic cores and genome-center VP foresee little or no loss over 12 months.

The bearish cases differ in mechanism:

- segment-by-segment attack, with Ultima high, Element mid and Roche clinical [Roche UK lead; ex-Illumina associate director];
- slow, erratic strategy [ex-Illumina staff scientist];
- innovation fatigue [PacBio lead];
- send-out labs revalidating on cheaper platforms [Roche UK lead].

### Clinical has replaced research as the growth engine, and research funding is the main drag

Experts agree NIH cuts weaken academic demand. They cite the 2025 indirect-cost cap [PacBio lead], RUO "off a cliff" [Nanopore director], 2026 volume down about 5% [genome center VP] and 2027 funding fears [South Carolina core]. The exception is the South Carolina core, whose output rose about 40% on single-cell demand.

Clinical work is steadier and reimbursement-backed. It reached 52% of Illumina revenue by Sep 2024 [ex-Illumina associate director], with oncology at roughly 50%–60% of clinical [Abbott scientist; ex-Illumina scientist now at Roche]. Liquid biopsy and MRD are the consensus growth areas. The flip side is concentration: roughly 10 to 20 large labs, such as Natera, Guardant, Tempus, Caris, BillionToOne and Exact, hold bargaining power and could defect [Nanopore director; ex-Illumina associate director].

### Roche AXELIOS: a contested newcomer

Views span the full range:

- "will just simply die out" [South Carolina core];
- not significant within a year [Georgia core];
- minimal near term, more threatening than smaller rivals later [genome center VP];
- "hype without a lot of data" [PacBio lead];
- 10%–20% share over three years and $1–2 billion of revenue in 10 years [Nanopore director];
- five-plus years to a real ecosystem, or five to seven if Illumina cuts price [Brigham specialist].

Experts broadly agree on its strengths: reads of about 1,000 bases, a four-hour genome versus Illumina's 24 hours, roughly Illumina-level or better accuracy, and a $150 duplex genome. Its weaknesses are cumbersome chemistry, two boxes and no epigenetics.

Details conflict. Capital cost was put at $750,000 [PacBio lead] or "less than a NovaSeq X" [Nanopore director]. Accuracy was "maybe not quite as good as Illumina" [Abbott, Jul] versus higher than Illumina's [PacBio, Aug]. One expert attributed AXELIOS to Element, apparently in error.

### Switching is slow, costly and mostly happens with new projects

Switching estimates rise with the stakes:

- a month or two of side-by-side testing for a startup [Abbott];
- about a year for automation [ex-Illumina staff scientist];
- four to five years to convert a clinical lab, and six to 10 for new LDT labs [Nanopore director];
- $2 million just for quality checks at one large company [Brigham specialist].

Rivals are consistently added alongside Illumina, not in place of it. Their wins come at lease or service renewal, in new labs and in new projects.

### Long-read stays a niche complement

Nearly every expert sees long reads as valuable in rare disease, structural variants, epigenetics and pathogens, but limited by cost, throughput, missing population databases and clinical software. The client estimate was about 10% of demand [Roche UK lead, Jul]. Outliers are the ex-Illumina staff scientist (98.5% confident large clinical labs adopt long reads) and the South Carolina core (expects Nanopore to gain).

Illumina's synthetic long reads, called TruPath or "Constellation," split opinion. Adoption estimates were 5%–6% [South Carolina] and 1%–2% [genome center VP], against "not at all likely" [Georgia]. Launch timing is also disputed: end-2024 [staff scientist] versus early 2026 [Nanopore director].

### Pricing is converging, quotes are opaque, and ancillary costs rise

Short-read pricing is "a race to the bottom" [PacBio lead], with "everyone at around the same price point" [ex-Illumina scientist now at Roche], though nobody chases Ultima downward [Nanopore director]. Genome-price claims cluster at $100–$200: $100 for Element, $150 for Illumina and Roche, and Nanopore at least double.

Illumina's list prices were unchanged over 90 days at both academic cores and the genome center, but deals are individualized and promised discounts were sometimes broken [Georgia]. Informatics and storage costs are flat or rising. Capex of roughly $1–1.2 million for a NovaSeq X is the biggest barrier to switching.

### Data and interpretation, not sequencing, is the new bottleneck

Experts from the NHS, PacBio, Roche and the ex-Illumina ranks agree that machines are easy and interpretation is hard. Open bioinformatics, integrated workflows and AI-ready data are seen as the next battleground.

### Multi-omics: right direction, unproven execution

Most experts call SomaLogic clever or sensible, but it has barely touched customers. The genome center uses Olink, small cores find it too costly, and the Georgia director was unaware of it. Experts describe it as early, shallow and possibly vulnerable to native protein sequencing. Illumina is seen as late in single-cell and spatial against 10x, with PIPseq the one product drawing positive evaluation.
