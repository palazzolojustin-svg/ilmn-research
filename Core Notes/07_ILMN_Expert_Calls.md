# Expert Calls on Illumina and the Sequencing Market

*Core Notes section 07. Synthesized from 101 source extracts as of 2026-10-10. Bracketed tags cite the source; full detail is in Working Notes (see INDEX.md).*

## Key takeaways

: Expert Calls on Illumina and the Sequencing Market

This section collects about 90 expert interviews held between January and October 2026. Most are with Illumina's customers: academic "core" labs (shared university facilities that sell sequencing to campus researchers), pharma research groups, clinical labs and contract research organizations (CROs, outside firms that sell lab work as a service). A smaller set comes from former Illumina employees and from staff at competitors such as Roche, Oxford Nanopore (ONT), PacBio and Complete Genomics. Read together, they tell a consistent story. Illumina's installed base and clinical franchise were intact through Q3 2026. But the debate moved from *whether* rivals can dent Illumina to *how fast* its share erodes and how much price it must give up to slow that erosion.

A caution before the detail: many later calls are AI-run "channel checks" (scripted surveys of a lab's volumes, vendors and prices) with small, sometimes inconsistent samples. Competitor employees and former insiders have obvious biases, and at least two experts appear twice under different labels. Treat individual numbers as anecdotes and patterns across many calls as the signal.

## Illumina's moat is the ecosystem, not the machine

Nearly every customer described the same lock-in. It rests on validated workflows, trained staff, analysis software and depreciation schedules more than on sequencer hardware. Clinical labs put the cost of validating a test on a new platform at roughly $100,000-200,000 and "hundreds of samples" per sample type [Expert: Colorado children's hospital chief genomics officer, Oct 2026; Expert: Texas reference lab VP, Oct 2026]. Cedars-Sinai's genome validation used 120 genomes and "six figures" of flow cells, the consumable chips sequencing runs on [Expert: Cedars-Sinai molecular pathology director, Apr 2026].

The most vivid example came from a former Illumina associate director. Adaptive Biotechnologies' clonoSEQ cancer test still runs on the older NextSeq 550, at nearly ten times the cost per read of a NovaSeq, because revalidating it is "incredibly hard" [Expert: former Illumina AD, Jul 2026]. Pharma customers added an accounting barrier. Sanofi depreciates instruments over eight years, so a NovaSeq X Plus bought in 2024 cannot be retired before about 2030 without a write-off [Expert: Sanofi head of genomics, Oct 2026].

DRAGEN, Illumina's analysis software, was repeatedly cited as an advantage rivals lack. A Vanderbilt core manager said "No way can beat the DRAGEN" [Expert: Vanderbilt NGS manager, Jul 2026]. Not everyone agreed. A former Illumina bioinformatician said most people "just don't use it" [Expert: ML scientist, ex-Illumina, Jul 2026], and an ONT sales director called it "not a reason why to stay" [Expert: ONT EMEA sales director, Oct 2026].

The practical result in Q3 2026 was that, of roughly 25 customer channel checks, nearly all still ran 100% of their short-read sequencing on Illumina. Only three reported actual displacement, two to Element and one to Ultima.

## Share estimates agree on direction, not on pace

Market share estimates came almost entirely from former Illumina staff and competitors, so they are opinion, not data. They agree Illumina is losing share but disagree on how much and how fast. The table below shows the main estimates. The useful takeaway is the range, from mild erosion to a fall toward half the market.

| Source | Share today | Where it is heading |
|---|---|---|
| Former Illumina commercial manager [Jun 2026] | ~70%, down from 92% in 2017 | Below 60% |
| Former Illumina AD [Jul 2026] | Mid-60s%, down from ~80%, losing ~3 points a year | "Flatline" near 50% |
| Former Illumina field scientist [Jul 2026] | ~70% | Loses "at least 30%" over four to five years |
| ONT EMEA sales director [Oct 2026] | ~80% in Europe | 55-60% in three to five years, with revenue "overall stable" |
| Former Illumina AD, now Kailos [Aug 2026] | 80%+ of the installed base | Bullish for two to three years |

Two framings recur. Rivals each attack one segment while Illumina must defend all of them [Expert: ex-Illumina AD, Kailos, Aug 2026]. And Illumina is better described as a premium brand facing a "race to the bottom" than as a technology laggard [Expert: former Illumina AD, Jul 2026]. Over the year, former insiders increasingly framed Illumina's problems as strategic, such as indecision between research and clinical, leadership churn and software, rather than technological.

## Academic demand followed NIH funding, not competition

Every academic call tied volume to the National Institutes of Health (NIH), the main US research funder. The picture moved through three phases.

**Trough (late 2025 to early 2026).** UIUC's core saw billing about 15% below normal in the last four months of 2025 and ran at a loss for the first time in 25 years [Expert: UIUC DNA Services director, Feb 2026]. Mount Sinai's core put Q3 and Q4 2025 down at least 20% versus a normal year [Expert: Mount Sinai core, Feb 2026]. UCSD's core said early-2026 reagent buying was about 10% down and called itself "panicked" [Expert: UCSD IGM director, Mar 2026].

The mechanism was explained by a TGen director who sits on NIH grant panels. Success rates for R01s, NIH's main five-year research grant, fell from 21% in 2023 to 18% in 2024 and 13% in 2025. New R01s from October 2025 to March 2026 numbered 496, versus 1,099 a year earlier [Expert: TGen director, Mar 2026]. Part of the cause was "multiyear funding," which charges all five years of a grant to one budget year and so funds fewer grants. Several experts called it "an accounting trick."

**Rebound (Q2 2026).** Most Q2 channel checks reported output, measured in gigabases (billions of DNA letters read), up 15-25% year on year against a weak Q2 2025 [multiple experts, Jul 2026]. A pharma group running large Perturb-seq studies (gene-editing screens read out one cell at a time) reported about 50% growth and possibly a doubling for 2026.

**Split (Q3 2026).** Federally funded academic cores were mostly flat to down about 10%, citing late NIH awards and work lost to CROs. Labs funded by biopharma, philanthropy or clinical testing grew 15-50%, driven by single-cell work, Perturb-seq, MRD and rare-disease genomes [Q3 2026 channel checks, Sep-Oct 2026].

One complication is that some 2025 strength was pulled forward. Delaware's core said researchers spent multi-year awards early, fearing clawbacks, so it expects a later dip [Expert: Delaware core director, May 2026].

## Volume grows faster than dollars

The most consistent economic finding is that sequencing volume rises faster than Illumina's revenue from it. The Broad Institute's Illumina spend was "basically flat" from 2024 to 2025 while it generated twice as much data, after moving fully to the NovaSeq X Plus [Expert: Broad EVP, Apr 2026]. Yale's Illumina spend fell from $6-7 million to $3-4 million on lower volume plus new discounts [Expert: Yale Genome Center director, Apr 2026].

A former Illumina product manager offered the explanation. In the early years, halving the price might bring four times more sequencing. Since the NovaSeq era, halving price lifts sequencing only about 25%. They added that on core sequencing consumables, "flat would be a huge win" [Expert: former Illumina AD, Jul 2026].

Lower prices do still create new demand. Cedars-Sinai launched a whole-genome "backbone" test in January 2026, with panels sliced from the genome in software, "only because we had access to the cheaper platform," and its Illumina spend rose [Expert: Cedars-Sinai, Apr 2026]. Elsewhere the effect is lagged or negative in the short run. UCSD and Duke warned that each cheaper box produces a consumables dip when budgets are tight, because researchers bank the savings [Expert: UCSD, Apr 2026; Expert: Duke core director, Jun 2026].

## Pricing: list increases on paper, discounts in practice

Price signals conflicted, which itself is the finding. Customers reported annual February list increases of about 3-5%, plus tariff or freight surcharges of about 5-6% [Expert: Arizona core, Apr 2026; Expert: cancer center core director, Oct 2026]. Vanderbilt fought an unannounced tariff charge and won a bigger discount in return, and reports from other labs suggest Illumina handled the surcharge inconsistently [Expert: Vanderbilt, Jul 2026].

At the same time, Illumina cut prices where competition bites. Examples include an extra 30-40% discount on the largest NextSeq 2000 flow cell [Expert: California biotech director, Jul 2026], cheaper 5B and 10B NovaSeq X flow cells, a larger 35B flow cell expected around January 2027, and trade-in credits for NovaSeq 6000 owners that one director called "a discount war" [Expert: academic medical center core director, Oct 2026]. A former insider estimated that Illumina's blended high-throughput consumable price fell 30-40% since 2022 [Expert: former Illumina AD, Jul 2026].

The pain concentrates in the middle. Several cores said Illumina discounts aggressively on its biggest machines but not on mid-sized ones, which is what pushed them toward Element [Expert: South Carolina core, Mar and Oct 2026]. The NovaSeq X's low cost per gigabase holds only for labs that can fill its largest flow cells. That favors big centers and CROs over small cores, which describe losing routine work to low-cost providers such as Plasmidsaurus and Novogene. The Louisville director summarized it as demand "rerouted," not lost [Expert: Louisville center director, Sep 2026].

## Roche Axelios: excitement, then price disappointment, then wait-and-see

Roche's Axelios sequencer, built on SBX ("sequencing by expansion," which copies DNA into enlarged molecules called Xpandomers and reads them through a nanopore), dominated the year's calls. Views moved in three stages.

**Before the February 2026 AGBT conference**, experts were skeptical of Roche's accuracy claims but worried about its diagnostics sales force [Expert: VCU core co-director, Jan 2026; Expert: former Illumina market developer, Jan 2026].

**After AGBT**, pricing converged at a $750,000 instrument and about $150 per 30X genome (each DNA position read about 30 times). That genome price depends on reusing the sensor chip about 20 times. Customers read it as roughly parity with Illumina, not disruption. The Broad compared it with about $200 at Illumina list [Expert: Broad EVP, Apr 2026], while Yale said Illumina already gives it $150 [Expert: Yale, Apr 2026]. Cedars-Sinai argued that only $50-75 would have triggered "massive adoption" [Expert: Cedars-Sinai, Apr 2026]. New practical drawbacks emerged: a box that is not modular (a partial run costs as much as a full one), complex library prep, and an overnight amplification step that erases much of the speed advantage in routine use [Expert: UCSD, Mar 2026; Expert: TGen, Mar 2026].

**Into the July 2026 research-use launch and Q3**, sentiment cooled to wait-and-see. Customers repeatedly flagged carry-over and drift on reusable sensors, a narrow menu of supported assays, analysis software weaker than DRAGEN, research-only status, and Roche's weak service reputation since its KAPA acquisition [Q3 2026 channel checks].

Opinion splits clearly by customer type. Near-hands-on users were most positive. The Broad, a beta site, expects to put it into production, starting with work Illumina does not do today, such as same-day genomes for newborn intensive care [Expert: Broad EVP, Apr 2026]. Dana-Farber and Vanderbilt planned trials. Clinical and high-volume skeptics were cooler. Yale's answer was "Nope." Cedars said no lab with a clinical NovaSeq X would switch.

The main upside case is "counting" work (experiments that tally sequences, such as single-cell RNA). There, a quoted $0.06 per million reads compares with roughly $0.40-0.60 on Illumina's 25B flow cell [Expert: TGen, Mar 2026]. Pharma users take this most seriously but expect the real contest around 2030, when their NovaSeq X units are depreciated [Expert: Sanofi, Oct 2026]. Share forecasts ranged from a "maximum 5%" in two years [Expert: former Illumina AD, May 2026] to 10-20% of Illumina's business in three years [Expert: ONT, ex-Illumina, Oct 2026].

## Ultima and Element are the nearer-term threats

By Q3 2026, several customers named Ultima, not Roche, as the real cost threat. They cited reagents at roughly one-third to one-fifth of Illumina's cost and genomes near $80 [Expert: Ivy League core director, Oct 2026; Expert: Tennessee NGS manager, Oct 2026]. Its constraints are quality, thin analysis pipelines and slow turnaround. A New York clinical lab rejected it because it cannot read about 10% of the genome, including "almost every clinically relevant gene" [Expert: New York clinical lab, Oct 2026].

Element is the consensus winner in mid-throughput. Cores that cannot fill a NovaSeq X are buying its AVITI24, which adds spatial and cell-imaging features. One South Carolina core expects 70-80% of its work to move there after a September 2026 grant purchase [Expert: South Carolina core, Oct 2026]. Large centers such as the Broad and McGill found little use for it.

## Illumina's response: upgrade the box you own

Illumina's defense was consistent across calls. It is cutting price on big flow cells and adding capabilities to the existing NovaSeq X without asking customers for new capital. These include the 35B and 5B flow cells; 5-base, which reads methylation (chemical tags that regulate genes) and genetic variants in one run; TruPath, which produces "synthetic long reads" (long-range genome structure inferred from short reads), launched at about $395 list per sample; and Q70 accuracy (about one error per 10 million bases).

NovaSeq owners found this persuasive. Minnesota's director said "If I had a NovaSeq, I'd be happy" [Expert: UMN Genomics Center director, Feb 2026]. Reviews differed by product:

- **5-base** drew the most consistent praise [Expert: Delaware, May 2026; Expert: UCSD, Apr 2026].
- **TruPath** split opinion sharply. Yale called its data "exceptionally good" [Apr 2026], while Delaware said it "is not going to work" [May 2026]. Labs that already own long-read machines generally dismissed it, and several doubted the $395 price.
- **Q70** mattered to cancer-recurrence testing users such as Natera but was called irrelevant by labs doing counting work.
- **SomaLogic**, the protein-measurement business Illumina acquired, drew interest but almost no adoption, partly because it requires buying a six-figure liquid-handling robot.

## Spatial imaging is a new headwind to sequencing volume

10x Genomics' Atera, launched at the AACR cancer meeting in April 2026 for about $495,000, was the year's other big event. It reads gene activity across a tissue slide by imaging, so it needs no sequencer. UCSD said, "If we don't buy an Atera, we're going to lose all of our spatial business" [Expert: UCSD, Apr 2026]. Duke's 10x single-cell consumables halved from about $1.2 million to about $600,000 as work shifted to spatial, and its director expects "less sequencing being done on the Illumina platforms" [Expert: Duke, Jun 2026]. Illumina's own spatial product, StrataMap, drew enthusiasm on price and capture area but works only on fresh-frozen tissue, not the preserved FFPE samples that make up most clinical material.

## Clinical is the long-term bull case, with caveats

Experts broadly agreed that clinical testing, especially MRD (minimal residual disease, blood tests for leftover cancer after treatment), is where growth and stickiness lie. A Natera associate director said sensitivity below five parts per million will be required to stay in the market by 2030 [Expert: Natera AD, Mar 2026]. A Natera associate director also recalled the MRD market at about $5 billion today and $30 billion by 2030 [Jul 2026], a figure to treat as a rough recollection.

There were two dissents. Former Illumina staff called the company's claim that clinical makes up more than 60% of revenue "very loose" [Jan and May 2026]. And Roche's diagnostics channel is the scenario that worries clinical observers most, especially if Roche moves its own Foundation Medicine testing to Axelios.

Elsewhere, commoditization is already visible. In European prenatal testing (NIPT, a maternal blood screen for fetal chromosome conditions), lab-to-lab prices fell 40-50% over five to six years in a market described as "capped" [Expert: Veritas VP, Jan 2026].

## VCU Genomics Core co-director (academic customer), 6 January 2026

### Who the expert is

Gregory A. Buck is a professor of microbiology and immunology at Virginia Commonwealth University (VCU) School of Medicine. He co-directs both the VCU Genomics Core and VCU's Center for Microbiome Engineering and Data Analysis. A genomics core is a shared university lab that sells sequencing services to campus researchers. The call was a repeat 40-minute Guidepoint session moderated by Andrew Carmen. Buck is a current customer of Illumina and 10x Genomics, not a former employee, so his evidence is first-hand and current.

He has run the core since it began. It started as a nucleic-acid synthesis core and moved to sequencing in the 1990s. It adopted next-generation sequencing (NGS, massively parallel DNA reading) in 2006-07 with the Roche 454 and early Illumina Genome Analyzers. Over the years it has also had Life Technologies and PacBio equipment and a little Oxford Nanopore (ONT). He labels his projections as tentative and flags where he is speculating.

### Fleet and purchases

The core has owned "pretty much all" Illumina generations. The table below shows its fleet as of the call [Expert: VCU core co-director, Jan 2026].

| Instrument | Status |
|---|---|
| NovaSeq X Plus | Delivered just before the 2025 holidays; production expected "within a month or so" |
| iSeq 100 | Delivered at the same time; replaces the MiSeqs |
| NextSeq 2000 | In production about three years; stays |
| MiSeq (two) | Being mothballed "as we speak" |
| HiSeq 4000 | Long mothballed |

Buck expects no more sequencer purchases in 2026. He may buy automation or more spatial equipment late in the year, and a funding window for expensive instruments opens in summer 2026. The core also owns 10x Visium HD (spatial) and Chromium (single-cell) systems and sequences their output.

The NovaSeq X Plus is meant to relieve the NextSeq 2000. Single-cell and spatial RNA work currently runs there on P4 flow cells (a flow cell is the consumable slide on which sequencing happens). Buck values the X's three flow cell sizes, 1B, 10B and 25B, meaning roughly 1, 10 and 25 billion reads.

His first expected effect is that NextSeq P4 work moves to the 1B flow cell, so "people will get more sequence for less money." He says skeptics who doubted the core had enough volume for a NovaSeq missed that the 1B flow cell makes the instrument usable at modest volumes. The 1B costs more per gigabase (a billion DNA letters, the standard unit of sequencing output) than the 10B or 25B. A filled 10B or 25B beats both the 1B and the NextSeq on cost per gigabase and per read, but only if filled. He guesses "maybe we'll have 25% utilization in 2026."

The iSeq 100 will take the microbial genomes that ran on the MiSeq. It will also serve as a cheap quality check on libraries (prepared DNA samples) before they are committed to expensive 10B or 25B runs.

### Demand and funding

Utilization "continues to increase at maybe 10%." Q3 2025 was flat "for reasons that are not quite clear," and Q4 picked up. Averaged over time, he sees 10-20% annual sequencing growth, which he credits to VCU's funding success. For 2026 he hopes for a "pretty steep increase," tentatively 10-20% or more. If volume does not rise, "we haven't really justified the purchase."

He gives three reasons for expected growth:
- **Repatriated work.** Researchers who outsourced high-throughput work may bring it back; Buck himself outsourced to the Broad Institute and New York Genome Center.
- **New external work.** A new collaboration will sequence for other Virginia state institutions.
- **Lower unit cost.** Cheaper NovaSeq sequencing should encourage more high-throughput work.

He cautions that work moving to his core from other institutions "doesn't necessarily bode that well for Illumina," because it shifts sequencing rather than adding to it. He does expect lower cost to induce more sequencing overall.

About 95% of work is in-house. "Anachronistic" VCU rules force a surcharge on external users, but the core found a workaround in 2026; he expects no big influx. Customers are 80-90% grant-funded, mostly by NIH (the National Institutes of Health), with few NSF grants. VCU has climbed into the top 50 NIH-funded institutions (from about 62nd) and roughly doubled outside funding in a few years. Buck calls this "the one thing that's saving us."

In 2025 one of his multi-year grants was terminated outright and another was cut about 70%. He says this was common, with behavioral genetics and DEI-related work hit hard. Some grants were reinstated after challenges. The shock came early in 2025 and eased over the year.

A CDC center grant spanning five states was cut 75%. His year-four share fell from nearly $1 million to $400,000, and year five is uncertain (possibly cut 50% or 70%). He thinks the CDC was targeted more than other agencies. He now fears further clawbacks less, because researchers are adapting how they write proposals.

### Indirect cost rates

Indirect costs are overhead added on top of grants. They fund compliance offices (human-subjects review, animal care, grants management) and subsidize cores. VCU's rate is 54%, about average. An early-2025 push to cap all rates at 15% would be "devastating," though Buck senses it will not be that dire.

Existing awards seem to keep their original rate, which he thinks is because they are contracts. Rates are renegotiated every five years. Schools that negotiated in 2024 are protected, but VCU renegotiates in 2026; he flags this as speculation from outside "the circle." Roughly 20% of the core's operating budget comes through the research vice-president's office, funded partly by indirects, so a cut would likely hit the core.

### Pricing, tariffs and outsourcing

Illumina applied a roughly 5% tariff surcharge on the instruments the core bought, then gave an offsetting discount, so the core saw no tariff impact. Buck understands Illumina generally covered about 5% of its tariff cost this way. He cited no other cost pressures.

Asked about cheap outsourcers such as Plasmidsaurus, Buck argued that in-house cores add design advice. The Broad "thought they knew more than we did" and returned data about 75% duplicative because it misjudged library amounts. Without upfront design help, users can waste $15,000-20,000 on unhelpful data. Most of his users consult the core first, and its quality controls keep failures rare.

### Single cell and spatial

Single cell means sequencing cells one at a time. About 75-80% of the core's single-cell work runs on 10x Chromium X, much of it legacy projects five or more years old, where protocol inertia blocks switching even for savings. 10x usage is up only "a few percent" year on year. Buck rejects blog claims that single cell is declining but says it is "probably not going up as fast as it used to."

Some users have moved to Parse, which uses combinatorial barcoding (tagging cells with combinations of labels instead of droplets) and is labor-intensive. Two or three have adopted PIP-Seq, Fluent's technology now sold by Illumina, and found it cheap and low-labor. He does not see Parse growing. He does expect PIP-Seq, which "does seem to work" and is being pushed by Illumina's sales force, to "eat into the Chromium business." He has "kind of ignored" Scale, which 10x acquired, lumping it with Parse as labor-heavy. Single-cell ATAC work (mapping open chromatin) is small.

Spatial transcriptomics measures gene activity by location in tissue. Buck is "really disappointed" in Visium HD uptake. Interest was high, but only "a couple people" have used it in over a year, and he feels 10x has "pretty much abandoned" it to push Xenium, its imaging-based product. VCU's NanoString GeoMx units (including two the core bought three or four years ago) have produced perhaps five or six readouts ever, and he hears little interest in CosMx.

VCU may buy a Xenium soon via the summer funding window, though possibly for another core. He gives several reasons spatial is slow:
- A single experiment costs several thousand dollars and typically needs about 20 samples.
- Samples are hard to acquire and handle.
- Planning is complex; one colleague has prepared for at least six months.
- Sequencing and analysis add cost and require a bioinformatician.

His own Visium HD project added about 100 custom bacterial probes to the human set. It worked, though 10x only advised on custom probes and did not support them. Budgets are not shifting from single cell to spatial at VCU; the two are complementary.

### New Illumina products and applications

**Proteomics.** Nobody has requested Illumina's protein assay, which is based on SomaLogic aptamers (short molecules that bind proteins). Dentistry colleagues are considering it, and others are looking at Olink. Buck says mass-spectrometry proteomics is slow and expensive. He cites about $400,000-500,000 for 100 samples on SomaLogic.

**5-base methylation.** Illumina's 5-base kit reads DNA methylation (chemical tags that regulate genes) alongside the genome. It is unused at VCU so far, but he expects interest on cost and throughput.

**Other applications.** Epigenetics is at most 5% of volume. ChIP-seq (10-20 runs ever) and ATAC-seq (under 10) are one-offs that may migrate to Illumina protocols. Small RNA work is a growing interest. Microbiome work is 20-25% of volume. The core is not CLIA-certified (the US lab standard required to return results to patients), and VCU's diagnostics lab does cancer panels, not microbiome.

### Competition

Buck finds Roche's Axelios, which uses sequencing by expansion ("Xpandomers"), very interesting. He thinks Roche is "still a bit overstating their accuracy" because it compares a strand with its fold-back copy rather than with a known reference. Only a few early-access units exist, and new sequencers typically take about a year to become turnkey, so it is too early for his core.

A PacBio Sequel "was nothing but problems" and went unused because reference genomes made short reads sufficient: "the users didn't come through for us." He fears obsolescence: the NovaSeq X is two and a half to three years old, and he hopes Roche or Illumina itself does not leapfrog it soon. He owns an ONT MinION in his own lab (used once or twice a year), as do four or five VCU investigators.

He remains "always bullish on sequencing." He expects clinical use, including routine diagnostic genomes, to carry the market as cost per gigabase falls.

## Former Illumina Senior Manager, Global Market Development (Tegus), ~15 January 2026

### Who the expert is

The expert spent about seven years at Illumina as sales specialist, oncology specialist, market developer and global market developer. Earlier they worked in regional marketing at 10x and in technical sales at Thermo Fisher. They hold a PhD from Nanyang Technological University, with postdoctoral training at the Genome Institute of Singapore and Harvard Medical School. They now lead precision medicine internationally for Astellas, a drug company, and twice flagged possible pharma-side bias.

The call is undated. Its references to Axelios marketing and the SomaLogic deal suggest late 2025 or early 2026. Their view of Illumina's internal plans is dated or secondhand. The two Illumina statistics discussed (20% growth, 65% clinical share) came from the interviewer, not the expert.

### Clinical adoption is the bottleneck

The expert called clinical sequencing, meaning testing used to diagnose and treat patients, the "holy grail" where the big revenue lies. They said its adoption sticks in three places:
- **Reimbursement**: whether insurers and health systems pay for the test.
- **Regulation**: how tests get approved.
- **Prescribing behavior**: how oncologists order tests and how readily pathologists adopt them.

The interviewer said Illumina's clinical consumables (the reagent kits bought for every run) grew about 20% excluding China for two quarters. The expert agreed and tied it to big US labs: Natera, Tempus, Caris and Foundation Medicine. They guessed, explicitly as inference, that the driver is MRD testing over the prior 6-12 months. MRD (minimal residual disease) testing uses blood draws to look for traces of cancer left after treatment.

They expect the big accounts to keep growing on MRD. Internally, though, how to "democratize" clinical sequencing to many smaller labs was "always the tough question to answer."

On Illumina's claim that clinical is over 65% of consumables, the expert called it "a very loose statement": "Illumina has been very generous and very broad in terms of classifying clinical use." The only FDA-approved comprehensive genomic profiling assay (CGP, testing hundreds of cancer genes at once) they cite is TruSight Oncology (TSO) Comprehensive, the approved version of TSO 500, plus legacy assets they doubt anyone runs. Most clinical volume is lab-developed tests (LDTs, tests labs design and validate themselves) at the big labs.

### Reimbursement, community oncology and turnaround

TSO Comprehensive is now reimbursed. The expert sees that as access expansion rather than a share grab from Foundation's send-out model, in which hospitals ship samples to a central lab. They expect "market expansion as opposed to a cannibalization kind of scenario" for now, because CGP coverage of patients is still low.

Whether community oncology practices can adopt NGS at scale is "the billion-dollar question," and the expert expects it to be difficult. Community doctors are only now getting used to CGP reports, as academic centers did 5-10 years ago. Running a test is getting easier, but the expertise to use the data is missing. A hub-and-spoke regional model, where local sites send samples to a regional center, fits best today. Turnaround is driven mostly by batching: a small lab waits until it has enough samples to fill a run.

### Roche

**Speed.** On Axelios's same-day genome, library prep (converting DNA into sequencer-ready form) plus sequencing takes about 24-36 hours on Illumina. Halving that saves only about 12 hours across a workflow that includes biopsy, processing, informatics and reporting, so it matters little in oncology. Speed is more relevant for pediatric rare disease in neonatal intensive care, and the expert has seen little Axelios oncology data.

**Sales force.** Roche's existing diagnostics sales force is "a big advantage." The expert remains skeptical of the product, perhaps from Illumina bias. Converting existing NGS labs is one prize. The bigger one, labs that have never done NGS, requires a "push-button system" pathologists trust.

**Companion diagnostics.** A companion diagnostic (CDx) is a test that determines whether a patient gets a specific drug. The expert sees compliance risk in tying tests to drugs. After Roche bought Foundation, some markets saw patients who bought FoundationOne end up on Roche drugs. Other drugmakers then grew wary of using FoundationOne for their CDx and LDT programs, and the expert expects the same reaction if Roche links its drugs to its sequencer.

**Applications on existing machines.** Roche's single-cell partnership with 10x follows Illumina's own playbook of adding applications to existing instruments rather than launching new boxes every few years. That old cycle "leaves a bad taste in people's mouths." Illumina is adding single cell, spatial (which it is doing "very well," per the expert), 5-base and methylation.

### Sub-$100 genomes, Thermo and 10x

Element's and Ultima's sub-$100 genomes are "a very nice, sexy number" with limited oncology relevance. Germline genomes (inherited DNA) are read at about 30X, meaning each position is read about 30 times. Tumors need "easily more than 120" and sometimes 200X, a point marketing rarely mentions. Prep, biopsy, FFPE blocks (formalin-fixed paraffin-embedded tissue, the standard preserved form) and informatics add further cost. "Even if sequencing is free," regulation and reimbursement take time, so the impact on somatic oncology (tumor-acquired mutations) is "very limited."

The expert has worked at both Thermo and Illumina. Thermo's Ion Torrent wins on a simple DNA-to-report workflow, small panels and slightly faster turnaround, especially in start-up labs. Illumina wins on technology strength and capacity, from small panels up to whole genomes. Labs that scale tend to move to Illumina and then to CGP.

10x leads single cell and spatial as incumbent. The expert recalled bake-offs where Illumina led on spatial and 10x on single cell. Illumina led on transcripts per cell (depth) and 10x on cells per sample (breadth). Because Illumina's installed base can try its products cheaply, the expert thinks Illumina "can close it really, really quickly."

TSO versus FoundationOne CDx depends on the audience. Foundation's test is centralized, so the in-house comparator is Roche's AVENIO kit. ## Cross-call themes

**Illumina's installed base is intact in Q3 2026.** Of roughly 25 customer calls, nearly all run 100% of short-read on Illumina, and only three report actual displacement. These are the South Carolina core (Element), the Tennessee center (Ultima, then Element) and the Element-first academic core.

The moat is described consistently as ecosystem, validation and depreciation, not hardware. Clinical labs cite $100,000–200,000 and hundreds of samples per validation. Pharma cites eight-year depreciation. Several labs said the NovaSeq X roadmap "changes nothing." Compared with earlier periods, the discussion has moved from whether Illumina can defend itself to how fast erosion happens.

**Roche AXELIOS sentiment cooled into launch.** Excitement after AGBT 2025 gave way to wait-and-see. The concerns are:

- a narrow assay menu
- tedious library prep
- carry-over on reusable sensors and drift as they age
- a price of $600,000–750,000, above what some labs hoped
- bioinformatics compared with DRAGEN
- research-only status

Estimates of share loss range from "25% threat" and "niche," through 10–20% in three years, to Illumina losing dominance in 5–10 years. Pharma users rate AXELIOS's cost per read the most seriously, but expect the contest around 2030.

**Ultima, not Roche, is increasingly named the real cost threat.** Customers cite reagents at roughly one-third to one-fifth of Illumina's cost and about $80 per genome. Element wins mid-throughput cost-sensitive cores. A clinical lab rejected Ultima over blind spots in about 10% of the genome.

**Demand split by funding source.** Federally funded academic cores are mostly flat to down about 10%. NIH delays, notices of award arriving late, and a shift of work to CROs and hubs drive this. Biopharma-, philanthropy- and clinically driven labs grew 15–50%, led by single-cell, Perturb-seq, MRD and rare-disease WGS.

Imaging-based spatial (Xenium, Atera) is a new, repeatedly flagged risk of cannibalizing sequencing. Long-read share is creeping up through isoforms, Fiber-seq and PacBio's SPRQ-Nx price cuts, but largely as an addition to short-read.

**Pricing is mixed.** Several labs reported increases of 5–10%, including a 6% tariff surcharge and freight. Others saw flat prices and contract locks. There are signs of competitive response: a NovaSeq X trade-in "discount war," 25B flow cell discounts, cheaper 5B/10B kits and free research reagents. The value of TruPath at $395 was widely doubted.
