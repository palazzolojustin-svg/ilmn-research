# Illumina at Investor Conferences and Special Events

*Core Notes section 06. Synthesized from 16 source extracts as of 2026-10-10. Bracketed tags cite the source; full detail is in Working Notes (see INDEX.md).*

Between August 2023 and December 2024, Illumina's management made ten investor-facing appearances: nine conference fireside chats (moderated question-and-answer sessions on stage) and one special strategy call. Together they trace a clear arc. In late 2023, investor relations and the CFO defended a sharp guidance cut while the company had no permanent CEO. A new CEO, Jacob Thaysen, arrived in September 2023 and promised focus and discipline. A new CFO, Ankur Dhingra, arrived in April 2024. In August 2024 the company set out a multi-year strategy with financial targets, and through late 2024 it tracked progress against that plan.

Almost everything below is a management claim rather than a verified fact. Statements made by the hosting analysts are labelled as theirs. These transcripts were produced by automated transcription, and some passages are garbled; where a reading is uncertain, that is noted.

## UBS MedTech, Tools and Genomics Summit (17 Aug 2023)

Salli Schwartz, Illumina's VP of Investor Relations, spoke with UBS analyst John Sourbeer about a week after the Q2 2023 call. Some audience questions arrived by iPad and QR code. She was upbeat on products and demand but candid that the guidance cut was a "meaningful takedown, which is frustrating, but temporary in a lot of the drivers" [UBS, 2023-08-17].

### Why 2023 guidance was cut

Illumina cut 2023 growth guidance for "core Illumina" from 6%–9% to roughly flat. Core Illumina means the business excluding GRAIL, the cancer-screening company it had acquired. Schwartz attributed about 25% of the shortfall to China. She split the other 75% roughly evenly between the NovaSeq X transition and macro-driven customer caution [UBS, 2023-08-17].

- **China.** The expected second-half recovery had "just not happened." Illumina's win rate also fell because of "more steeply discounted pricing coming from our primary competitor."
- **NovaSeq X transition.** The NovaSeq X is Illumina's newest high-throughput sequencer, launched in late 2022. It "has been going really well, lots of demand, lots of placements" (instruments installed). But customers were "bleeding down" their stocks of NovaSeq 6000 consumables faster than expected. Consumables are the reagent kits customers must buy for every sequencing run. Early technical issues also took time to fix across all customers, which delayed consumables purchases.
- **Macro caution.** Schwartz saw hopeful signs in Walmart's consumer trends, retail sales, inflation data and central bank signals. Even so, customers were deciding slowly, spending cautiously and managing expenses tightly.

### NovaSeq X demand

At the Q2 call Illumina reported about 260 cumulative X orders, about 60 of them in Q2, and Sourbeer noted that placement guidance had been raised [UBS, 2023-08-17].

- **Clinical orders.** About 40% of orders came from clinical customers (labs running patient tests). Unlike past launches, these customers were building new tests rather than replacing 6000 work, so the demand was additive.
- **New customers.** About 15% of orders (per the Q1 call) came from customers new to Illumina or to high-throughput sequencing. Ambient shipping helped: X consumables ship at room temperature without dry ice. Schwartz called this "a huge deal to our customers," because it opens "more far flung regions" and emerging markets where cold chain (refrigerated logistics) can't be guaranteed. Illumina had last cited selling into about 35 countries.
- **Launch pace.** Feedback was "pretty phenomenal," and Illumina had "rolled out more Xs faster and to more places than we have done any launch before." Supporting all those installs took time, which was part of the cut.

### Pull-through and the 6000 replacement math

Pull-through is annual consumables revenue per installed instrument. Schwartz said X pull-through would be volatile while the installed base grew this fast, and at launch Illumina had said it would take six to eight quarters to stabilize. The 10B flow cell was already available. A flow cell is the consumable chip on which sequencing happens, and the number denotes its capacity. The larger 25B was "very highly anticipated" for Q4 2023 and was expected to start a consumables ramp. Schwartz allowed for "a little bit of budget flush" (customers spending leftover year-end budgets) but would not overstate it [UBS, 2023-08-17].

Illumina had about 1,000 NovaSeq 6000 customers:

- About 750 owned one unit, and Schwartz expected all of them to replace it over time.
- About 250 (roughly 25%) owned several. About 20% of X orders were multi-unit, broadly matching that share, and some early adopters had already reordered.

The Q2 call had cut average 6000 pull-through to $800,000–$900,000, because research customers were moving to the X faster than expected. Clinical customers would keep their validated 6000 tests longer. Schwartz was enthusiastic about liquid biopsy (cancer testing from blood) and MRD (minimal residual disease, testing for leftover cancer after treatment). She noted that morning's single-cell news and said "there's a ton of opportunity here."

### Mid-throughput (NextSeq)

Sales cycles were lengthening because of macro conditions and competition, since procurement departments like to evaluate alternatives. Even so, Illumina's mid-throughput win rate accelerated in Q2 versus Q1 2023 [UBS, 2023-08-17]. Pull-through ranges moved as follows:

- **NextSeq 550:** cut from $100,000–$150,000 to $80,000–$130,000, reflecting China and lost COVID surveillance demand.
- **NextSeq 1000/2000:** held at $120,000–$170,000. Q2 came in at the low end, while the installed base grew 8% in the quarter.

XLEAP, the new chemistry used on the X, would reach the NextSeq 1000/2000 in the first half of 2024 as a software-style upgrade, with no new instrument needed. Schwartz said anticipation of XLEAP was helping current sales, as was DRAGEN, Illumina's onboard analysis hardware and software. She expected this transition to be "more contained" than the X's.

### Costs, margins and long-term growth

Illumina was downsizing, delaying or dropping projects. That meant headcount cuts, lower vendor and travel spend, and a pending real-estate review. R&D was cut but remained "a key point of commitment" [UBS, 2023-08-17].

The 25% operating margin target came from the October 2022 Investor Day and was reiterated on the Q2 call. Operating margin is operating profit as a share of revenue. The transcript ties the target to 2025 in one place, while Schwartz spoke of "next year" (2024). She said CFO Joydeep Goswami had called it a stretch, because the gap had widened from 300 to 500 basis points (a basis point is one hundredth of a percentage point). The levers were:

- further operating-expense trimming;
- gross margin gains from spreading fixed factory costs over more units as X production ramped (gross margin is revenue minus production costs, as a share of revenue);
- a mix shift back toward higher-margin consumables;
- cuts to cost of goods sold.

She called the historical 25–30% range "a very reasonable place for us to be."

Sourbeer noted China was about 10% of revenue. Schwartz said mid-teens long-term growth "still makes sense," though probably not in a straight line. That growth would come from three sources:

- lightly penetrated markets;
- reimbursement and awareness;
- elasticity, the idea that lower prices drive disproportionately more use. Examples were more samples, multiomics (measuring several molecular layers per sample), and moves from exomes to whole genomes or liquid biopsy.

She said clinical markets are "bigger and faster growing than research." The NovaSeq X Dx, the diagnostic-cleared version, had "a nice rollout," including in China and in Europe, where some countries require Dx instruments.

### SEC, GRAIL, long reads and population programs

- **SEC.** The Q2 10-Q disclosed an SEC inquiry. Illumina had received a document request letter in July and was cooperating. Schwartz linked the SEC's interest to the "very public proxy fight," regulatory proceedings in several countries and the CEO's departure earlier in 2023.
- **GRAIL decision tree.** A U.S. Fifth Circuit ruling on the FTC challenge was expected by end-2023. An EU Court of Justice ruling on whether the EU had jurisdiction at all was expected in late 2023 or early 2024. Winning the EU case would void the €432 million EU fine. If Illumina lost either case, it would divest without further appeal. If it won both, the Board would keep or divest GRAIL based on shareholder value. The European Commission's divestment order was hoped for in Q3, likely September.
- **Long reads.** Long-read sequencing reads much longer stretches of DNA. Schwartz called it complementary to short reads, which are cheaper "by quite a bit," and did not name rivals such as PacBio or Oxford Nanopore. Illumina's long-read product was announced in March 2023 and rolled out in May. An enrichment assay was coming "very soon," and paired with the 25B it would give a $600 whole genome with long reads. More than 40 customers were evaluating it.
- **Population programs.** National sequencing programs numbered "30 something" and made up less than 5% of revenue.

## Morgan Stanley Global Healthcare Conference (11 Sep 2023)

CFO Joydeep Goswami, who was also Chief Strategy and Corporate Development Officer, spoke alongside Schwartz with Morgan Stanley's Tejas Savant. This was days after Jacob Thaysen was named CEO. Goswami was confident and defensive on competition but candid about the misses [MS, 2023-09-11].

**New CEO.** Illumina was "thrilled," and Goswami was "glad that's behind us" on the search. He said Thaysen brings diagnostics and tools experience, plus strength "on the stronger margins front at a scale company like Agilent."

**China.** Goswami gave no numerical split among the causes:

- macro weakness;
- competition from BGI, the Chinese sequencer maker;
- local-supplier preference in tenders (competitive bids);
- discounting;
- U.S. limits on which Chinese entities Illumina may sell to.

Illumina had expected China to return to normal growth in 2023 without stimulus. The recovery stalled from late Q2, and peers then reported "a very similar kind of outlook." Second-half guidance already assumed more deals lost to BGI and some price pressure. Goswami said Illumina is "pretty directive" about which customers it will fight for on price. Guidance excluded China's new hospital anti-corruption crackdown. He was "not concerned" about Illumina's own compliance but could not control the effects on end customers.

**Why the pressure hit mid-throughput, not high-throughput.** In high throughput, Goswami said, customers also value workflow, data analysis, data compression, backward compatibility and ambient shipping. Competition is not new, citing Thermo and QIAGEN in the past. Illumina still earns a price premium even in China, although expired original patents let copycats into mid-throughput. Mid-throughput win rates were unchanged everywhere except China; what had slowed was conversion of the sales funnel. Schwartz called the X "unparalleled or unrivaled" and said "no one else can compete with" it.

Goswami saw no spillover to Europe yet. Germany was "one of our faster-growing markets in Europe," despite press reports of German economic strain. Q4 guidance assumed only normal seasonality, including the usual year-end lift in sequencing and microarrays (chip-based genotyping, including direct-to-consumer kits). It assumed no big budget flush, and the expected Q4 bump was tied to the 25B.

**NovaSeq X.** About 110 instruments had been purchased, roughly 10% penetration of about 1,000 NovaSeq customers. Among the ~750 single-unit customers, penetration was only about 5% (about 50 customers), because heavy multi-unit users switch first. Fixes for two unusual Q2 technical issues had been deployed since late Q2, and the remaining gaps reflected customer vacations and lab readiness. The X has at least 2.5 times its predecessor's capacity. Goswami said customers "somewhere in the middle" either wait to fill a flow cell or run it below capacity and pay a "slightly higher effective price that we recover," as in the earlier HiSeq-to-6000 transition.

**Mid-throughput and Element.** XLEAP was "on track" for early 2024, with no commitment to pull it forward. Savant cited an Element Biosciences press release announcing 100 AVITI units. Goswami countered that rivals report placements, not orders, possibly at sites that also own Illumina machines. He expected Illumina's premium to persist even as dollar-per-gigabase prices keep falling (a gigabase is a billion DNA letters read), which was already in the model. He expected NextSeq 1000/2000 pull-through to stay within $120,000–$170,000. Savant cited 15–20% of orders from new customers, and Schwartz said ambient shipping makes some of that "truly incremental."

**Pricing philosophy.** Goswami said pricing is based on customers' total cost of operation and is "very selective." New products are priced proactively, with some reactive matching. Cheaper sequencing alone has "limited utility" once sequencing is a small part of customers' costs: "We just dropped the price on DNA, it's not going to help." This is why Illumina invested in DRAGEN and data compression, and he expected competition to become more value-based.

**Margins.** "R&D has not been a sacred cow," Goswami said, and the cuts had been "very surgical." Illumina had invested ahead of the 2021–early 2022 growth and then found its cost structure "clearly inappropriate," though programs for core instruments and end-to-end assays were protected. When the targets were set, Illumina expected 7.5% growth in 2023 and at least 22% margins entering 2024. Instead, "we are at 20% and close to flat for this year" (the transcript is ambiguous about exactly which figures these are). He promised "we will get the margin expansion" but deferred to Thaysen to review the target.

**GRAIL.** Savant expected divestiture under new leadership. Goswami said the EC order "gates the options" and that Illumina was "locked and loaded." The two cases were the Fifth Circuit (FTC) and the European Court of Justice (on Article 22 jurisdiction). Winning both would allow divestiture, a cheaper standalone GRAIL, licensing or other options, but "if we happen to lose either one of those two cases, then divestiture is a certainty." He did not endorse Savant's suggestion that GRAIL could be off the books by mid-2024.

**M&A and strategy.** Tuck-in deals (small acquisitions) continue, but large deals are hard "given where the regulators are these days." A business development team screens technology licenses and partnerships in areas such as proteomics (protein measurement) and spatial biology (mapping molecules in tissue). Deals must improve the sequencer's utility, add value and target large, fast-growing markets. Goswami called GRAIL "a very different kind of bet" that could have rivalled Illumina's own size. Otherwise, "It's not an or, that's an and": modular R&D frees capital for end-to-end workflows. Illumina had "pivoted" this way two to three years earlier, and he said results would show in 2024–2025 launches.

## Evercore ISI HealthCONx (28 Nov 2023)

Host Vijay Kumar opened bearishly. The shares were down about 50% for the year and 50–80% off their peak, and the X had cut cost per sample from about $600 to about $200 with no sign of 3x volumes. Goswami admitted "we and others in our industry were wrong about how China would evolve in '23." He framed the problems as timing, "a question of when rather than if," with NGS penetration "under 10%." NGS (next-generation sequencing) is the technology that reads DNA at massive scale [Evercore, 2023-11-28].

**Elasticity versus transition.** Goswami said X adopters grow sequencing faster than non-adopters. Elasticity runs on "a five or six year cycle": the price drop comes up front, while volume builds for years. In clinical labs, tests validated on the 6000 stay there; some still run on the HiSeq six years after the 6000 arrived, which he called "astounding." Meanwhile the X hosts new tests that were not viable before, driven by reimbursement, MRD and MCED (multi-cancer early detection), so the volume is "accretive." Research customers use the X for larger cohorts, multiomics, single-cell and spatial work. Schwartz said 6000 consumables declines would offset X growth less in 2024: "we should see those lines cross."

**Pull-through and Q4.** Kumar cited a 6000 pull-through range of $700,000–$900,000. Goswami corrected it to $800,000–$900,000, tracking toward the low end. Heavy users had waited for the 25B flow cell, "the real workhorse," which launched in late October 2023. He expected an X consumables ramp in the first half of 2024 and expected 25B pull-through to exceed the 6000's.

The typical customer path was a 6000 inventory burn-down, roughly a quarter of validation runs, then a ramp with initial stocking. After that, volume trends would resume "or maybe slightly accelerated." Disruption was limited to customers who had ordered an X. Q4 guidance implied sequencing consumables down mid-single digits against an easy Q4 2022 comparison. Goswami cited 25B validation runs only, holidays, more 6000 burn-down and no budget flush, whereas Q4 2022 had a small one. The software problems were resolved, and the 25B update also improved the 10B.

**Orders.** Q3 orders were 50 systems, against about 60 expected; Schwartz called it "still a pretty decent quarter." Goswami said the funnel "remains incredibly strong," but conversion had slowed. Academic customers were waiting for 2024 funding, and clinical demand was "a tale of two cities": well-funded customers were buying fleets while others stalled. Opportunities are scrubbed weekly. Shifting conversion probabilities on advanced pipeline were why Illumina "rewound" that disclosure. No customers were swapping two 6000s for one X; the transcript's "about 75% have one X" likely means one 6000.

**Margins and 2024.** Gross margin was about 65–66%. A return toward 70% "can. And it will," Goswami said, reaching the high 60s to low 70s over time. Schwartz said the 2017–2018 NovaSeq 6000 cycle followed the same dip-and-recover pattern. Goswami expected sequential gains for several quarters, though Q1 is seasonally weakest. He said "nothing structurally" prevents core operating margin returning to the high 20s to 30%, but gave no date and said R&D cuts would not harm leadership.

Thaysen, about 60 days in, was running portfolio reviews and budgeting, so Goswami's 2024 comment was explicitly "less to be a guidance." Consensus then expected 10%-plus growth. Goswami said revenue would be roughly in line with 2023 in dollars. Operating margin would be roughly flat, because higher gross margin and lower headcount would be offset by restoring variable and stock compensation that went unpaid in 2023. Full guidance was due in February 2024.

**GRAIL.** The EU Court of Justice hearing was set for mid-December 2023, and the Fifth Circuit ruling was expected by year-end. A Board special committee, whose members were not named, would speed decisions. Kumar noted Illumina had outlaid $1 billion-plus. Goswami said inbound interest predated the divestiture order. A Form 10 (the SEC filing needed for a spin-off) would come before year-end, enabling a sponsored spin or sale, and "our advisers are telling us there is interest."

## Piper Sandler Healthcare Conference (29 Nov 2023)

In one of his first appearances as CEO, Thaysen spoke with David Westenberg. He gave no financial figures. His tone was upbeat and conciliatory, and he declined to be a "Monday morning" quarterback about his predecessors [Piper, 2023-11-29].

**Why he joined.** He cited "passion for oncology and genomics," Illumina's role as pioneer and the energy of San Diego staff. He thought the Board wanted a global industry insider with a record of commercializing innovation at similar scale, and said his profile "pretty much" fits. To investors who saw him as an operator, he cited a physics PhD and founding a MEMS company (tiny semiconductor-style devices) with many patents. He also cited running R&D at Dako, and said his strength is nurturing an innovation culture, including business and process innovation. He dismissed talk of a "brain drain."

**Underrated strengths.** He named three:

- the product roadmap, including automation;
- informatics, which he called "highly undervalued," citing fivefold data compression and built-in pipelines;
- global service reaching customers within 24 hours, which he said Illumina is "the only company" able to provide.

On roughly $4 billion of revenue: "I don't want to consider us mature. I will do a lot to keep us agile, but at the same time, drive everything else to go back to be a very high profitable company."

**Competition and technology.** Westenberg said Element, Ultima and BGI were matching Illumina on cost per gigabase. Thaysen replied that Illumina invented that metric as a proxy for workflow cost, and the goal now is the cost of running a whole lab, including sample prep, informatics and service. He said SBS (sequencing by synthesis, Illumina's core chemistry), "reinvented" as XLEAP, remains "the best chemistry out there." He cited papers, not company data, showing more than 80% of reads at Q40 (about one error per 10,000 bases). Short reads suit most solid-tumor and liquid-biopsy samples, and switching ecosystems is "simply too costly" for customers.

**M&A and China.** "For sure, we're not looking at any larger acquisition at this point," Thaysen said; only bolt-ons, possibly in informatics. China is "around 10% of our revenue," the local team was "still very bullish," and he wants Illumina to act and be seen as a local player.

**Clinical elasticity.** Westenberg said over 50% of revenue is clinical. Thaysen said fewer than 50% of U.S. cancer patients are sequenced at diagnosis, and only about 1–2% for monitoring. Clinical validation slows X adoption: "even though we're all frustrated it takes time, it will come, and it will come strong back." His five-year hope is that "at least 90%" of cancer patients get full sequencing profiles, with Illumina "the central part of the ecosystem" for multiomics.

## J.P. Morgan Healthcare Conference (9 Jan 2024)

Thaysen gave about 20 minutes of prepared remarks, and Goswami joined for Q&A with Rachel Vatnsdal. Thaysen noted that GRAIL must be "held and operated separately" under the EC order. He described core Illumina as having more than $4.4 billion of 2023 revenue with strong cash flow, more than 9,000 employees, more than 9,000 customers in more than 160 countries, and more than 9,800 patents in about 800 families [JPM, 2024-01-09].

**Preliminary Q4 2023 results** beat internal expectations, "primarily driven by the NovaSeq X instruments and our consumable sales." The table gives the figures as transcribed. "Non-GAAP" means adjusted to exclude certain items, and the 3.8% consolidated margin may be a transcription error.

| Q4 2023 measure | Figure | Comparison |
|---|---|---|
| Consolidated revenue (incl. GRAIL) | ~$1.12B | Up ~3% year over year |
| Consolidated non-GAAP operating margin | ~3.8% (as transcribed) | None given |
| Core revenue (ex-GRAIL) | ~$1.09B | Up 2% year over year |
| Core non-GAAP operating margin | ~18% | None given |

More than 400 high-throughput instruments (X and 6000 combined) were placed in 2023. Illumina shipped 352 X units against an expected 330–340, and Thaysen called the X "the most successful launch in the history of Illumina." The company entered 2024 with a backlog of 38 instruments (orders not yet shipped). The total installed base exceeded 25,000 instruments, with more than 2,200 placed in 2023. Thaysen said Q4 was "less about the budget flush" than about multi-unit buying and 25B uptake, which "truly unleashed the full power of the NovaSeq X." Goswami added that a revamped 10B helped consumables "finally" flow.

Thaysen also announced three items:

- the acquisition of Partek, a multiomics software company;
- three new members (BMS, GSK and Novo Nordisk) of the Alliance for Genomic Discovery with Nashville Biosciences. They join founders AbbVie, Amgen, AstraZeneca, Bayer and Merck, and the alliance will sequence more than 250,000 whole genomes linked to longitudinal health records;
- XLEAP rollout in 2024 to the "NovaSeq 1K and 2K," which most likely means the NextSeq 1000/2000.

**2024 outlook.** "Our preliminary view is that our results will look very similar to '23," meaning roughly flat revenue and core operating margin. Thaysen corrected the premise that this covered EPS: "we didn't make any comments about EPS." He made no first-half versus second-half distinction, noting that a year earlier peers had wrongly expected a second-half recovery. Goswami said only the X utilization ramp would make the halves differ. 2024 X placements would be below 2023's, because the large backlog carried into 2023 would not repeat, though fleets would keep expanding.

**Three priorities.**

1. **Customers and commercial excellence.**
2. **Operational excellence.** The early-2023 goal of $100 million in savings was exceeded at about $175 million, through headcount, footprint, vendor and IT cuts. A new "portfolio optimization strategy" would direct investment to the work with the most customer impact.
3. **Resolving GRAIL.** The divestiture was announced on 17 December 2023. Options were a sale or a capital-markets route such as a "sponsored spin," with terms targeted by end-Q2 2024. Any funding would likely use debt while protecting the investment-grade credit rating.

**Elasticity, markets and China.** Thaysen gave the example of a customer running bulk RNA and single-cell RNA on separate lanes of one flow cell, and noted clinical customers moving from tissue to liquid biopsy. Goswami said nearly 400 high-throughput placements in a "high interest, high inflation environment" showed demand. He added that each customer's first-year price step-down masks volume growth.

Asked about the Analyst Day targets of mid-teens growth and higher margins, Thaysen said "I'm not ready to provide any numbers." He still saw no reason Illumina could not return to "historic margin numbers." China is "approximately 10%" of sales. He expected it to stay challenged in 2024 as Illumina becomes more "made in China," improving in 2025 and beyond. On competition: "I love winning. But what I more love than that is to go out there and make a difference for our customers." On long reads, Goswami said population programs use them on about 10,000 samples, "less than 10%" of the total.

## RBC Capital Markets Global Healthcare Conference (14 May 2024)

New CFO Ankur Dhingra, who joined in mid-April 2024, appeared with Schwartz and RBC's Conor McNamara. Dhingra spent 18 years at Agilent, including as CFO of its life sciences unit under Thaysen, whom he called "wicked smart." He then served as CFO of a molecular diagnostics company that used Illumina products and of a biopharma company. Joining was "pretty much a no-brainer." Goswami stayed on as an adviser [RBC, 2024-05-14].

**Q1 2024 and guidance.** Dhingra described a "pretty sizable revenue beat," led by consumables and X placements, with a seasonal backlog build. Operating margin rose 190 basis points, and operating expenses fell year over year. Full-year guidance was held: revenue similar to 2023 and operating margin "closer to 20%." Because capital spending and China had not changed, banking the beat "would probably be a prudent approach." Schwartz said clinical sequencing consumables grew high single digits in Q1. About 35% of 2023 X shipments went to clinical customers, Q1 was similar, and instrument shipments matched the February ranges.

**Pricing and launches.** Annual price increases saw "very good realization." Sequencing volume grew more than 35% in Q1, while the move to the X and XLEAP lowered net price per unit, as modelled. Pull-through was within its ranges. The P4 flow cell launched in March 2024, and about 25% of eligible NextSeq 1000/2000 customers had installed the enabling software (the transcript garbles the instrument name). The mid-March XLEAP launch caused "a little bit" of purchase pausing. Schwartz put the lag from launch to adoption at one to two quarters, longer for clinical revalidation.

**R&D, growth and the fall event.** On R&D at about 20% of sales, Dhingra said: "If I can't justify a return on it, then it doesn't make sense." He was "blown away" by the pipeline, "a good problem to have," but gave no specifics. When McNamara called Illumina highly innovative, he replied "It's still is." McNamara noted EBITDA margins historically around 35% (EBITDA is earnings before interest, taxes, depreciation and amortization). Dhingra cited the 35%-plus volume growth and contrasted it with how hard it was to lift Agilent from mid- to high-single-digit growth. Schwartz promised a virtual strategy event "in the fall" with a financial algorithm.

**GRAIL money.** Under its EC agreement, the most capital Illumina must put into GRAIL at divestiture was about $1 billion. The EC fine is accrued, stayed and backed by a guarantee. An EU Court of Justice ruling might come later in 2024, and a win would void the fine. If Illumina lost, a separate appeal would run into 2025 or 2026, so any payment would "not [be] this year."

## Bernstein Strategic Decisions Conference (29 May 2024)

Dhingra, about six weeks in, spoke with Eve Burstein. She introduced his past roles as CFO of Summit Therapeutics and of CareDx (a transplant diagnostics company), plus his Agilent years. Schwartz had been at Illumina almost 2.5 years, after MSCI and Moody's. Dhingra restated three priorities: "Getting back to growth as a company remains priority number one," then profitable growth and a quick GRAIL resolution [Bernstein, 2024-05-29].

**GRAIL.** A spin-off (including an amended Form 10 and NASDAQ and SEC steps) and a sale were running in parallel, because each takes regulatory time. A decision was targeted by end-June 2024. Under the EC framework a spin is more within Illumina's control, while a sale needs EC approval of the buyer and may take longer. Execution was expected "within 24," apparently meaning within 2024.

**Strategy preview.** Dhingra cited being "in a production environment at $200" per genome, with a whole genome in a "15 hour run." He described the sequencer "as a detector" at the center of sample-to-answer workflows and multiomics. The event would set explicit return targets and "some sort of algorithm" for margin growth from a "20-ish percent" core operating margin. He said it would be "more like an Investor Day," new products were unlikely, and he placed it in September. In the event, it was held in August.

**Market-making and commoditization.** Dhingra said market-making spend is declining as a share of revenue. Asked whether followers get a free ride on Illumina's investment, he said "Yes, they do," but they lack "a 25,000 instrument base" and Illumina's reliability. "I don't think we're anywhere near commoditized." Drawing on his CareDx experience, he said sequencing is "not the largest part" of end-to-end test cost.

**Academic market.** Burstein argued downside risk outweighed upside. Dhingra said funding was "stable to slight growth" with "good bipartisan support." Schwartz said tracked grant counts and dollars had held up, and genomics funding was growing faster than the overall NIH budget. Single-cell work needs about 30–50% more sequencing output than a standard workflow, so "we still get a fair share there." Dhingra also cited partnerships with key opinion leaders and an MRD partnership with BMS and J&J.

**Clinical market.** The FDA had issued a final rule regulating laboratory-developed tests (LDTs, tests designed and run in a single lab). Dhingra's view: "Well, if they don't, somebody else will." He said "roughly about a third" of Xs went to clinical customers, mostly for test development. This matches RBC's ~35% of shipments but is below the ~40% of orders cited in August 2023. Schwartz called the LDT rule "an opportunity" for Illumina's regulatory expertise. Dhingra said menu expansion matters as much as cheaper runs.

**Multiomics and capital.** Dhingra cited the SomaLogic proteomics partnership and the vision of the sequencer as "the core instrument around all of the omics." He framed this as market expansion, not taking share from partners. Cash would be deployed "Judiciously": staying investment grade and doing tuck-ins only. "It isn't our intent right now to then layer on a really large acquisition."

## 2024 Strategy Update Special Call (13 Aug 2024)

One week after Q2 earnings, Thaysen, CTO Steve Barnard (at Illumina since 1998) and Dhingra presented, with Schwartz moderating questions from 16 brokers. Seeking Alpha listed Q2 2024 EPS of $0.36, which it recorded as $0.54 below consensus. It listed revenue of $1.11 billion, down 5.44% year over year and $26.63 million above consensus. ### Vision and market

Thaysen called Illumina "a biology platform company rooted in NGS." He said the industry is moving "from cost per gigabase" to "total cost of workflow." He gave these market sizes:

- a total market of more than $125 billion, of which NGS serves about $10 billion;
- a liquid-biopsy market for Illumina of nearly $800 million, growing at a 34% compound annual rate;
- MRD as a $10 billion opportunity. About 20 million cancers are diagnosed a year, 10 million patients could benefit, and each would need about $1,000 of consumables a year.

In a clinical whole-genome workflow, sequencing is about 40% of cost, and analysis and interpretation about 45%. Progress so far:

- 469 NovaSeq X Plus units installed since March 2023;
- nearly half of high-throughput data already on the X;
- X pull-through above $1 million;
- a "much more proactive stance on competition."

Thaysen named pharma as a third customer group, "historically underserved." He announced that the Broad Institute would adopt the just-acquired Fluent single-cell technology for ultra-high-volume Perturb-seq, screens that show how disabling genes changes cells.

### Pipeline (Barnard)

Barnard cited more than 100 invention disclosures so far in 2024 and nearly 9,000 patents. The X delivers more than 320 times the daily output of a decade ago, rising from 25 to 8,000 gigabases per day. It carries up to eight lanes, runs onboard DRAGEN for four applications per flow cell and uses about 90% less packaging. He called it "the fastest-growing high-throughput sequencer in our history." Two products were due within 12–18 months:

- **5-base genome.** It captures genetic variants plus methylation (a chemical mark on DNA that regulates genes) from one library prep. Library prep is the lab step that readies DNA for the sequencer.
- **Comprehensive genome.** It moves library prep onto the flow cell and covers hard-to-read regions and large structural variants. It was already in testing, and Barnard said it "could be the de-facto genome of the future."

Illumina Protein Prep, built with Standard BioTools, was in early access ahead of a full launch in early 2025. It measures 10,000 proteins, extendable to 20,000. Barnard also highlighted Partek software and the AI tools PrimateAI and SpliceAI: "this isn't PowerPoint science."

### Financial targets (Dhingra)

Consumables are more than two-thirds of revenue. Headcount was down about 10% from peak, 2024 savings were on track at about $100 million, and Q2 gross and operating margins each rose more than 200 basis points versus FY2023. The targets were:

- **Revenue.** High-single-digit growth by 2027, accelerating each year. Core sequencing is "by far" the largest driver. Multiomics is "almost more an upside." Services already generate more than $500 million a year, growing mid-single digits.
- **Volume and the X transition.** Gigabase output averaged more than 25% annual growth over five years and recently ran at 35–40%. Around mid-2025, about two-thirds of high-throughput volume and half of high-throughput consumables revenue should be on the X. By 2H 2025, about 75% of volume should be.
- **Mid-throughput.** Nearly 60% of NextSeq 1000/2000 customers had upgraded for XLEAP, up from about 25% at RBC in May. On China: "we're not making a call when conditions will change in China."
- **Margins.** Operating margin expansion of 500 basis points through 2027 from about 21% in 2024, aiming for the top-quartile "high 20s." Up to two-thirds would come from operating leverage (revenue growing faster than costs), backed by $200 million of gross run-rate savings over 3.5 years.
- **EPS.** "Double-digit to teens" growth for 3.5 years from a base near $4. Dhingra said "the teens go from 13% to 19%."
- **Cash.** Core 2024 free cash flow of about $950 million, "approaching $1 billion" excluding GRAIL investment. A new $1.5 billion share buyback was authorized, with M&A limited to tuck-ins.

## How the tone and message shifted

The 2023–2024 messaging moved through five phases:

- **Late 2023.** Management was defensive and explanatory. It blamed China, the 6000-to-X transition and macro, insisted on "when, not if," and left the margin targets in limbo.
- **January 2024.** Thaysen's arrival brought a reset: no long-term numbers, three priorities, no large M&A and a promise of discipline.
- **Spring 2024.** Dhingra added return-on-investment language and prudence on guidance.
- **August 2024.** The strategy call replaced the abandoned mid-teens growth and 25% margin goals with lower but explicit targets. These were high-single-digit growth by 2027, roughly 26% operating margin, and double-digit-to-teens EPS growth.
- **Late 2024.** Management sounded confident on execution, costs and transition metrics, but assumed no macro or China recovery.

Recurring disputes to watch:

- the bear case on elasticity versus management's transition story;
- whether clinical customers will move their validated tests at all;
- conflicting China revenue shares (about 10% versus about 7%);
- installed-base counts (more than 25,000 total versus more than 21,600 active).

The rest of this section covers six appearances between January 2025 and March 2026:

- two JPMorgan presentations, each used to preannounce fourth-quarter results;
- a Q&A-only investor session at AGBT 2025, the main genomics technology meeting;
- three fireside chats (one-on-one interviews on stage) at Bernstein, Wolfe Research and TD Cowen.

Thaysen spoke at all of them, with Dhingra and Barnard at some. Over the period, the investor question moved from "can Illumina return to growth?" to "is the guidance too conservative?" Three things stayed constant: the X transition, the "highest-quality insight at the lowest end-to-end cost" strategy and the 2027 targets. China, NIH funding, tariffs and Roche's entry into sequencing came and went as headwinds.

## AGBT 2025 Investor Session (February 25, 2025)

### Format

This Q&A-only session at AGBT (Advances in Genome Biology and Technology) in Marco Island, Florida, was hosted by Schwartz, with Thaysen, Barnard and Dhingra answering. No guidance was given. The tone was confident and combative. Thaysen framed competition as sequencer makers versus end-to-end workflow providers, and Barnard challenged rivals' technical claims. All comparisons below are management claims the analysts did not test. Several names are garbled in the transcript; for example, "DARCO" is likely Dako [AGBT, 2025-02-25].

### What each executive chose to highlight

- **Thaysen picked Constellation**, "an unbelievable cool technology that completely sets us apart." He expected it to become the standard for rare disease and germline (inherited) sequencing. He cited a session the day before in which Dr. Stephen Kingsmore showed hemophilia A variants, which Thaysen said had not been possible before; "even other long reach techniques have difficulties."
- **Barnard picked spatial**, measuring gene activity while keeping its location in tissue. He said Illumina was completing a full portfolio that year "from proteomics down to genomics" across bulk, single-cell and spatial modes, "a huge inflection point."
- **Dhingra picked Perturb-Seq.** Illumina had launched a 5-billion-cell atlas with the Broad Institute, to be built over "two or three years at the most." He said it lays the foundation for a pharma strategy under the services pillar and could materially speed drug discovery.

### Constellation versus long-read competitors

Thaysen said Constellation targets both the long-read market and deeper insight in existing markets such as rare disease, at Illumina-level pricing. The table summarizes Barnard's comparison, which he called "no comparison across the market today."

| Platform | Throughput and turnaround (Barnard's claims) | Library prep |
|---|---|---|
| Illumina Constellation | 16 complete genomes, with phasing and structural variants, in 24 hours on a 10B flow cell (eight lanes) | About 10 minutes |
| PacBio | "Maybe three genomes incomplete," over 24 hours of sequencing | Two days |
| Oxford Nanopore | About three days for one sample, not at diagnostic-grade variant accuracy | — |

**Price.** Asked about $1,000 per genome, Thaysen said the price is "in the hundreds of dollars, it's not in the thousands." It was in early access at one genome per lane, and multiplexing (several genomes per lane) would cut costs further. A premium is possible, but he warned against dividing a 10B flow cell's cost by eight: "We want to open up markets with it."

**Single cell and spatial.** Thaysen claimed a cost per cell "at least two times less than anybody else." In spatial, Illumina images whole slides while rivals cover small areas. Citing his pathology background, he said tissue needs "centimeters of insights," not millimeters. Pricing would scale from a few experiments to thousands.

### Roche's new sequencing technology

Roche was presenting its technology later that day. Thaysen said customers "are buying into a whole ecosystem," which Illumina already offers, with automated sample prep feeding directly into the sequencer. Roche had shown only a "technology overview," not a product, so he would not speculate.

Barnard described two fronts. In whole-genome diagnostics, Roche is "still classically short read," while Constellation adds phasing, structural variants and 10-minute prep. In counting applications (RNA, single cell, spatial), sequencing is only 10%–20% of experiment cost, so cheaper sequencing targets "the wrong Pareto." Spatial prep takes two days with costly kits, which is why there has been no "really big science in spatial." The Fluent acquisition (instrument-free, scalable library prep) and Illumina's flexible spatial product address this. He questioned whether Roche "really understands" the NGS market.

**Roche as a diagnostics competitor.** Thaysen said he beat Roche's Ventana unit while at Dako. His arguments were:

- Diagnostics depend on companion diagnostics (tests paired with a specific drug), and pharma prefers independent partners.
- Illumina's TSO 500 tumor test is an FDA-approved kit with companion-diagnostic claims, with more being added to its labeling.
- Clinical customers "care about insight." A new IVD (in vitro diagnostic) technology "takes a long time," because it must perform repeatedly in decentralized labs, not just in a KOL's hands.
- A brand alone does not establish a technology: "it remains to be seen."
- Because Roche owns Foundation Medicine, many potential customers "might consider twice" before buying from a vertically integrated competitor.

**Accuracy.** Barnard said Illumina has "a very nice IP portfolio in nanopores" but will not launch below Q30 (about one error per 1,000 bases). A technology starting at Q20 must compensate with "exotic, time consuming" library prep, which he said Roche's three-instrument workflow would show. Clinical expectations will exceed Q50 within two to five years because of MRD's "needle in a haystack" demands, which requires a native Q30 start plus library prep. He called Roche's "clinical company" positioning "a big question mark." Thaysen added that duplex sequencing (reading both DNA strands) "comes with a penalty" of extra sequencing. He noted the MiSeq i100's CMOS chip already sequences in four hours, and Illumina could build bigger chips if speed mattered.

### Pricing logic, Ultima, reagent rental and partners

- **End-to-end pricing.** Doug Schenkel (Wolfe) asked whether adjacencies protect sequencing spend. Thaysen said that in single cell, spatial and proteomics, cheaper sequencing barely moves total cost. Owning sample prep lets Illumina cut the prohibitive part, enabling bigger experiments and more sequencing. "Just coming out with a sequencer" won't win.
- **Reagent rental.** Instruments are a small part of revenue, and Illumina already has rental deals. The model suits clinical labs with known volumes, where price can track reimbursement, but not grant-funded academia. A NovaSeq buyer generates about $1.3 million of pull-through a year for five to six years, supporting lifetime-value pricing.
- **Ultima.** Thaysen did not address its wins at Chan Zuckerberg and UK Biobank.
- **Partners such as 10x Genomics.** "We will always make sure that they're successful on our platform," and Illumina does not lock customers in. "Some companies like to compete in press releases," but the Broad 5-billion-cell project "is real."

### NIH, academic demand and China

Thaysen called customer conversations "very positive." Budgets remained and core labs were still running volume, but there was "more concern, more risk," mostly about future grants. Normal conditions will return: "And they will." Barnard said nervous customers favor reliable vendors. Thaysen added: "No CEOs ever get fired by hiring McKinsey… Nobody is going to be blamed with going with Illumina." Dhingra said about half of instruments placed in the quarter to mid-quarter went to research customers.

Illumina had just been placed on China's unreliable entity list, a government list that can restrict foreign firms. Thaysen said Illumina was "still operating completely in China." Customers want it to stay, and there had been "multiple conversations with the right parties." Illumina had known of the listing for only 48 hours at its previous check-in.

### Roadmap, informatics and the "shifting money" debate

- **Accuracy.** Dan Brennan (TD Cowen) cited a Q55 slide and roughly $8 billion of Illumina R&D over a decade (his figure). Barnard said Q55 is in the pipeline for MRD, but physicians "could care less about the Q scores." What matters is callability, whether a variant in the genes of interest can be detected. Thaysen said calling 95% of a genome fails "if you can't measure BRCA." Dhingra said the Constellation map "paves the path for our most medically relevant genome."
- **Spatial.** FFPE compatibility (the standard preserved clinical tissue format) was in research, likely releasing in 2026, "probably second half." Adding protein to single cell and spatial is "absolutely" planned, as is a methylation-based "five base" genome.
- **Informatics.** Barnard said Illumina Connected Multiomics (ICM), built on Partek, would cut three weeks of single-cell analysis to hours. He named DRAGEN's cost savings, an AI team with "more access to data than anyone," and multiomics on top as differentiators. Thaysen said customers who adopt Illumina pipelines won't return to "home brew," and that Illumina is one of few companies able to build foundation models for pharma.
- **Is R&D money just shifting?** An unidentified analyst asked whether Element, Ultima and spatial simply move money around. Thaysen disagreed: academics run five to ten spatial samples because hundreds are unaffordable, and "pharma is going to be all over this." Barnard cited history: Illumina won roughly 70% of the HapMap project with end-to-end arrays, and later the Genomics England 100,000 Genomes project.

### A harder 2025 than planned

2025 was meant to be an execution year, but "the deck was stacked a little bit differently." The four headwinds were China's unreliable-entity listing, new competition, NIH funding problems and tariffs. Illumina chose to "control what we can control." It cut about $100 million of 2025 costs, with more beyond, and shifted some priorities while keeping the strategy "completely" unchanged. If a headwind forces a strategy change, "you have the wrong strategy out the gate."

### The long-range plan and the growth math

The plan (high-single-digit growth and 500 bps margin expansion by 2027) now excluded Greater China, as stated on the Q1 2025 call. Asked if it still held, Thaysen said "Exactly, yes," citing three drivers:

1. **The X transition.** The X cut the sequencing price point by two-thirds, which requires 100% more volume just to stand still. About 65% of volume was on the X but under 50% of revenue, with 50% expected in 2H 2025. After that, "growth is going to be easy because the denominator is very different."
2. **New products.** Spatial, Constellation and the 5-base genome, all added since August 2024, were worth "probably another 1 point" of growth.
3. **Margin.** Cost cuts were accelerated to offset China.

Asked whether volume would fade after the discount was lapped, Thaysen said fully transitioned customers keep growing, and many had raised their own guidance. The table shows his long-term model. Together, these two assumptions imply high-single-digit revenue growth.

| Item | Past few years | Long-term model |
|---|---|---|
| Sequencing volume growth | 30%–40% (described as "elevated") | Mid-20s percent |
| Annual price decline | — | Mid-teens percent |

### Academic exposure, China and clinical risk

**Academic.** Research is about 50% of revenue (including some pharma). The long-range plan had assumed about 6% annual growth there, and Illumina had cut 2025 US academic and government guidance by 15%. Thaysen sized the exposure step by step:

- clinical is over 50% of revenue and research about 45%;
- about half of revenue is from the Americas, putting research exposure under 25%;
- about half of that is academic, so roughly "10%, 12%" of revenue is truly exposed.

He said uncertainty, not the grants themselves, was the overhang, and hoped it would clear "maybe over summer." Guidance assumed a market "subdued actually all the way through '25." Priorities such as autism research need heavy sequencing, new tools could attract shifting grant money by 2026 or 2027, and pharma interest in Perturb-seq should hold.

**China** was "less than 5%" of revenue and, after cost cuts, "an upside from a P&L perspective." The original plan never counted on China growth, "so without China, it's going to be a little bit easier." But while Illumina could not place instruments, "we don't have a future in China." A US–China trade deal was "a little bit out of our hands," and he did not say the odds had improved.

**Clinical switching risk.** Burstein noted clinical is about 55% of revenue. Q1 2025 was strong, with good X placements and mid-single-digit consumables growth, and the August 2024 plan assumed low-double-digit clinical market growth. Thaysen gave no market-share figure and said "investors worry too much." His reasons were these:

- Labs have built assays, infrastructure and informatics around Illumina.
- "There's nobody in the industry that will match us on the innovations" over three years.
- "Nobody that made a decision going with Illumina has ever regretted it."
- Defectors would be welcomed back "when they realize they made a mistake."

### Products, AI and the insider purchase

- **Roadmap sharing.** Longer roadmaps help academics write grants and clinicians plan assays. Customers now attend full-day campus sessions on unreleased plans.
- **Constellation** can take "a lot" of the long-read market, starting with human resequencing (reading a genome against a reference) and "probably not de novo" assembly at first. A sick newborn normally needs trio sequencing (child plus both parents), but with phasing only the patient is needed. Thaysen therefore expected Constellation's true turnaround to beat rivals claiming the fastest sequencing. On-flow-cell prep alone was not differentiating, since labs automate batch runs anyway; the added proximity reads made the product. Layering the 5-base genome on top excites him most.
- **5-base genome.** It adds methylation to standard reads, replacing a costly two-workflow process.
- **Spatial.** Commercial release was set for 2026. Spatial had been "way too expensive," so papers used one to three samples over 1–2 mm and covered only part of the transcriptome (all expressed RNA). Illumina targets whole-transcriptome, single-cell resolution across a whole slide at a price allowing 40–60 or even hundreds of samples. Thaysen did not say what feedback was still being sought.
- **AI.** More than 40 staff work on foundation models using NVIDIA tools. "I don't know any other company… that have the capabilities." He did not address AI threats.
- **Insider purchase.** Thaysen and Dhingra bought shares as soon as their trading window opened, which Board changes had delayed. Thaysen called it "probably one of the best investment decision I've ever made," citing "a lot of upside" and a "moat" no rival matches.

## Wolfe Research Healthcare Conference (November 19, 2025)

### Format and the 2027 targets

In this fireside chat with Schenkel, Ankur and Conor of Illumina were present but did not speak. Schenkel joked that Thaysen, in a first CEO role since September 2023, "picked a doozy" of policy shocks. Thaysen summed up his tenure: GRAIL separated and "doing a great job," the strategy clarified, 2025 execution hit by "a few hiccups," and now, "knock on wood," "fully in execution." The opportunity remains "still massive" because sequencing is not yet standard of care [Wolfe, 2025-11-19].

Thaysen confirmed the targets: high-single-digit growth in 2027, about 500 bps of expansion "up to 26% operating margin," and double-digit earnings growth. One thing had changed: "the high single-digit growth is ex China at this point." Schenkel noted China was heading to about 2%–3% of sales by end-2025, so growth should accelerate in any scenario, and Thaysen agreed.

The drivers were the X transition (main driver) and elasticity, which "does" work, with volume growing "very, very rapidly." Multiomics should add about 1–2 points to 2027 growth, up from "probably another 1 point" at Bernstein. An academic recovery would be upside but was not assumed. Thaysen endorsed Schenkel's framework of low-single-digit academic growth, the transition plus clinical volume, and share-of-wallet products. He added that pharma is "starting to become a real contributor" for the first time, buying at very large scale ("hundreds of thousands" of genomes, "billions of cells" for Perturb-Seq). A new BioInsight business would serve multiple pharma partners, and Illumina would collaborate even with competing sample-prep vendors.

### Clinical transition detail

On the Q3 2025 call, Illumina showed a case study of 40 clinical customers who fully converted from the 6000 to the X. Schenkel read the chart as under 5% growth in year one, then accelerating. Thaysen explained the shape: in year one, volume gains and price cuts are "almost in an equilibrium." Customers don't pocket savings; they compete by offering larger assays within reimbursement (what insurers pay per test). Once the price cut is lapped, volume drives growth.

About 50% of high-throughput revenue was on the X. Research had "more or less completely moved," leaving clinical at "less than 50%." Thaysen and Schenkel described four clinical cohorts:

1. **Low-volume labs** that stay on the 6000, a smaller cohort.
2. **Labs keeping old assays on the 6000** while putting new, bigger assays on the X. These grow "out the gate."
3. **Labs moving from exome to whole genome.** An exome covers only protein-coding regions. Revenue improves from the start while the remaining exome business declines. Cohorts 2 and 3 are "absolutely the largest."
4. **Price-driven movers** such as NIPT (non-invasive prenatal testing). They are under 20%, mostly already moved, and "not going to be a big cohort."

Cohorts 2 and 3 use the 25B, the flow cell with the biggest price change. The others likely stay on the 10B, priced closer to high-volume 6000 pricing. The case study excludes brand-new sequencing customers.

### Roche, China, costs and innovation

**Roche.** Thaysen saw no change in customer behavior: "I absolutely enjoy competition and especially when we're winning." Schenkel cited Roche's further investment in Freenome, which is evaluating Roche's AXELIOS platform. Thaysen cautioned that announcements take a long time to become reality. He repeated the Dako/Ventana lesson that pharma preferred non-integrated partners and still prefers Agilent. He saw a "huge opportunity" over 3–5 years in decentralized testing, and noted pharma puts its assays where the most instruments are.

**China timeline.** Thaysen described the sequence:

- *February 2025:* listed on the unreliable entity list after US tariffs, initially without sanctions.
- *About a month later:* an import/export ban on instruments. Illumina could serve existing customers but not install new machines.
- *Around Q3:* permission to supply OEM partners (firms selling Illumina-made instruments under their own brand), including China manufacturing. This was under 20% of China operations but "a good step forward."
- *After the Q3 call:* sanctions lifted, though customers need permits. Clarity was expected "over the next quarter or so," and budget cycles of about six months push real placements "into '26."

Thaysen had met MOFCOM (China's Ministry of Commerce) the prior week. He wants Illumina off the list, but "there's more to it than what we can control." Schenkel judged China growth in 2026 mathematically hard but plausible by 2027. Thaysen said that is "at least our hope," with China general manager Jenny doing her utmost.

**Costs and innovation.** Cost opportunities were not exhausted: "We haven't even started really on AI yet." Schenkel had visited Cambridge, UK, and saw no innovation slowdown. Thaysen named 5-base and Constellation as favorites, because clinical growth will come from them. He conceded "everybody have been able to do methylation for years," but said Illumina made it simple end to end. He also cited single cell, spatial, Fluent and the SomaLogic proteomics acquisition. No 2026 numbers were given.

## TD Cowen Health Care Conference (March 3, 2026)

### AGBT 2026 roadmap

In this fireside chat with Dan Brennan, Thaysen, now also Interim Chief Commercial Officer, was relaxed and said the company was growing "very strongly." His AGBT message was that customers who invest in Illumina keep benefiting from its innovation [TD Cowen, 2026-03-03]:

- **New X flow cells.** These cover higher and lower capacity needs and include a faster-turnaround path and an unpriced 35B. Customers "really love… that we're not launching an instrument," because their installed machines keep improving; funding-short research labs valued this most.
- **Q70 accuracy**, aimed at high-sensitivity MRD, which "nobody can do… today." Things will come out over 18 months, but not all by a date like "June '27." It is "not a pop," because clinical validation takes time.
- **TruPath (formerly Constellation).** It has 10-minute prep and combines short- and long-read information. It launched at $395 per genome, delivering on AGBT 2025's "hundreds of dollars." It runs 16 samples per run, rising to "likely around 96" within 18 months, and any X owner can start immediately. Thaysen expects it to become "the new standard of the genome" in germline testing and blood cancers. On pricing only at launch: "We don't see any value in coming out too early with pricing."
- **Spatial** remained in early access, launching "during the next 6 months," and Illumina was "overwhelmed with the interest." It claims at least 4x cheaper insight, and "we can do even better." Asked to compare with 10x Genomics panels (Brennan's illustration of about $7,000, or an alternative around $4,000), Thaysen gave no price. He prefers "cost of insight" metrics: per cell, per sample or per square millimeter.

### Roche, Ultima and Element

Roche had shown "all the promises," and Illumina was now seeing "the consequences." Thaysen critiqued AXELIOS 1 on four counts: a "very different workflow" with library-prep challenges; a single-lane flow cell that complicates mixing applications; difficulty with panels (targeted gene tests); and pricing that "requires you to do high throughput."

He conceded Roche's strength in counting applications. He put that segment at "probably less than USD 100 million" of Illumina's served space. There, halving sequencing price lets researchers do only "20% more," so it does not drive elasticity. He rejected commoditization as "not true at all" and saw no "freezing of the market," which is why Illumina won't preview future instruments. He gave no size for whole genome but said "no other technology… can actually provide the insight that TruPath can," citing DRAGEN, a tertiary analysis platform with Amgen for rare disease, and an oncology interpretation platform. On Ultima and Element, nothing new: "we fight every day on the streets, which we should."

## How the message evolved

**Growth.** Management moved from promising a return to growth in 2025, to claiming it delivered (Q4 2025 up 4%), to guiding 2026 to just 1%–3%.

**Transition milestones.** The X share of volume rose from over 60% (Jan 2025) to 65% (May 2025) to 78–80% (late 2025), meeting the 75%/50% milestones set in January 2025. Volume growth stayed above 30% throughout.

**Definition of the target.** The 2027 target was quietly redefined to exclude China, as China fell from about 5% of revenue to about 2%–3%. Margins beat plan, with about 200 bps of expansion in 2025 versus 170 bps guided.

**Constellation/TruPath** went from an unpriced technology to "hundreds of dollars" to $395 per genome.

**Roche.** The stance moved from dismissing an unlaunched "technology overview" to detailed critiques of AXELIOS 1, while management claimed no change in customer behavior throughout.

**Pharma** moved from a vision to a named business, BioInsight, with three named partners.
